<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Yogananda was the monastic name his guru gave him in 1915; Self-Realization Fellowship glosses it as bliss through divine union. Paramahansa, literally supreme swan, is a title for a monk of the highest attainment, which Sri Yukteswar conferred on him in December 1935. The 1946 first edition spells it Paramhansa, and the Ananda movement keeps that spelling.</aside>

<p>Los Angeles, the evening of 7 March 1952. In a banquet room at the Biltmore Hotel, the city's civic and Indian community is giving a dinner for Binay Ranjan Sen, India's ambassador to the United States. Sen has spent the past days with the swami at the Mount Washington headquarters of Self-Realization Fellowship, and he will later write that he felt he had gone back three thousand years to an ancient ashram.</p>
<p>Yogananda, fifty-nine, long-haired, in an orange robe, gives the last speech. He talks about India and America and what each could learn from the other. He ends by reading his poem "My India." According to the accounts his disciples published, he reaches its last lines, "Where Ganges, woods, Himalayan caves, and men dream God, I am hallowed; my body touched that sod," and slides to the floor.</p>
<p>The death certificate gives a heart attack. His followers call it mahasamadhi, a yogi's deliberate, conscious exit from the body. Sen, writing years later, said that no one present felt like mourning and that what he felt was "a feeling above all of exaltation."</p>

## Life

Mukunda Lal Ghosh was born on 5 January 1893 in Gorakhpur, in what is now Uttar Pradesh, the fourth of eight children of a Bengali family. His father, Bhagabati Charan Ghosh, was a senior officer of the Bengal-Nagpur Railway, and the family moved with his postings before settling in Calcutta. In *Autobiography of a Yogi* he writes that both his parents were disciples of Lahiri Mahasaya, the Benares householder who taught Kriya Yoga, and that Lahiri blessed him as an infant and foretold that he would be a yogi. That story belongs to the book's devotional frame. The family connection to Lahiri's circle is the documented part, and it gave the boy a ready-made spiritual world of pictures on the wall, family stories and visiting disciples. His mother died when he was eleven, an event the *Autobiography* treats as the first great wound of his life.

The book describes a boyhood spent looking for saints in Calcutta and Benares and a failed attempt to run away to the Himalayas. In 1910, by Self-Realization Fellowship's chronology (some other accounts say 1909), he met Swami Sri Yukteswar Giri, a disciple of Lahiri Mahasaya with a hermitage at Serampore, upriver from Calcutta. Yukteswar was strict, sharp-tongued and interested in astrology, in the cycles of the ages and in showing that the Bible and the Hindu scriptures taught the same truths. Mukunda trained with him on and off for about ten years. He studied at Scottish Church College in Calcutta and took his BA from Serampore College, affiliated to Calcutta University, in 1915. In July 1915 Yukteswar initiated him into the Giri branch of the order of swamis, and he became Swami Yogananda Giri.

<aside class="note"><strong>Yogoda.</strong> His name for his early system of body and breath training, used for the Indian society he founded in 1917 (Yogoda Satsanga Society) and for the American lessons of the 1920s. Its core is a set of tense-and-relax exercises meant to draw what he called cosmic energy into the body by will.</aside>

His first institution was a school. On 22 March 1917, according to the organisation (some sources say 1916), he opened a residential school for boys at Dihika in Bengal. In 1918 the Maharaja of Kasimbazar gave it his summer palace and grounds at Ranchi, where it combined ordinary schooling with meditation, outdoor life and the energization exercises. This was the start of Yogoda Satsanga Society of India. Gandhi visited the Ranchi school in September 1925, after Yogananda had left for America, and wrote in its guestbook that the institution had deeply impressed him.

In August 1920 Yogananda sailed from Calcutta on the *City of Sparta* to attend the International Congress of Religious Liberals in Boston, organised by the American Unitarian Association. He spoke there on 6 October 1920 on "The Science of Religion," a lecture later published as a small book. Self-Realization Fellowship counts its own founding from that year, though the legal corporation dates from 1935. He stayed in Boston for about three years, then began touring. From 1924 he lectured across the country, filling large halls in New York, Los Angeles and other cities, and enrolling students in paid classes where he taught the energization exercises and meditation. In 1925 he acquired an estate on Mount Washington, a hill above Los Angeles, and dedicated it on 25 October as his headquarters. The same year he started a magazine, *East-West*, later renamed *Self-Realization*. In 1927 President Calvin Coolidge received him at the White House.

The early American years had their troubles. Basu Kumar Bagchi, a boyhood friend who had taken the name Swami Dhirananda and come to help run the American work, left in 1929 and later sued over money he said he was owed. Sources date the lawsuit to 1929 or 1935, and the claim was settled. A later associate, Sri Nerode, also left and sued in 1939.

From the mid-1920s Yogananda's students could take his teaching home in printed form. Instructional booklets called *Yogoda* lessons were in circulation by 1924. In 1934, according to Self-Realization Fellowship (another of its own pages says 1935), he announced a comprehensive series of printed lessons for home study, sent by post at regular intervals and assembled over the next few years. A student who worked through them could apply for initiation into Kriya Yoga. This correspondence course became the backbone of the organisation and the main channel by which people who never met him learned his techniques.

In June 1935 he set out for India by way of Europe and the Middle East with two Western students, among them his secretary C. R. Wright. He reached Bombay on 22 August. On 26 and 27 August he stayed with Gandhi at Wardha, and by Self-Realization Fellowship's account he gave Kriya Yoga instruction to Gandhi and several of his followers at Gandhi's request. He was reunited with Yukteswar at Serampore on 30 September. On 29 November he visited Ramana Maharshi at Tiruvannamalai, a meeting recorded in the ashram's *Talks with Sri Ramana Maharshi*. On 25 December, at Serampore, Yukteswar gave him the title Paramahansa. He also met Anandamayi Ma, whom the *Autobiography* calls the joy-permeated mother. During this year he arranged a permanent endowment for the Ranchi school and the Indian society. Yukteswar died at Puri on 9 March 1936, and Yogananda returned to America late that year.

<aside class="note"><strong>Hong-Sau.</strong> The concentration technique taught in the first Self-Realization Fellowship lessons. The student silently follows the natural breath with two syllables, hong on the inhalation and sau on the exhalation, until breath and mind grow calm.</aside>

While he was away, James J. Lynn, a Kansas City businessman and his most prosperous disciple, later known as Rajarsi Janakananda, had built him a hermitage on the cliffs at Encinitas, north of San Diego, as a surprise. Yogananda spent much of the next decade there, writing. A temple built on the cliff edge in 1938 slid into the sea in 1942. He opened a temple in Hollywood in 1942 and, in 1950, dedicated the Lake Shrine at Pacific Palisades, a garden retreat around a lake.

*Autobiography of a Yogi* appeared in December 1946 from the Philosophical Library in New York, with a preface by the Tibetan Buddhism scholar W. Y. Evans-Wentz. It tells his life as a pilgrimage among saints: levitating yogis, a woman who lived without eating, a perfume saint, his guru's resurrection appearance after death, and above all the line of masters from the deathless Mahavatar Babaji to Lahiri, Yukteswar and himself. In the book's account, Babaji appeared to him in Calcutta before he left for America and told him that he had been chosen to spread Kriya Yoga in the West. Yogananda revised the book for later editions, adding a final chapter on the years 1940 to 1951 in the third edition of 1951. It has sold in the millions and is still his main recruiting voice.

He became an American citizen in 1949. In his last years he worked with disciples on his long commentaries on the Bhagavad Gita and the Gospels, recorded a few short talks, and spent more time in seclusion. He died at the Biltmore on 7 March 1952. He was buried in the Great Mausoleum at Forest Lawn Memorial Park in Glendale. Rajarsi Janakananda succeeded him as president of Self-Realization Fellowship, followed in 1955 by Daya Mata, who led it until her death in 2010.

## The teaching

Yogananda's central claim is that God can be experienced directly, by method, in this life, and that the method is a science. He called the goal Self-realization, the soul's direct knowledge of its oneness with God. Religions, in his account, differ in their outer forms and agree in this inner aim, and the yogi reaches it by withdrawing life energy from the senses and turning it inward toward the spine and brain, where it can be felt as light, sound and joy.

**Kriya as technology.** The *Autobiography*'s chapter "The Science of Kriya Yoga" sets out the claim plainly. "Kriya Yoga is a simple, psychophysiological method by which the human blood is decarbonized and recharged with oxygen." The practitioner "mentally directs his life energy to revolve, upward and downward, around the six spinal centers." Each such cycle, he wrote, speeds up the slow natural evolution of the soul, so that "that half-minute of Kriya equals one year of natural spiritual unfoldment." He did the arithmetic: a thousand Kriyas in eight hours give a thousand years of progress in a day. Compared with slower methods, Kriya, he wrote, "may justly be called the 'airplane' route." The chapter explains the principle and withholds the technique itself, which is given only after preparation and a pledge of secrecy.

That framing is the reason he belongs in this part of the book. Lahiri Mahasaya taught Kriya as a private initiation to householders in Benares. Yogananda presented the same practice in the vocabulary of 1920s America, as a laboratory method with predictable results, open to anyone willing to put in the hours. He insisted it had "nothing in common with the unscientific breathing exercises" of popular teachers, and he called it "the easiest, most effective, and most scientific avenue of approach to the Infinite."

**The course.** In Self-Realization Fellowship's lessons as they stand today, the student first learns three techniques over a basic series of eighteen lessons mailed every two weeks: the Energization Exercises, in which each part of the body is tensed with will and then relaxed; the Hong-Sau technique of concentration on the breath; and the Aum technique, in which the meditator listens inwardly for a sound identified with the creative vibration of God. After several months more of practice the student may apply for Kriya initiation, and the Kriya technique itself is taught in further printed lessons. The organisation regards the preparatory techniques as part of Kriya Yoga. The design is Yogananda's: a graded curriculum, distributed by post, leading to a guarded initiation.

**One teaching under two scriptures.** Yogananda read the Bhagavad Gita as an allegory of the inner battle. The war at Kurukshetra becomes the conflict in every person between the faculties that pull toward the senses and those that pull toward the soul, with characters standing for psychological forces; Bhishma, the grand old warrior, stands for ego. The Gita's instructions on breath, such as the verse about offering the inhaling breath into the outgoing one (IV.29), he took as references to Kriya. His commentary ran in his magazine from 1932, was revised from 1948 and was published in two volumes as *God Talks with Arjuna* in 1995. He applied the same method to the four Gospels in *The Second Coming of Christ* (2004), subtitled "The Resurrection of the Christ Within You." There the second coming is an inner event, the awakening of the Christ consciousness in each disciple, and the purpose of the book, in its own words, is "to reveal the complete harmony and basic oneness of original Christianity" with original yoga. In the *Autobiography* he went further and wrote that "Elijah, Jesus, Kabir and other prophets were past masters in the use of Kriya or a similar technique."

**The guru who becomes the teachings.** Yogananda taught the necessity of a true guru and placed himself fourth in a line that runs from Babaji through Lahiri and Yukteswar. He also arranged that the line should stop with him. Self-Realization Fellowship quotes him as saying "When I am gone the teachings will be the guru," and states that no later leader of the society will take the title. Its presidents are spiritual successors and representatives. A student today receives the guru's instruction in his printed words, under the organisation's custody.

I put him in Part 5 because he answered the part's question more confidently than anyone else in it. For Yogananda liberation was the result of a technique applied with discipline, and he said so in numbers. What I find most new in him is the format. Lahiri had insisted that a householder could practise in the middle of ordinary life; Yogananda arranged for that householder to live in Ohio and receive the method in the mail. The Energization Exercises are his own invention, and his allegorical Gita is a real, sustained piece of interpretation. The deeper doctrine, that one inward science underlies Christ and Krishna alike, he learned from Yukteswar.

## Lineage

Yogananda stands at the end of the line Self-Realization Fellowship calls its gurus: Babaji, Lahiri Mahasaya, Sri Yukteswar and himself. The Lahiri Mahasaya chapter tells the earlier part of that story.

<MiniTree id="yogananda" />

The edge from Yukteswar is documented in every account: Yogananda trained at Serampore for years and took his monastic vows from him in 1915. His tie to Lahiri runs through his parents, who were Lahiri's initiates; the infant blessing and the prophecy belong to the *Autobiography*'s devotional story. The edge from Babaji is a claim made in the *Autobiography* about a figure known only through disciples' accounts. Christianity appears as an influence because Yogananda built half his written commentary around the Gospels. Outward, his line runs to the leaders of Self-Realization Fellowship and Yogoda Satsanga Society and to the disciple who broke away, Kriyananda, founder of Ananda. The Gandhi edge rests on Yogananda's and his organisation's account of the 1935 Wardha visit.

## The practice

The Kriya technique is given only on initiation and is not described here. The stages below follow the course as Self-Realization Fellowship describes it on its own site.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Energize.</strong> Standing, the student tenses and relaxes each part of the body in turn, willing energy into it, with some exercises done on a double breath. Yogananda taught that will draws cosmic energy into the body through the medulla at the base of the brain.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Concentrate.</strong> Seated with spine upright, the student practises Hong-Sau, watching the breath without controlling it and mentally pairing it with the two syllables until the breath slows and the mind settles.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Listen.</strong> In the Aum technique the meditator turns attention inward to listen for subtle inner sound, understood as the cosmic vibration Aum, and tries to become absorbed in it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Apply for Kriya.</strong> After the basic series of lessons and several months of steady practice, the student applies for initiation and receives the Kriya technique in printed lessons, with a pledge to keep it private.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Practise daily.</strong> The initiate, called a Kriyaban, practises Kriya daily along with the earlier techniques, sitting afterward in stillness.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

His voice survives on a handful of short talks recorded in his last years, which Self-Realization Fellowship and Yogoda Satsanga Society publish, and in silent and newsreel film used in documentaries. The 1946 first edition of the *Autobiography*, now in the public domain, is freely available online. Ramana Maharshi's ashram keeps the record of their 1935 meeting. In it Wright asks how to realise God, and Yogananda asks how the spiritual uplift of the people is to be effected; Ramana answers that methods "differ according to the temperaments of the individuals and according to the spiritual ripeness of their minds."

## Critiques and questions

**Science as idiom.** Yogananda's claims that Kriya decarbonises the blood and that half a minute equals a year of evolution are stated as physiology and arithmetic, but he offered no measurement for them, and none has since established them. The historian of religion Anya Foxen, in *Biography of a Yogi* (Oxford University Press, 2017), reads his system as a proto-modern yoga that blended Indian ideals of the superhuman yogi with American New Thought, Unitarian liberalism and physical culture. On her reading, the scientific language belonged to the culture he was addressing as much as to the practice.

**The hagiography.** Much of what readers know about the Kriya line comes from the *Autobiography*: the deathless Babaji, materialisations, Yukteswar's appearance after death. The book presents these as eyewitness or trusted testimony, and there is no independent evidence for most of them. The same applies to his own death. In May 1952 the mortuary director of Forest Lawn, Harry T. Rowe, issued a notarised letter, which Self-Realization Fellowship published and *Time* reported that August, saying that no physical disintegration was visible in the body twenty days after death. Devotees cite it as a sign of yogic attainment. Skeptics point out that the body had been embalmed.

**Which text is the book?** Yogananda revised the *Autobiography* in his lifetime, and Self-Realization Fellowship, which bought the rights in 1953, has continued to publish revised editions; it says its editions "and none others, incorporate all of the author's wishes" and that later changes followed his instructions to his editor Tara Mata. In 1993 Crystal Clarity, the publisher of the Ananda movement, reprinted the 1946 first edition, arguing that later revisions number in the hundreds and some "substantially change the meaning of the original text." Similar questions hang over the Gita and Gospel commentaries, which were compiled and edited from his manuscripts and talks for decades after his death.

**The Ananda litigation.** In 1962 the Self-Realization Fellowship board asked Kriyananda (J. Donald Walters), a disciple since 1948 and then its vice-president, to resign. In 1968 he founded Ananda near Nevada City, California. In 1990 Self-Realization Fellowship sued Ananda in federal court over names, images and copyrights. In 1995 the Ninth Circuit held that "Self-realization" was generic for a spiritual organisation and that the society's registration of "Paramahansa Yogananda" as a mark was invalid, while sending its claims on the full name "Self-Realization Fellowship" back for trial. Ananda says the case also left many of Yogananda's early writings, including the first edition of the *Autobiography*, in the public domain. A jury in 2002 found that Ananda and Walters had infringed Self-Realization Fellowship's copyrights in magazine articles and recordings of his talks and awarded about 29,000 dollars, a small fraction of what had been sought. Each side described the result as vindication. A separate 1998 jury verdict against Walters for sexual harassment and fraud, in a suit by a former Ananda resident, concerns Kriyananda's conduct and not Yogananda's; Ananda's leaders called that case a smear campaign.

## Descendants

Self-Realization Fellowship in the United States and Yogoda Satsanga Society of India continue his work with monastic orders, temples, retreats and the lessons, now also available in an app; Brother Chidananda has been president since 2017. Ananda, the largest independent group, teaches Kriya in Kriyananda's lineage and publishes its own editions of Yogananda's work. Many smaller Kriya teachers, some tracing their authority through other disciples of Lahiri or Yukteswar, owe their Western audiences to readers who first met Kriya in the *Autobiography*. The book itself has a long afterlife beyond any organisation. A 1999 HarperCollins panel named it one of the hundred most important spiritual books of the twentieth century, and many readers have said it first set them on a spiritual path.
