<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Meher Baba means "compassionate father." His first close followers gave him the name around 1921, and the Trust that keeps his memory uses the same translation. The family name Irani marks a Zoroastrian family of Iranian origin settled in India.</aside>

<p>Visitors in those years describe a routine that never changed. The man in white sat quietly while his hands moved in a private code. One of his closest companions, the mandali, watched the gestures and spoke their meaning aloud, so that every question and every answer passed through a second voice. Since October 1954 he had used no alphabet board and no writing. The gestures were all he had left, and the mandali learned them as one learns a language.</p>

## Life

Merwan Sheriar Irani was born on 25 February 1894 in Pune, the second of seven children of Sheriar and Shireen Irani, according to the Avatar Meher Baba Trust. His parents were Zoroastrians. His father came from Khorramshahr in Persia and had wandered in search of spiritual experience before settling in Pune. As a boy Merwan founded a club for his schoolmates, the Cosmopolitan Club, which followed world affairs and gave to charity. He played instruments, wrote poems and spoke several languages. He studied at Deccan College in Pune.

<aside class="note"><strong>Sadguru and qutub.</strong> In Baba's usage the Hindu word sadguru and the Sufi word qutub both name a person who has realised God and works to lift others.</aside>

### The meeting under the neem tree

In his own account the turn came when he was nineteen. Hazrat Babajan was an elderly Muslim woman who sat day and night under a neem tree in the Char Bawdi quarter of Pune. Biographers call her a Pashtun from the Afghan side of Balochistan. Estimates of her age range so widely, from about 1790 to 1820 for her birth, that no one knows how old she was when she died on 21 September 1931. Merwan passed her tree on his bicycle, and according to the Meher Baba Trust's chronology he first received an embrace from her in May 1913. The famous kiss on the forehead, after which he fell into a long trance and was shaken out of ordinary life, is dated January 1914 by the Trust and by Wikipedia's article on Babajan. Some other accounts simply say 1913. Everything about the event rests on his own later telling and on the accounts of his followers, since Babajan herself spoke little of her past.

For the next seven years or so he was, in his own telling, being unveiled. The Trust says four more teachers were involved. In 1915 he is said to have visited Narayan Maharaj at Kedgaon in April, Tajuddin Baba in Nagpur at about the same time, Sai Baba in Shirdi in December, and Upasni Maharaj at nearby Sakori in the same month, according to the Trust chronology. Upasni Maharaj is the one of the five with whom his association is longest and best attested. Upasni had himself spent about three years with Sai Baba from 1911, settled at Sakori in 1917, and received Merwan there repeatedly. Biographies say Upasni helped him bring his inner experience together with ordinary waking consciousness.

In late 1921, according to Charles Purdom's biography as summarised by Wikipedia's article on Upasni, Upasni praised the young man in public and called him the Sadguru of the age. Around the same time his first followers began calling him Meher Baba.

### Bombay, Meherabad and the silence

In 1922 Meher Baba and a small group of followers set up house in Bombay at a place they called Manzil-e-Meem, "House of the Master." The chronology dates their stay from 7 June 1922. In 1923 the household moved to a site near Ahmednagar that became Meherabad, where a school for children of every caste and religion, a hospital and a dispensary grew up over the next few years. He demanded strict obedience from the people around him.

On 10 July 1925 he stopped speaking, and he did not speak aloud again. The Trust, Wikipedia and the New World Encyclopedia all give that date. At first he wrote on a slate or in chalk. By the Trust chronology he began using an alphabet board on 7 January 1927, while another account says he wrote his last message on 1 December 1926. A companion read out the letters as he pointed. The Trust's biography describes the silence as "a limitation that He assumed for the spiritual upliftment of humanity." Wikipedia summarises his position as one in which the silence was not undertaken as a spiritual exercise and belonged solely to his universal work.

### The West, the masts and the New Life

He first travelled west in 1931, sailing on the same ship as Gandhi, and met him several times on board. In 1932 Mary Pickford and Douglas Fairbanks Jr. received him at Pickfair in Hollywood, and newsreels in New York and London filmed him. In 1934 he announced that he would break his silence at the Hollywood Bowl, postponed the event and sailed for Hong Kong instead.

From the late 1930s he spent years seeking out masts, people whom he described as drunk with love of God. He travelled widely to find them, feed and wash them and sit with them. In October 1949 he began what he called the New Life, a period of "complete helplessness and hopelessness in total reliance on God" in the Trust's words, which he ended in February 1952.

<aside class="note"><strong>Mast.</strong> A word Baba took from the Sufi phrase for someone intoxicated with God. A mast may look mad to outsiders. Baba said the mind of a mast is slowed down, while the mind of a madman is sped up.</aside>

In April 1952 he opened the Meher Spiritual Center at Myrtle Beach, South Carolina. On 24 May 1952 a car carrying him was struck head-on near Prague, Oklahoma, and he broke a leg and suffered facial injuries. A second accident near Satara on 2 December 1956 killed his companion Dr. Nilu and left him in constant pain.

### The Avatar declaration and the last years

In August 1953 he began dictating <i>God Speaks</i> at Dehradun, letter by letter on the alphabet board. The Trust's own pages date his public declaration of being the Avatar to February 1954, and one encyclopaedia entry gives 10 February. Meher Baba's Call, a printed statement of 12 September 1954, repeated the claim "irrespective of the doubts and convictions" of others, and on 30 September 1954 at Meherabad he gave a Final Declaration. In October 1954 he put the alphabet board away and began using hand gestures only. <i>God Speaks</i> appeared from Dodd, Mead in 1955.

He signed the deed of the Trust on 6 April 1959. The East-West Gathering of 1962 brought Western followers to India. In 1966 the pamphlet <i>God in a Pill?</i> carried his statements against LSD and marijuana, discussed below. In late 1968 he said his work was complete. He died on 31 January 1969 at Meherazad. Wikipedia reports that his last gestures conveyed "Do not forget that I am God." Thousands came to the Last Darshan in Pune afterward, including many hundreds from the West.

## The teaching

### The five Perfect Masters

Baba taught that a handful of people in every age have realised God completely. In <i>God Speaks</i> he calls the one who then uses divine powers to help others a Perfect Master, and equates the role with the Hindu sadguru, the Sufi qutub and the Christian apostle. He said there are always 56 God-realised souls in a body on earth, and that exactly five of them hold the office of Perfect Master. When one dies another takes over at once. At the time of his own God-realisation, he said, the five were Sai Baba of Shirdi, Upasni Maharaj, Hazrat Babajan, Tajuddin Baba and Narayan Maharaj.

The Avatar is the Perfect Master of a different order, the Ancient One who returns. Baba said an Avatar comes every 700 to 1,400 years and that the five Perfect Masters of the time bring him down. In his words, "During the Avataric period, the five Perfect Masters make God incarnate as man." He listed Zoroaster, Rama, Krishna, Buddha, Jesus and Muhammad as earlier descents and said the Avatar of his own age was himself. In his scheme the five Masters of his own time were the five he had met.

### God Speaks

<i>God Speaks: The Theme of Creation and Its Purpose</i> is the closest he came to a system. He dictated it in 1953 and 1954, and Eruch Jessawala wrote it down. The first line of the main text says "ALL souls (atmas) were, are and will be in the Over-Soul (Paramatma)."

The story begins before creation, in what the book calls the Beyond-the-Beyond State of God. The book says the first urge was latent in the Over-Soul, an urge to know itself, and a chart in the book calls this the Original Whim. The image Baba uses is an infinite ocean and its drops, each a soul that seems separate. The drop has to find out that it is the ocean. Its consciousness is awakened through impressions, called sanskaras, which pile up as it passes through forms. The passage runs through stone, metal, vegetable, worm, fish, bird and animal, each shaping the next, and arrives in the human form, where consciousness is complete enough for the soul to turn back. Reincarnation in human form wears down the impressions. Then comes the return, which Baba calls involution, through planes of consciousness that lead to the seventh, where the soul knows itself as God. The Trust names that last state divine union or God-realisation. A soul that has got there and also uses its powers for others is a Perfect Master.

The book is dense, with numbered charts and a table inside the back cover that sets its terms beside Sufi, mystic and Vedantic ones.

### The Discourses and the personal teaching

The <i>Discourses</i>, dictated by alphabet board over many years and published in several volumes, give practical advice on karma, maya, love and service. The New World Encyclopedia notes that the seventh edition contains editorial changes Baba did not authorise.

Baba himself said he came "not to teach but to awaken," and that he laid down no fixed precepts. Followers sometimes quote another line, "You have not to renounce anything but your own self." The teaching comes down to love of God, selfless service and giving up the sense of "I, me, my and mine," and he said the last is possible only through his grace. He taught that all religions and all beings are one in essence. The Trust adds that he also bathed lepers and gave aid to the poor.

### The silence

He described the silence as part of his work for the world and denied that it was a personal practice. He said that the world had received enough words and now needed to live them. He promised more than once to break it, and the Final Declaration of 1954 stated that he would speak "One Word, the Word of Words." Contemporary accounts say he never spoke it aloud. See Critiques below.

### The drug statements

In the 1960s young Westerners learned about Baba partly through the debate over psychedelics. In 1964 and 1966 he answered written questions from several American academics, including Richard Alpert, later known as Ram Dass. Wikipedia dates one set of clarifications to 4 July 1964, given through his secretary Adi K. Irani. Sufism Reoriented published them in 1966 as <i>God in a Pill?</i>

He said drugs such as LSD and marijuana were harmful "physically, mentally and spiritually." On the claim that a drug could show a person God, the pamphlet records: "If God can be found through the medium of any drug, God is not worthy of being God." He said that continued non-medical use of LSD could end in madness, and that LSD experiences, even if they feel mystical, were shadows of the subtle plane with no bearing on spiritual advancement. He allowed that medically supervised LSD might help some mental disorders. Devotees ran a campaign against drugs in the United States, Europe and Australia. A summary on one devotee site says it had limited success but drew new followers.

I find the architecture the interesting part. To my reading, the separate ideas in <i>God Speaks</i> have close relatives in Sufi metaphysics, in Advaita and in Theosophical writing about planes. The assembly is unusual because it is presented as knowledge dictated by the one who has seen the whole cycle, and the tone is a map's, with no argument offered for it. Whether the map is true is a matter of faith, and the book does not claim otherwise.

## Lineage

Meher Baba sits at the end of a short, tightly connected chain. Sai Baba of Shirdi taught Upasni Maharaj, Upasni taught Merwan, and Babajan, Tajuddin and Narayan Maharaj stand to the side as independent saints he named among his masters. In the other direction his descendants run to Sufism Reoriented, the Trust at Meherabad and the musicians who took up his name.

<MiniTree id="meher-baba" />

The edges differ in strength, and the book grades them. Upasni Maharaj is **documented**. Merwan stayed at Sakori repeatedly from about 1916 and 1917, Upasni praised him publicly in late 1921 according to Purdom, and the two met for the last time on 17 October 1941 at Dahigaon. Hazrat Babajan is **testimony**. She is a real person with a tomb and a known neem tree, and the kiss is dated either 1913 or January 1914, but the event rests on Baba's own account. Sai Baba of Shirdi is **testimony**. Baba dated one meeting to December 1915. The standard Marathi biography of Sai Baba, the <i>Shri Sai Satcharita</i>, does not mention him, according to Wikipedia. The story that Sai Baba greeted him as Parvardigar is **hagiographic**, found in devotional lives. Tajuddin Baba of Nagpur (born 1861, died 17 August 1925) and Narayan Maharaj of Kedgaon (1885 to 1945) are **testimony**. Both have followings of their own, but each link to Merwan is a single visit in 1915 reported in Baba's biography. Sources give Tajuddin's birth date as 17 or 27 January 1861.

Baba also drew on a wider Sufi inheritance, which the tree marks as an influence. The designation of the five as the Perfect Masters of the age, with himself as the Avatar they brought down, is his own claim. Of the five, only Upasni is reported to have named him publicly, in late 1921 as Sadguru of the age.

## The practice

Baba gave no fixed technique. The teaching pages of his centres describe a way of life in place of a method.

## Critiques and questions

No criminal charge or court judgement against Meher Baba appears in the sources reviewed for this chapter. The questions raised about him concern evidence, prediction and sources.

**The silence and the Word.** The Final Declaration of 30 September 1954 listed events Baba said were coming. <i>The Awakener</i>, a magazine of his followers, prints notes he gave at Satara on 13 November 1954, including that he would "break My Silence, and speak One Word, the Word of Words." The same notes mention that he would suffer humiliation and, finally, drop his body "when I shall be stabbed in the back." The Trust's chronology calls the declaration's predictions enigmatic. According to Wikipedia, citing contemporary accounts, he did not break the silence audibly before he died, which disappointed some followers while others read it as a test of faith. Earlier, in 1934, a planned silence-breaking at the Hollywood Bowl was postponed and he left for Hong Kong.

**The lineage claim.** The five Perfect Masters are known historical figures. Of the five, only Upasni is reported to have named him publicly. For the other four, the sources reviewed show nothing they said about him, and the link rests on Baba's account and devotee biographies. The <i>Shri Sai Satcharita</i> does not mention him. Kevin R. D. Shepherd, a critical biographer of Sai Baba, Upasni and Babajan, treats the three movements as separate and linked only by personal meetings, and says early Marathi sources should guide the record where later accounts differ.

**Sources and editing.** Most of what is known about his life comes from devotees. The main biography, Bhau Kalchuri's <i>Lord Meher</i>, has been criticised by Shepherd as having sources often unidentified and as existing in several versions. A summary of the Discourses says the seventh edition contains editorial changes Baba did not authorise.

**Authority and press.** Contemporary newspapers treated his late-1930s tours with Western women as a scandal. Paul Brunton's <i>A Search in Secret India</i> (1934) gave a disillusioned account, according to Wikipedia. Baba demanded absolute obedience from those closest to him, and people who failed to comply during the New Life were sent away, in the New World Encyclopedia's summary of his life.

**The slogan.** The phrase "Don't worry, be happy" is widely credited to Baba. Wikipedia and several devotee and music sites say he often used it with Western followers and that it was printed on posters and cards in the 1960s. Bobby McFerrin said in a 1988 interview with <i>USA Weekend</i> that "[w]henever you see a poster of Meher Baba, it usually says" the phrase. Beyond the posters, none of the pages checked give the exact words Baba used, the date or the person he said it to. A devotee page quotes a different line he gave to an exhausted nurse, "Always say, 'All is well and beautiful. I will be happy.'" The story that McFerrin saw such a poster at the apartment of the musicians Tuck and Patti is told without a source. The song was released on 24 July 1988, reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 on 24 September 1988 and won three Grammys in 1989.

## Descendants

The Avatar Meher Baba Perpetual Public Charitable Trust grew from the deed Baba signed in 1959 and became active after his death. It runs the Tomb-Shrine at Meherabad and clinics, a school and pilgrim facilities. Mani S. Irani chaired it until 1996, followed by Bhau Kalchuri until 2013. In the United States the Sufi group he renamed Sufism Reoriented keeps his teaching and publishes <i>God in a Pill?</i> and <i>God Speaks</i>, and the Meher Spiritual Center in South Carolina and Meher Mount in California are centres of devotion. Most of his followers accept the Avatar claim. Wikipedia says he had hundreds of thousands of followers, mostly in India, and gives a 1971 estimate of 7,000 in the United States. In rock music, Pete Townshend of the Who heard of Baba from Mike McInnerney in autumn 1967, by his own account to Rolling Stone, and dedicated <i>Tommy</i> (1969) to him, and Wikipedia says the 1971 song "Baba O'Riley" was named in part after him.
