## Life

Ramalingam was born on 5 October 1823 at Marudur, near Chidambaram, the youngest of five children of Ramayya Pillai and Chinnammai. His father died the next year. His mother took the family to Chennai around 1826, where he lived with his eldest brother Sabhapathy. The boy preferred the Kandha Kottam temple to school. An account on the sriramv.com site says his sister-in-law Parvathi persuaded him of the worth of education. The same account adds that as an infant he laughed during the lamp offering at the Chidambaram shrine, and that he spent long hours alone before a lamp set in front of a mirror, coming to name God as the light of grace. Wikipedia says he reported a vision of Murugan as a child. He was married to his niece under family pressure, and the sources say he showed no interest in household life.

He grew up in the Chennai neighbourhood of Ezhukinaru and visited the Tiruvotriyur temple, where he composed his first hymns. In 1858 he left Chennai, debated at Chidambaram, and lived nine years at Karunguzhi, near Vadalur. He became known as Vallalar, "the generous one."

Three institutions mark the Vadalur years. In 1865 he founded a fellowship, first called the Samarasa Vedha Sanmarga Sangam. In 1872 it was renamed (<span class="script" lang="ta">சமரச சுத்த சன்மார்க்க சத்திய சங்கம்</span>), the Samarasa Suddha Sanmarga Sathya Sangam. On 23 May 1867 he opened a free kitchen on land given by local villagers, serving anyone regardless of caste. On 25 January 1872, Thai Poosam day, he opened the Sathya Gnana Sabha, the Hall of True Knowledge.

The poems reached print in his lifetime. The first volume of the *Tiruvarutpa* appeared in 1867 from the Asiatic Press in Madras, edited by his disciple Thozhuvur Velayutha Mudaliar with a short biography appended. Two further instalments followed, in 1880 and 1885, after his disappearance. The editor arranged the verses in sections on the model of the Tirumurai, the canon of Shaiva hymns, and Tamil Wiki says the letters of Ramalingam show that the first four books were published with his consent. In October 1873 he raised a flag of brotherhood at his one-room residence at Mettukuppam, the Siddhi Valagam, and gave a last address.

On 30 January 1874, according to his followers, he went into a room at Mettukuppam, told disciples not to open it and said he would not be found there. Wikipedia and other English accounts say the room was opened in May 1874 and found empty. The Tamil account of his disciple Thozhuvur Velayutha Mudaliar, as summarised on Tamil Wiki, says it was opened after a year. Followers believe he merged into the light, and this is a claim of faith.

## The teaching

The centre of his teaching is *jeevakarunyam*, compassion for every living being. The scholar C. Srinivasan, writing in 1968, called it his "watchword" and described absolute compassion toward all living things. In practice it meant feeding the hungry without asking their caste, refusing to kill animals for food, and treating cruelty as the barrier to knowing God. He taught that God is Arutperunjothi, the light of grace, and gave his followers a short mantra, *Arutperunjothi, Thanipperunkarunai* (<span class="script" lang="ta">அருட்பெருஞ்ஜோதி தனிப்பெருங்கருணை</span>), which Srinivasan translates "Supreme Grace-Light: Supreme Universal Compassion."

He called his way **Suddha Sanmarga**, the "pure true path." He placed it above six older paths including Vedanta and Siddhanta, and he rejected caste, idol worship and elaborate ritual. The Sathya Gnana Sabha reflects this. It is an octagonal, lotus-like hall with no image. An iron chain of 21,600 knots, the number of breaths a person is said to take in a day, fences it. Five steps rise on each side to a central altar. A sanctum is hidden behind seven curtains, and worshippers face a lamp's flame as a symbol of the Divine. The Sabha's own site says the curtains stand for the barriers that hide the light and were removed one by one at the dedication in 1872. It also says he locked the hall in 1873 because visitors were looking at it with the outer senses alone. Wikipedia adds that meat-eaters may worship only from outside. The lamp is kept lit at all times. The site gives the instruction for worship as standing at the central door, fixing a concentrated mind at the root of the nose between the eyebrows, and praying for grace. It places the divine centre inside the person, so that the body is the true temple and the hall an outer symbol of that inner truth.

His most unusual claim is about the body. Srinivasan summarises the aim as the "conquest of death" through grace, a progressive transformation of the body through stages he named the golden body, the body of grace and the body of bliss. Followers read the disappearance story in this light.

**The poems.** The *Tiruvarutpa* holds about 5,800 poems in six books. In one widely sung piece, as the Tamil Nation site paraphrases it, he asks for the company of sincere devotees and freedom from hypocrites. Another asks Shiva to bless every kind of person equally, the learned and the unlearned, the strong and the weak, the good and the wicked. The same page says he valued plain language for spiritual ideas.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="vallalar" />

His hymns belong to the Tamil Shaiva tradition that runs back to the Nayanars. No human guru is named in the sources consulted. His influence runs forward. Accounts of the life of Vethathiri Maharishi (1911 to 2006) say he drew inspiration from Ramalinga. This is a connection by testimony, since Vethathiri was born decades after Ramalinga's disappearance.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Feed without asking caste.</strong> The free kitchen at Vadalur, opened in 1867, served anyone, and it still runs through volunteers.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Refrain from killing.</strong> The path asks for a meatless diet and for compassion toward every living creature.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Sit before a lamp.</strong> The flame is a symbol of the Divine and not a deity, and his last address recommends meditation on it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Fix the attention.</strong> The Sabha's instruction places it at the root of the nose, between the eyebrows, with a prayer for grace.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Sing the poems.</strong> Followers sing the <i>Tiruvarutpa</i> and hold classes on the <i>Kural</i>.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Repeat the mantra.</strong> The short formula <span class="script" lang="ta">அருட்பெருஞ்ஜோதி தனிப்பெருங்கருணை</span> is the one he gave his followers.</div></li>

## Critiques and questions

**The naming dispute.** The traditionalist scholar Arumuga Navalar rejected Vallalar's hymns, calling them *Marutpa*, poems of delusion, on the argument that the title *Tiruvarutpa* belonged to ancient Shaiva works. The feud, called the Arutpa-Marutpa dispute, was carried on by followers on both sides, and Tamil Wiki dates it from 1867 to 1904. An article on the print publication of the *Tiruvarutpa* in *Modern Asian Studies* describes the printed first volume as modelled on editions of Tamil classics such as the *Periya Purana*, a way of claiming a place beside the canon. It reports that opponents mocked Ramalingam as "Calculation Ramalinga," a jibe at his lower standing than the Vellalar castes that dominated Shaiva institutions.

**The disappearance.** The accounts consulted differ on when the room was opened, and the claim that he merged into light rests on his followers' testimony.
