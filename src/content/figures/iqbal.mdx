## Life

Muhammad Iqbal was born on 9 November 1877 in Sialkot, in the Punjab, into a family of Kashmiri descent. He studied at the Scotch Mission College in Sialkot and then at Government College, Lahore, where he took a BA in 1897 and an MA in philosophy in 1899. At Lahore the orientalist Thomas Arnold became his teacher and friend. He began publishing Urdu poems in the journal *Makhzan* from 1901; "Tarana-e-Hind" ("Saare Jahan se Achha"), still sung in India, dates from this period.

In 1905 he went to Europe. He took a BA at Trinity College, Cambridge (1907), earned a doctorate at Munich with a thesis on *The Development of Metaphysics in Persia* (the degree is dated 1907 or 1908 in different sources), and was called to the bar at Lincoln's Inn in 1908. He returned to Lahore and practised law for the rest of his working life, while poetry and philosophy became his real work.

His long Persian poem *Asrar-i-Khudi* (The Secrets of the Self) was published in Lahore in September 1915, followed by *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi* (The Mysteries of Selflessness) in 1917 or 1918 (sources differ). R. A. Nicholson's English translation of the first, *The Secrets of the Self* (1920), made him known in Europe. Later collections include *Payam-i-Mashriq* (1923), a reply to Goethe, the Urdu *Bang-i-Dara* (1924), and *Javid Nama* (1932), a journey through the heavens in which Rumi is his guide. He was knighted in the New Year honours of 1923 (some sources give 1922).

At the request of the Madras Muslim Association he gave six lectures at Madras, Hyderabad and Aligarh, in 1928 and 1929 by the usual dating. They were published at Lahore in 1930 and, with a seventh lecture, by Oxford University Press in 1934 as *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*.

Iqbal also took part in politics. In December 1930, as president of the annual session of the All-India Muslim League at Allahabad, he proposed a consolidated Muslim state in north-western India, and he attended the Round Table Conferences in London. Pakistan regards him as its national poet and as one of the thinkers behind its creation. He died in Lahore on 21 April 1938.

## The teaching

**Khudi.** Iqbal's central word is *khudi*, the self or ego. In ordinary Persian and Urdu it can mean selfishness; Iqbal used it for the individuality that each person has the task of building. The self grows through desire, love, effort and action, and weakens through passivity and imitation. Its aim is to become more fully itself, to the point where it can stand in God's presence without being dissolved.

**Against self-annihilation.** This set him against a strand of Sufism and of Indian thought that sees the goal as *fana*, the passing away of the self into God. The first edition of *Asrar-i-Khudi* opened with couplets attacking the poet Hafiz as a teacher of intoxicated passivity. Khwaja Hasan Nizami and other Sufis protested, and the argument ran in print for years. Iqbal dropped the Hafiz couplets and the preface from the second edition of 1918, but kept his position. He did not reject Sufism as a whole. Rumi, whom he called his master, was in his reading a poet of striving love and endless growth.

**An unfinished universe.** In the *Reconstruction* lectures Iqbal set this view of the self inside a philosophy of religion that drew on the Quran, on the Muslim philosophers and on Henri Bergson's idea of creative time. Reality, he argued, is a living process. The universe is "not a block universe, a finished product, immobile and incapable of change." God is the ultimate self, and creation continues; human beings share in it. He wrote that "God becomes a co-worker with him, provided man takes the initiative." Personal immortality was for him something a self could achieve by growing strong, and could fail to reach.

**Ijtihad.** The same logic applied to law and society. Iqbal called *ijtihad*, independent reasoning on questions of Islamic law, the principle of movement in Islam, and urged that it be revived after centuries in which jurists had mostly followed earlier rulings.

He read Nietzsche closely and borrowed his image of the self-overcoming individual, while rejecting his atheism; scholars still argue about how much of the perfect man of Iqbal's poems comes from Nietzsche and how much from the Sufi idea of the perfect human.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="iqbal" />

Iqbal took no initiation into a Sufi order that he made central to his teaching. His tree shows influences, all documented in his own writings. Islamic theology and philosophy, Rumi within the Sufi tradition, and his teacher Thomas Arnold shaped him. His relation to the Sufi doctrine of fana is better described as a reaction than an inheritance. His heirs are the many Muslim modernists, in South Asia and beyond, who took up the *Reconstruction* as a starting point.
