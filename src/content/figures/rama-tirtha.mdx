## Life

Tirath Ram was born on Diwali, 22 October 1873, in Muraliwala, a village a few miles from Gujranwala in the Punjab, into a family of Goswami Brahmins of modest means. His father was Hiranand Goswami. His mother died when he was an infant, a few days old in some accounts and about a year old in others, and he was raised by an elder brother, Gurudas, or by his father's sister; the sources differ. As was common, he was married as a boy, at about ten or eleven.

He was a gifted student of mathematics. With help from a municipal scholarship he studied at Forman Christian College in Lahore, a college run by American Presbyterian missionaries, and took an MA in mathematics at Government College, Lahore, in 1895. He then taught mathematics at Forman Christian College and at Oriental College, Lahore. One of his early pamphlets was on how to study mathematics.

He was at the same time an ardent devotee of Krishna, and he gave talks on devotion for the Sanatana Dharma Sabha in Lahore. He began to read Vedanta under the influence of Swami Madhava Tirtha of the Dwarka Math, one of the monasteries associated with Shankara. In November 1897 Swami Vivekananda came to Lahore and lectured there, and Tirath Ram helped arrange the visit. His disciple Puran Singh tells the story that the young professor gave Vivekananda a gold watch, and that Vivekananda put it back in his host's pocket, saying he would wear it there. Puran Singh and later writers treat the visit as the moment that turned him toward renunciation.

Over the next few years he withdrew in stages, giving away his possessions, going off to meditate by the Ganga near Rishikesh and returning to teach. He took sannyasa and the name Rama Tirtha around the turn of the century; the date is given as Diwali 1899, as 1900, and as early 1901 in different sources. He left his wife and children and his post. He lived for a time in the forests at Brahmapuri, a few miles from Rishikesh, and by the accounts of his followers he carried no money and no luggage.

In 1902, with the support of Kirti Shah, the Raja of Tehri, he went to Japan. In Tokyo in September 1902 he met a young Sikh student, Puran Singh, who became a sannyasi under his influence for a time and later his biographer. From Japan he went on to the United States, where he spent about two years, largely in California. He stayed with Dr Albert Hiller and his wife, spent time at Shasta Springs, gave many lectures in and around San Francisco, and tried to arrange places for Indian students at American universities. His American hosts wrote his talks down.

He returned to India in 1904 and lectured to large crowds. By 1906 he was ill and had withdrawn to the Himalayas, near Tehri, where he planned a systematic book on practical Vedanta that he never finished. On Diwali, 17 October 1906, he went into the Ganga and drowned. His followers say he gave his body to the river, and the *Encyclopaedia of Sikhism* calls it a deliberate act, citing an address to death found on his desk. Other accounts treat it as a drowning without saying more. He was thirty-three. Muhammad Iqbal wrote an elegy for him.

## The teaching

Rama Tirtha's teaching is Advaita: the self of each person is the one reality, God, and the sense of being a separate, needy individual is the root of suffering. He spoke of himself in the third person as "Rama," and his followers explain the habit as a practice of standing apart from the personal ego. His lectures are full of the language of joy. Puran Singh's preface quotes him in a typical vein: "All that thou seest, that thou art."

**Vedanta for use.** He called his teaching practical Vedanta, the same phrase Vivekananda used, and meant something similar by it. A line from his notebook, quoted in *The Tribune*, puts it bluntly: "Vedanta printed in books and placed in shelves to be eaten by the worms won't do." Knowledge of the self was supposed to show up in fearlessness, cheerful hard work and freedom from anxiety about possessions. He wrote in his notebook that "Poverty is blessed."

**Oneness and the nation.** He drew social conclusions from non-duality. He spoke against caste distinctions, for the education of women, children and labourers, and for the training of young Indians in science and industry abroad. He tied self-realisation to love of country: "Tune yourself in love with your country and people," in a passage quoted by the Divine Life Society. Bhagat Singh later named him as one of the Punjab's contributions to the national movement, and the revolutionary Ram Prasad Bismil wrote a poem about him.

**Many religions, one truth.** The *Encyclopaedia of Sikhism* notes his equal respect for all religions and his particular regard for Sikhism, Islam and Christianity. His followers describe him as at home in Persian, Urdu, Hindi, Sanskrit and English literature, and say that he once lectured in Persian at a mosque in Egypt.

His writings and American lectures were edited after his death, much of it by Puran Singh, and published as *In Woods of God-Realization*. The Swami Rama Tirtha Pratisthan keeps them in print.

## Inherited and new

  <div class="cols">
    <div>
      <span class="label">Inherited</span>
      <ul>
        <li>Shankara's Advaita, through the Dwarka Math teacher Madhava Tirtha.</li>
        <li>Punjabi Krishna devotion and the Sanatana Dharma revival in Lahore.</li>
        <li>Vivekananda's example: the English-speaking monk lecturing on Vedanta in the West.</li>
        <li>The Persian and Urdu literary culture of Lahore.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div>
      <span class="label">New</span>
      <ul>
        <li>An exuberant, poetic Vedanta of joy, delivered in the first person as "Rama."</li>
        <li>Self-realisation tied explicitly to national renewal, hard work and technical education.</li>
        <li>An early Punjabi and North Indian voice for Neo-Vedanta, bringing the Bengal movement's themes to Lahore and the Himalayas.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p class="final">My verdict: his doctrine is Vivekananda's practical Advaita in a different temperament, more lyrical and more personal; his originality lies in its tone and in carrying it into Punjabi and Urdu culture.</p>

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="rama-tirtha" />

Rama Tirtha had no single guru. The tree shows Vivekananda as an influence, based on Puran Singh's account of the Lahore visit of November 1897, and Madhava Tirtha of the Dwarka Math as his first teacher in Vedanta. Below him stand Puran Singh, who met him in Tokyo in 1902 and became his biographer, and his sister's son Hariwansh Lal Poonja, the teacher known as Papaji, born four years after his death. That family tie, reported by Papaji and his biographers, links the Neo-Vedanta of this part to the direct path of Part 3.
