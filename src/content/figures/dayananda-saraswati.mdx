<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> He was born Mul Shankar. As a celibate student he took the name Shuddha Chaitanya, and at his initiation as a monk he became Dayananda, "bliss of compassion." Saraswati is the name of the monastic order. He should not be confused with Swami Dayananda Saraswati of Arsha Vidya (1930–2015), a twentieth-century teacher of Advaita who appears in Part 2.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Varanasi, 16 November 1869. In a garden called Anand Bagh, under the presidency of the Maharaja of Benares, Ishwari Prasad Narayan Singh, a wandering monk in his mid-forties faces more than two dozen of the city's pandits. Their leaders are Swami Vishuddhananda and the grammarian Bal Shastri. A large crowd has come to watch.</p>
<p>The question is whether the Vedas sanction the worship of images. The monk's position is simple and, in Varanasi, scandalous. He asks the pandits to show him one verse in the Samhitas, the four ancient collections of Vedic hymns, that commands image worship. Later commentaries and the Puranas will not do. He wants the text itself.</p>
<p>What happened next depends on whom you read. Orthodox accounts say he was defeated on points of scripture and grammar. Arya Samaj accounts say the pandits could not produce the verse, and that the meeting ended in shouting and disorder before he could reply. Historians agree that after this day the monk from Kathiawar was known across northern India.</p>

## Life

Mul Shankar was born on 12 February 1824 in Tankara, a small town in the Morvi state of Kathiawar, in present-day Gujarat. The date comes from a lunar reckoning, and some early Arya Samaj publications give the year as 1825. His father, Karshanji Lalji Tiwari, was a prosperous Brahmin and a strict devotee of Shiva. His mother was Yashodabai. He received the sacred thread at eight and began learning Sanskrit and the Yajurveda by heart.

The story that the Arya Samaj tells about his awakening comes from his own account. At about fourteen, on the night of Shivaratri, his father made him keep the full fast and vigil in the temple. Late in the night, with the others asleep, he watched a mouse come out of its hole, run over the image of Shiva and eat the offerings. He asked himself whether this could be the god of the stories, who rules Mount Kailash and grants boons and curses. In the translation the Arya Samaj prints, he concluded: "This image has not the power to drive away even an insignificant mouse from its presence!" He went home and broke his fast. His father was furious.

A few years later his younger sister died, and then a paternal uncle he loved. In his account these deaths turned the boy's doubt about the image into a question about death itself and how to escape it. When his parents began arranging his marriage, he left home, in 1846, at about twenty-two, and did not return.

For the next fourteen years or so he wandered. He was given the name Shuddha Chaitanya as a brahmachari, a celibate student, and in the late 1840s he took full sannyasa from a monk of the Saraswati order, usually named as Purnananda Saraswati, at Chanod on the Narmada river. He studied Vedanta and practised yoga with teachers in Gujarat and along the Narmada, walked into the Himalaya, and visited the pilgrimage towns of the north. His own short autobiography, written in 1879 and 1880 at the request of Colonel Henry Olcott for *The Theosophist*, covers these years and stops around 1860.

In 1860 he reached Mathura and became the student of Swami Virjananda, a blind grammarian then in his eighties. Virjananda had lost his sight to smallpox at five and ran a small school where students paid no fees. He held a firm view about which books were worth reading. The ancient works of the rishis, the *arsha* texts such as Panini's grammar, were true. Later works, the *anarsha* texts written by ordinary men, were corrupt. Dayananda's biographers say his teacher told him to throw his later grammar books into the Yamuna. He stayed about three years. When he left, the story goes, he offered the customary gift to his teacher, a handful of cloves. Virjananda asked instead for a promise that he would spend his life spreading the knowledge of the Vedas and fighting the errors of the later books. Virjananda died in 1868.

The 1860s were spent preaching in the Gangetic plain, still in Sanskrit, still almost naked, wearing a loincloth. The Kashi debate of November 1869 made him famous. From December 1872 to April 1873 he stayed in Calcutta, where he met the leaders of the Brahmo Samaj, the reform society founded by Rammohan Roy. Debendranath Tagore visited him, and Keshab Chandra Sen discussed rebirth and non-duality with him and advised him to lecture in Hindi so that ordinary people could follow. He took the advice. From then on he spoke and wrote in Hindi, which he called *Arya bhasha*, the language of the Aryas.

His main book, the *Satyarth Prakash*, "The Light of the Meaning of Truth," was first published in Benares in 1875. On 10 April 1875 he founded the first Arya Samaj, the Society of Aryas or Society of the Noble, in Bombay, with a constitution of twenty-eight rules. The Samaj spread fastest in Punjab. At Lahore in 1877 the rules were cut down to the ten principles that the movement still uses.

He wrote fast for the rest of his life. He produced an introduction to the Vedas, the *Rigvedadi Bhashya Bhumika*, a complete Hindi and Sanskrit commentary on the Yajurveda, and a commentary on the Rigveda that had reached hymn 7.61 when he died. He wrote a manual of household rites, the *Sanskar Vidhi*, and a pamphlet in defence of the cow. In 1878 he entered an alliance with the Theosophical Society of Helena Blavatsky and Henry Olcott, who at first took him to be a teacher of their own universal wisdom. The partnership fell apart over whether he taught a personal God, and in March 1882 he attacked the founders publicly in Bombay as Buddhists, not supporters of the Vedas. In 1880 he set up the Paropkarini Sabha, a trust to carry on his work and publish his books. In 1882 and 1883, at the Navlakha Mahal in Udaipur, he prepared the revised second edition of the *Satyarth Prakash*.

In 1883 the Maharaja of Jodhpur, Jaswant Singh II, invited him to stay. He fell seriously ill there at the end of September. Arya Samaj biographies say he had rebuked the Maharaja for his attachment to a courtesan, Nanhi Jan, and that she bribed his cook, Jagannath, to put poison or ground glass in his milk; they add that the cook confessed and that Dayananda forgave him and gave him money to escape. This account comes from his followers and has not been independently confirmed. He was moved to Mount Abu and then to Ajmer, where he died at the Bhinai House on the evening of 30 October 1883, the night of Diwali. Arya accounts say he died reciting the Gayatri mantra, with the words "Thy will be done."

<aside class="note"><strong>Samhita.</strong> A "collection." The four Vedic Samhitas (Rig, Yajur, Sama and Atharva) are the hymns and formulas themselves. The Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads were composed later as commentary and reflection. Most Hindu traditions count all of them as Veda. Dayananda counted only the Samhitas.</aside>

## The teaching

Dayananda's answer to the question of this part is a confident yes. The Vedas can be read by reason because, read correctly, they *are* reason. He did not set reason against revelation, as Rammohan Roy sometimes did, or move the seat of authority into the heart, as Debendranath Tagore did. He held that God had given human beings one perfect book at the start of the world, and that every true thing found later by science or philosophy was already in it. Everything that contradicted reason in the Hindu inheritance came from later books, and those books could be discarded.

**One book and its servants.** The first three of the ten principles set out the structure. "God is the efficient cause of all true knowledge and all that is known through knowledge." God is "formless, omnipotent, just, merciful," and "He alone is worthy of being worshipped." Third: "The Vedas are the scriptures of all true knowledge." In the statement of beliefs printed at the end of the *Satyarth Prakash*, he says the four Vedas are "absolutely free from error, and are an authority unto themselves." The Brahmanas, the auxiliary sciences, the Upanishads and the later law books are "works of a dependent character," to be accepted only where they agree with the Vedas. The Puranas, which most Hindus of his day knew far better than any Veda, he rejected outright, along with the Tantras. He said the true *puranas* were the old Brahmana texts, and that the *Bhagavata* "and other books of that sort are not true (real) Puranas."

This was a narrower canon than any major school of Hindu philosophy had used. Shankara, Ramanuja and Madhva built their systems on the Upanishads. Dayananda moved the Upanishads to the second rank and put the hymns at the centre.

**How the revelation came.** The Arya Samaj teaches that the Vedas were given at the beginning of creation to four sages, Agni, Vayu, Aditya and Angiras. Since the revelation is older than all history, Dayananda argued, it cannot mention historical people or events. "There are no stories in the Vedas," he wrote in the introduction to his commentary. Names that look like those of kings, rivers or gods have to be read by their root meaning. This is his *yaugika* method, from *yoga*, "joining," the meaning that comes from a word's derivation. He relied on Panini's grammar and Yaska's ancient etymological dictionary, the *Nirukta*, both *arsha* works by his teacher's standard.

The method produced striking results. Agni, the fire god of the hymns, becomes in many verses a name of the one God. The battle of Indra with the serpent Vritra becomes a lesson in natural science and right conduct. Where a hymn seems to invoke many gods, Dayananda reads many names of one God, or natural forces that God governs. He also held that the Vedas contain the principles of the sciences, including mathematics and the physical sciences, and his commentaries find them there.

**Three eternal things.** His theology is often called *traitavada*, the doctrine of three. God, individual souls and *prakriti*, the primal matter of the universe, are all beginningless. God shapes the world out of matter, sustains it and dissolves it in endless cycles, and gives each soul the fruit of its actions. God is the efficient cause of the world and never its material cause. Here he broke with Advaita Vedanta, the philosophy of the Saraswati order he belonged to, which teaches that Brahman alone is real and that the individual self is finally identical with it. For Dayananda, God and the soul "are two distinct entities," related "as the pervader and the pervaded," like father and son. The soul is eternal, finite in knowledge, and free to act.

Two consequences follow. God, being formless and all-pervading, never takes a body, so there are no avatars, and the *Satyarth Prakash* denies that God ever incarnates. And since God is everywhere and has no form, an image of God is an error. He called the worship of "dead, inert objects" wrong. He applied the same reasoning to pilgrimage: "So-called sacred places on land and water are not tirthas." A real *tirtha*, a crossing place, is whatever carries a person across suffering, such as truthfulness, knowledge and good company. He rejected offerings to dead ancestors in the popular form and taught that the real *shraddha* is service to living parents.

**Liberation with a term.** For Dayananda, *moksha* is the soul's release from suffering into the bliss of God, gained by knowledge, right action and worship. The soul does not dissolve into God, because it is a distinct entity. And liberation does not last for ever. The liberated soul enjoys it for an immense period, the *parantakala*, the lifetime of the creator Brahma, after which it takes a body again. He argued that a finite soul with finite merit cannot earn an infinite reward. Few Hindu teachers before him had said this.

**Varna by merit.** He kept the four *varnas* of the old social order, priests, rulers, traders and labourers, but held that a person's class "should be determined by his merits," and he opened the study of the Vedas to all, including women and those of low birth. This was one of the places where his teaching and his reforms were the same thing. If the Vedas are God's knowledge for humanity, every human being is entitled to read them.

**Argument as a religious act.** The last four chapters of the *Satyarth Prakash* examine other religions in turn: the sects of India in chapter 11, the Charvaka materialists, Buddhists and Jains in chapter 12, Christianity in chapter 13 and Islam in chapter 14. He quotes the scriptures of each and tests them, as he understood it, against reason and the Vedas. He treated public debate, the old Indian *shastrartha*, as a duty, and he debated pandits, missionaries and maulvis wherever he went.

<aside class="note"><strong>Shastrartha.</strong> A formal public debate on the meaning of scripture between learned opponents, often before a ruler or an assembly. It is an old Indian form. Dayananda made it his main method of preaching.</aside>

What strikes me most is how much he dared. He took the most authoritative and least read part of the Hindu inheritance and claimed that it had been misread for two thousand years by the very commentators who preserved it. His doctrines of God, soul and matter have older roots in Nyaya, Yoga and the dualist schools, but the combination, the narrow canon and the finite liberation are his own. Whether his readings of the hymns are right is a separate matter, and most specialists outside the Arya Samaj think they are not. As a piece of religious thinking it is one of the most original of the century.

## Lineage

Dayananda belongs to no single line. He was initiated in the Saraswati branch of Shankara's monastic order and then rejected its philosophy. His real teacher was Virjananda, and his relation to the Brahmo reformers in Calcutta was a mix of advice taken and doctrine refused.

<MiniTree id="dayananda-saraswati" />

The documented edges are his study with Virjananda in Mathura, which he described himself, and his meetings with Debendranath Tagore and Keshab Chandra Sen in Calcutta in 1872–73, which are in his early biographies. The Brahmos had given up the infallibility of the Vedas by the 1850s, and he regarded that as their great mistake. His relation to the Upanishads is a demotion more than a descent. The edges running out from him are institutional and well recorded: the Arya Samaj and its schools, the Gurukul movement of Swami Shraddhananda, and many twentieth-century figures who read the *Satyarth Prakash* young, Baba Ramdev among them. Sri Aurobindo, who did not follow him, wrote that Dayananda "will be honoured as the first discoverer of the right clues" to the Veda.

## The practice

Dayananda gave his followers a daily discipline drawn from Vedic household religion, set out in the *Satyarth Prakash* and the *Sanskar Vidhi*. The Arya Samaj describes it as part of the five daily duties, the *pancha mahayajna*.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Sandhya.</strong> Morning and evening worship at the meeting of night and day: sipping water, breath control, recitation of the Gayatri and other Vedic verses, and silent contemplation of the formless God. No image is used.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Svadhyaya.</strong> Daily reading and recitation of the Vedas and of books consistent with them.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Havan.</strong> The fire offering (also called <i>agnihotra</i> or <i>deva yajna</i>), in which ghee and fragrant materials are offered into a small fire while Vedic verses are chanted. Dayananda explained it partly as worship and partly as a way of purifying the air.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Service to the living.</strong> Respect and care for parents and elders in place of rites for the dead, hospitality to learned guests, and feeding animals and the poor.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>The sanskaras.</strong> Sixteen life-cycle rites, from conception to cremation, performed with Vedic verses only and without temple priests.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

Dayananda died in 1883, before sound recording reached India, and no recording or film of him exists. A small number of photographs survive. His voice is in his books, which he dictated in Hindi and Sanskrit, and in the short autobiography he wrote for *The Theosophist* in 1879–80. The *Satyarth Prakash* has been translated into English several times, first by Chiranjiva Bharadwaja in 1906.

## Critiques and questions

**His reading of the Vedas.** Most Vedic scholars, Indian and Western, read the Samhitas as the liturgy of a society that worshipped many gods, and they treat Dayananda's monotheistic and scientific readings as a projection onto the text. In a 1982 study in the *Indian Journal of Theology*, Y. D. Tiwari argued that his theological assumptions decided his results in advance, and that his idea of an infallible scripture owed something to Christian and Muslim views of the Bible and the Quran. The orthodox pandits of Varanasi objected for the opposite reason: he had thrown away the commentaries and later scriptures that tradition used to read the Vedas. Sri Aurobindo took a middle position. He did not accept all of Dayananda's readings, but wrote that "there is then nothing fantastic in Dayananda's idea that Veda contains truth of science as well as truth of religion."

**The Kashi debate.** The outcome of the 1869 debate is still contested. Orthodox accounts record a defeat; Arya Samaj accounts record an unfair and disorderly meeting. Even the date varies in secondary sources, which give 16 November or 22 October 1869.

**Polemics against other religions.** The last four chapters of the *Satyarth Prakash* criticise Jainism, Buddhism, Christianity and Islam in sharp language, and they drew sharp replies, including from the Ahmadiyya founder Mirza Ghulam Ahmad in the 1880s. The government of Sind banned the book in 1944 over its fourteenth chapter, on Islam, and the ban was not lifted there. In 2008 the Delhi High Court dismissed a petition seeking a ban in India. The Arya Samaj's position is that the chapters apply the same test of reason to every faith, including the sects of Hinduism, which chapter 11 criticises at length.

**Niyoga and finite liberation.** Two of his teachings unsettled many Hindus who otherwise admired him. He accepted, under strict rules, the old practice of *niyoga*, in which a widow or a childless wife could have children by an appointed man. And his teaching that liberation ends after a cosmic period contradicts nearly every other Hindu school.

**The theosophists.** Olcott and Blavatsky first presented him to their followers as an adept. The alliance ended in 1882 with public charges on both sides, and the Theosophical record of him afterwards is hostile. The quarrel says more about the gap between his strict Vedic theism and their esoteric universalism than about either party's honesty.

## Descendants

The Arya Samaj outlived him and split within ten years of his death, in 1893, into a "college" party and a "Gurukul" party, divided over diet and education. The college party built the Dayanand Anglo-Vedic schools and colleges, starting in Lahore. The Gurukul party, led by Munshi Ram, later Swami Shraddhananda, founded the Gurukul at Kangri near Haridwar in 1902 to give Vedic education on the old residential model. Lala Lajpat Rai, Shyamji Krishna Varma and many other public figures of the next generation came out of the Samaj, and in the 1920s Shraddhananda led its *shuddhi* campaign to bring converts back to Hinduism. Arya Samaj branches spread with Indian migration to Fiji, Mauritius, Suriname, South Africa and East Africa. The Paropkarini Sabha in Ajmer still publishes his works. Baba Ramdev, in Part 13, has said that reading the *Satyarth Prakash* as a boy set him on the path to a Gurukul education.
