<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Chandrasekhara, "he who wears the moon on his crest," is a name of Shiva, and Chandrasekharendra Saraswati was the monastic name given to him in 1907; seven earlier heads of his monastery had carried it. Devotees call him the Paramacharya, "the supreme teacher," Mahaswami, or in Tamil Maha Periyava, "the great elder." The Tamil title above, <span class="script" lang="ta">தெய்வத்தின் குரல்</span>, is <i>Deivathin Kural</i>, "the voice of God," the name given to the collected volumes of his talks.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Chingleput, south of Madras, January 1931. A thirty-two-year-old English journalist who has come to India looking for a yogi who can prove that the higher yoga is real is taken by an Indian friend to a camp outside the town. The friend's own guru is staying there for a day. Brunton later wrote that he was received by a short man in an ochre robe with a "noble face" and eyes that were "extraordinarily tranquil and beautiful."</p>
<p>The man is thirty-six. He has been head of a South Indian monastery since he was twelve, and that same month he has entered Kanchipuram for the first time in that office. They talk about the state of the world. According to the account the monastery's biographer took from Brunton's book, the head of the monastery tells him that nothing but spiritual understanding between nations will produce goodwill. Then Brunton asks the question he came with. Can the swami direct him to a master who can give him proof?</p>
<p>The swami does not take him on as a disciple. He tells him to go to a sage who lives on the holy hill of Arunachala, Ramana Maharshi, and he makes Brunton promise that he will go. Brunton keeps the promise, and the book he publishes in 1934, <i>A Search in Secret India</i>, makes Ramana Maharshi known in Europe and America. In it he calls the man at Chingleput the spiritual head of South India and the Shankaracharya of Kumbakonam. That place name is itself part of this chapter's story.</p>
</section>

## Life

Swaminathan was born on 20 May 1894 in Villupuram, a railway town about 160 kilometres south of Madras. His father, Subrahmanya Sastri, was a schoolteacher. His mother, Mahalakshmi, came from Eachangudi near Tiruvaiyaru. The family were Smarta Brahmins whose language at home was Kannada; Wikipedia's account says their ancestors had come from Karnataka generations earlier, which it marks as needing a source. He was the second child. His sacred-thread ceremony was performed at Tindivanam in 1905, where he was going to a mission school.

In early 1907 the head of the Kanchi Kamakoti monastery, the 66th in its list, fell ill and died at Kalavai, a village in what is now Ranipet district. His newly installed successor, a young man named Lakshmikantan who was Swaminathan's first cousin, fell ill with a fever and died about a week later. Swaminathan's mother had sent the boy to Kalavai to be with his cousin's family. By his own later account he was taken there by cart and learned on the way that he would not be going home. "I was stunned by this unexpected turn of events," he recalled. On 13 February 1907, aged twelve, he was given the robe and the name Chandrasekharendra Saraswati and became the 68th head. Sources give his age as twelve or thirteen, and one date in Wikipedia's infobox, 9 May 1907, differs from the 13 February date in the body of the same article and in the monastery's own account.

He was then educated as a monk. Pandits taught him at the monastery's base in Kumbakonam from about 1909, and from 1911 to 1914 he studied at Mahendramangalam on the Kaveri. Because he was a minor, a government Court of Wards managed the monastery's property until he turned twenty-one in May 1915. Accounts by devotees say he took a lasting interest in mathematics, astronomy and photography, and the Hinduism Today obituary says he could converse in English and French as well as many Indian languages.

In March 1919 he began a tour of the whole country that lasted twenty years, travelling on foot or by palanquin, camping in villages, holding a nightly puja and giving talks. The tour was called a vijaya yatra, a "victory journey," the traditional name for a Shankaracharya's circuit. T. M. P. Mahadevan, a professor of philosophy at the University of Madras who wrote the monastery's biography of him, lists the stages. In 1927 he reached Palghat and toured Kerala; Gandhi met him there at Nallicheri, in a cattle shed at the camp, the swami speaking Sanskrit and Gandhi Hindi. On 25 January 1931 he entered Kanchipuram, his first visit as head. In September 1933 he set out for the north, reaching Varanasi on 6 October 1934, and in February 1935 he visited the Banaras Hindu University at the invitation of its founder, Madan Mohan Malaviya. He spent the monsoon retreat of 1935 at Kalighat in Calcutta, came back down the east coast through Orissa, and closed the circuit at Rameswaram on 10 June 1939 by bathing the deity there with Ganges water. Mahadevan says he then kept silence for more than six months.

The Kanchi monastery had its administrative seat in Kumbakonam in these years, which is why Brunton and others called him the Shankaracharya of Kumbakonam. Over his long tenure its centre of gravity moved to Kanchipuram, where he stayed for three years from 1953.

At Kanchipuram on 22 March 1954 he gave sannyasa to his chosen successor, a young man named Subrahmanyam, who became Jayendra Saraswati. In 1957 the monastery marked fifty years of his headship at Kalavai, where it had begun. He spent 1957 to 1959 in Madras, his first stay there in about twenty-five years, and received a stream of visitors that included the Chicago anthropologist Milton Singer, the novelist Arthur Koestler and Queen Frederika of Greece. In 1966, for the United Nations Day concert in New York, he wrote a short Sanskrit hymn, <i>Maithreem Bhajata</i>, "cultivate friendship," which M. S. Subbulakshmi sang at the General Assembly hall on 23 October 1966. In later decades he toured Andhra and Maharashtra and camped for a time at Satara. His successor's own disciple, Vijayendra Saraswati, received sannyasa in 1983.

His personal life was spare. For decades, according to Hinduism Today, he ate one meal a day of a simple grain with fruit juice or milk, and he did not touch money offered to him. People queued to tell him their troubles. "I have come to know, as none other perhaps, the endless afflictions of humanity," the same magazine quoted him as saying.

He died at the monastery in Kanchipuram at 2.58 in the afternoon of 8 January 1994, four months short of his hundredth birthday. Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao cancelled his engagements to attend, and the burial was delayed an hour for his arrival. The body was seated in a basket and placed in a pit of salt, sandal paste and herbs, with his staff broken in three beside it. He was the first head of the monastery to be buried on its Kanchipuram premises, and the shrine there opened for worship on 11 January.

<aside class="note"><strong>Shankaracharya.</strong> The title, "teacher in the line of Shankara," is held by the heads of the monasteries that trace themselves to Adi Shankara, the Advaita philosopher usually dated to the eighth century. The holder is a celibate monk, chosen and initiated by his predecessor, and is treated by followers as a living embodiment of the founder.</aside>

## The teaching

Chandrasekharendra Saraswati wrote very little. What survives is speech: decades of talks in Tamil, given at camps and festivals, which the Tamil writer Ra. Ganapathy edited from notes and tape into the seven volumes of <i>Deivathin Kural</i>, each running to about a thousand pages. The first two volumes were translated into English as <i>Hindu Dharma</i>, published by the Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan, and a fuller English edition later appeared as <i>Voice of God</i>. The talks are discursive and patient. He explains a ritual, then the story behind it, then the moral, then the Vedantic meaning, and circles back.

**The Vedas come first.** For him everything rested on the Veda as revealed sound, to be preserved exactly as received. Reciting it, maintaining the households and schools that could recite it, and performing the rites it prescribed were the core of his office. Hinduism Today, in its 1994 obituary, quoted his view of the institution he headed: "When there is no Vedas, where is the need for this mutt? Where is the need for a pontiff?" He wanted a Vedic school in every locality, and the monastery under him funded the teaching of Vedic recitation, set up conferences on temple ritual and sculpture, and rebuilt shrines to Shankara at sites from Rameswaram to Rishikesh.

**Advaita is the goal, and duty is the road.** The end point of his teaching was Shankara's non-dualism: the self and the absolute, Brahman, are one. He did not teach a short path to that knowledge. In the classical scheme he defended, a person first purifies the mind by doing the duties of their station without selfish motive, then turns to devotion, and only then becomes fit for the inquiry that ends in knowledge. Mahadevan records the message he gave at his golden jubilee in 1957, a line from Shankara: "Let action be performed well; thus let God be worshipped!" He spoke of Advaita as the ground under every other view. "Advaita is not a school among schools of thought," Mahadevan reports him saying, and "Though other systems may quarrel with Advaita, Advaita does not quarrel with them."

**All religions point to one God.** On the question this part of the book asks, his answer begins close to Vivekananda's. In the chapter of <i>Hindu Dharma</i> titled "The Unity of Religions" he says that "All religions have one common ideal, worship of the Lord, and all of them proclaim that there is but one God." The forms differ because peoples, places and temperaments differ. "This one God accepts your devotion irrespective of the manner of your worship." Elsewhere, on the many sects within Hinduism, he uses the old image of rivers: "Like the rivers merging in the ocean all these paths have one meeting point, the Paramatman." Mahadevan adds that he spoke of a Madhva saint and a Muslim Sufi as people who had both known the Advaitic experience.

**So stay where you were born.** From the same premise he drew the opposite practical conclusion from Vivekananda's. If God accepts every sincere form of worship, there is no reason to change form. "So there is no need to abandon the religion of your birth and embrace another." He thought conversion insulted both religions involved, since it implied that God was confined to one of them, and he noted that Hindu ritual, for all its detail, has no rite of conversion. The aim among faiths, he said, was harmony. "The goal must be unity, not uniformity." In 1964, when Pope Paul VI visited Bombay and the Hindu Mahasabha planned black-flag protests, he opposed the protest; Hinduism Today quoted him: "We should do nothing disrespectful to the pope." He also told his followers that organised attempts at conversion should be met by deeper religious discipline at home.

**Varna is duty by birth.** The same reasoning governed his defence of varnashrama dharma, the order of the four classes of society and the four stages of life. In the chapter "Character and Vocation by Birth" he reads the <i>Bhagavad Gita</i>'s statement that the classes are made according to temperament as meaning that temperament itself comes by birth: "Sri Krsna establishes that an individual owes his caste to his birth." Children raised in a family trade, he argued, "naturally develop a liking and aptitude for the family vocation," and a society that kept to hereditary callings had harmony and little competition. He presented each group's rules as duties for the good of all. In "Brahmins are not a Privileged Caste" he argued that the Brahmin's discipline was the hardest: study the Vedas, teach without charging, live on what is given, "however small the sum may be." When modern India abandoned these divisions, he said, "it has become topsy-turvy."

He held this line all his life while making changes at its edges. Hinduism Today, in its centenary profile of 1993, reported that he gave up the palanquin traditionally carried by non-Brahmin bearers, that the monastery began serving free meals to Dalits, and that he refused to sanction dowry among those who sought his blessing. Wikipedia's account says he encouraged women to recite the Vishnu Sahasranama, the thousand names of Vishnu. The same 1993 profile says he declined to condemn the caste system outright despite appeals from Gandhi and Nehru, and gives his reason in his own words: "If I myself were to relax them, how much more lax will the people become?"

<aside class="note"><strong>Varnashrama dharma.</strong> The classical scheme of four social classes (varna) and four stages of life (ashrama), each with its own duties. Defenders read it as a division of labour for the common good; critics read it as the scriptural basis of caste hierarchy. In practice it overlaps with jati, the many hereditary birth-groups of Indian society.</aside>

I read him as the most useful foil in this part of the book. Vivekananda and Radhakrishnan took the old idea that all paths lead to one goal and made it a modern creed of tolerance and exchange. The Paramacharya accepted the same premise and drew a conservative conclusion from it, that each person should keep the path of their birth, in religion as in caste. Judged by originality he scores low, and he would have taken that as praise. His achievement was to make the case for staying put sound reasonable to millions of listeners in the century that was most eager to move.

## Lineage

He stands in the line the Kanchi monastery traces back to Adi Shankara, as its 68th head. His initiation in 1907 was an emergency succession after the deaths of the 66th and 67th heads within days of each other.

<MiniTree id="chandrasekharendra-saraswati" />

The succession edges are documented by the monastery and by the press of the time: from his cousin Mahadevendra Saraswati, the 67th head, to him in 1907, and from him to Jayendra Saraswati in 1954. How far the line runs back beyond the eighteenth century is the subject of a long dispute, set out below under Critiques. The edge to **Paul Brunton** is documented in Brunton's own book and in the monastery's biography: in January 1931 he sent Brunton to **Ramana Maharshi**, and through Brunton's book that meeting shaped how the West first heard of Ramana. His meeting with **Gandhi** in 1927 is recorded by Mahadevan, though neither man seems to have changed the other's views.

## The practice

He taught the ordinary practice of a Smarta Hindu household and asked people to keep it. His talks return to the same few things again and again.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Daily rites.</strong> Perform the duties of one's station: the twice-born at dawn and dusk, the householder at the domestic fire and shrine, everyone through daily worship of a chosen deity.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep the Veda alive.</strong> Support families and schools that recite it, and learn what one can. He saw the loss of recitation as the loss of the tradition itself.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Devotion in shared forms.</strong> Recite the Vishnu Sahasranama, sing the Tamil devotional poems of the Tiruppavai and Tiruvempavai in the winter month of Margazhi, and visit temples; he started a children's singing competition for these poems.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Charity without display.</strong> Give land and food, marry without dowry, and live simply. At his sixty-first birthday in 1954 he asked followers to endow land for charitable use.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Silence and inquiry.</strong> For those ready, the last stage is Advaitic contemplation. In his own evenings, Mahadevan writes, he sat in silence after the puja before speaking, a practice Mahadevan links to Dakshinamurti, Shiva as the teacher who teaches in silence.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Is Kanchi one of Shankara's monasteries?** The monastery holds that Adi Shankara founded it at Kanchipuram, spent his last years there, and that its list of sixty-eight heads runs unbroken from him. Its traditional chronology places Shankara in 509–477 BCE and the founding in 482 BCE, far earlier than the eighth-century date accepted by most historians. It says the seat moved to Kumbakonam in the eighteenth century to escape war and returned to Kanchipuram later. Supporters cite copper-plate inscriptions published by the epigraphist T. A. Gopinatha Rao in 1916, which he read as showing the monastery at Kanchipuram until 1686, and eighteenth-century records of royal grants. The Sringeri monastery in Karnataka, which the older tradition counts among Shankara's four seats, does not recognise Kanchi's claim. Critics argue that the Kumbakonam monastery began as a branch of Sringeri, often dated to 1821, and acquired its Kanchi identity in the nineteenth century, when the East India Company's collector made its head sole trustee of the Kamakshi temple in Kanchipuram in 1842 over the protests of the temple's hereditary priests. In 1987 V. R. Sharma published <i>Kanchi Kamakoti Math: A Myth</i>, and the journalist K. P. Sunil argued in the <i>Illustrated Weekly of India</i> that the monastery's history had been rewritten in the twentieth century under Chandrasekharendra Saraswati. The scholar Vidyasankar Sundaresan, reviewing the evidence on the Indology list in 2000, concluded that the documents support the monastery's official list only from the 1700s. Other scholars, such as C. J. Fuller, treat the Kanchi heads as Shankara's spiritual descendants regardless of the date of the institution. The question is about the institution's age; neither side disputes that he was trained and initiated in a living Advaita lineage.

**Caste.** His defence of varna by birth drew steady criticism from the Dravidian movement in Tamil Nadu, which saw the monastery as the head of Brahmin privilege. Hinduism Today reports that followers of the Dravidar Kazhagam paraded his portrait garlanded with sandals in the mid-1960s and that he made no public reply. K. Veeramani, the Dravidar Kazhagam's leader, published a critique in 1988, <i>Kanchi Sankarachariar: Saint or Sectarian?</i> Wikipedia's account of the monastery says the Vedic education it fostered under him was directed at male Brahmin students. His own answer, given throughout <i>Hindu Dharma</i>, was that each group's duties served all, that Brahmins carried the heaviest discipline, and that hatred between groups was the real harm. Readers will weigh those arguments differently; the book reports both.

**The legend.** Devotees call him the "walking God" and describe him in his lifetime as an avatar. A large literature of devotees' testimony tells of healings, foreknowledge and answered prayers. These are claims made by followers. The monastery's own biographer, Mahadevan, stresses his learning and discipline over miracles.

## Descendants

His successor, Jayendra Saraswati, took the monastery in a more public direction, founding schools, hospitals and a deemed university. He left the monastery for a period in 1987 and was readmitted by the senior swami. Ten years after Chandrasekharendra's death, on 11 November 2004, Jayendra was arrested in connection with the murder of A. Sankararaman, the manager of the Varadaraja Perumal temple in Kanchipuram; the case was transferred to Puducherry, and in November 2013 the Puducherry sessions court acquitted him, his disciple Vijayendra Saraswati and the other accused. Jayendra died on 28 February 2018, and Vijayendra heads the monastery today. The older swami's lasting influence runs less through the institution than through his talks. <i>Deivathin Kural</i> remains one of the most widely read religious works in Tamil, and his stance, that the religions share a goal while each should keep its own forms, remains the main orthodox reply to the universalism of Vivekananda and Radhakrishnan.
