## Life

The man later known as Swami Dayananda Saraswati of Arsha Vidya was born Natarajan on 15 August 1930 in Manjakkudi, a village in the Kaveri delta, the eldest of four sons of a Brahmin family. His father died when he was eight. At seventeen he moved to Madras, learned English and worked in journalism and office jobs, and for a time enlisted in the Air Force. He should not be confused with Swami Dayananda Saraswati of the Arya Samaj (1824–1883), who appears in Part 1.

In 1952 he heard Swami Chinmayananda lecture in Madras, joined the new Chinmaya Mission and became its secretary within a year. A meeting with Swami Pranavananda at Gudivada in Andhra Pradesh in 1961 changed how he understood the texts. His student and biographer Anantanand Rambachan credits Pranavananda with showing him that the Veda is a *pramana*, a valid source of knowledge, in the same sense that the eyes are a valid source of knowledge of colour. In 1962 Chinmayananda gave him sannyasa and the name Dayananda Saraswati. He also spent time in Rishikesh studying the Brahma Sutras.

From 1963 he worked at Sandeepany Sadhanalaya, Chinmayananda's residential school in Bombay, and became its principal teacher. From the early 1970s he ran its intensive multi-year courses in Vedanta and Sanskrit himself. Rambachan, who arrived from Trinidad in 1973, describes him teaching Shankara's *Tattvabodha*, the Upanishads and the Gita with Shankara's commentaries, verse by verse. In 1979 he began a three-year course at the mission's centre in Piercy, California. He went on to teach ten such long courses and to found his own centres under the name Arsha Vidya, "knowledge of the rishis": the Arsha Vidya Pitham at Rishikesh, the Arsha Vidya Gurukulam at Saylorsburg, Pennsylvania (1986), another at Anaikatti near Coimbatore, and one at Nagpur.

In his later years he became a public organiser for Hindu causes. He convened the Hindu Dharma Acharya Sabha, a body of heads of monasteries, and founded AIM for Seva in 2000 to run hostels for rural children. He spoke at the Millennium World Peace Summit of religious leaders at the United Nations in 2000 and took part in Hindu–Jewish and Hindu–Buddhist dialogues. He died at his ashram on the Ganga at Rishikesh on 23 September 2015, and was awarded the Padma Bhushan posthumously in 2016.

## The teaching

Dayananda taught Shankara's Advaita, but with a strong view of what kind of thing Vedanta is. In his account, the Upanishads are a *pramana*, a means of knowledge. The human problem is ignorance of what one already is, a sense of being small, incomplete and wanting. Ignorance can be removed only by knowledge, and knowledge of the self cannot come from perception or inference, because the self is the one who perceives and infers. It comes from the words of the Upanishads, unfolded by a teacher who has been taught in the same way. Rambachan sums up the premise: "Ignorance is dispelled only by knowledge and knowledge must be derived from a valid source."

**Knowledge, with experience secondary.** This puts him at odds with the line of this part that runs from Vivekananda to Radhakrishnan, which grounds Vedanta in mystical experience and treats the texts as reports of it. For Dayananda an experience, however exalted, comes and goes; liberation, which he called freedom from self-inadequacy, is the settled understanding that the self is already whole. Meditation and ethical discipline prepare the mind, and the teaching does the liberating. Rambachan's book *The Limits of Scripture* (1994), a critique of Vivekananda's turn from scripture to experience, carries this position into academic debate.

**Teaching as method.** He made the teacher's skill central. Rambachan recalls his "meticulous care in his choice of words" and that each class tried to give "the vision of Vedānta in its entirety." He taught Sanskrit alongside the texts so that students could follow the commentaries, and he insisted on the *sampradaya*, the unbroken chain of teachers, as the way the method is kept.

**Against conversion.** His answer to the question of this part was that every religious tradition deserves to be left intact. He argued that missionary efforts to convert Hindus uproot people from a living culture. At a meeting in New Delhi in 2001 he said that a person converted by proselytisation "is uprooted from his tradition." He went further than earlier Hindu critics of missions and called the attempt to convert another person a form of violence; the phrase became the title of a booklet, *Conversion is Violence*. The historian of religion Chad Bauman, in a 2015 study of these writings, takes the argument seriously as a claim about religious harm and also warns that calling conversion violence can slide into excusing violence against those who seek converts.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="dayananda-arsha" />

He took sannyasa from Chinmayananda in 1962 and so belongs, through Sivananda, to the Saraswati branch of Shankara's Dashanami order. Swami Pranavananda of Gudivada is shown as the decisive intellectual influence. His own students include Swami Paramarthananda in Chennai, Swami Viditatmananda at Saylorsburg, the scholar Anantanand Rambachan and many teachers in the Arsha Vidya network, which continues to run courses on his method.
