## Life

Nirmala Salve was born on 21 March 1923 at Chhindwara, a district town in what is now Madhya Pradesh. Her parents, Prasadrao Keshavrao Salve and Cornelia Karuna Jadhav, are described in the Wikipedia entry on her brother N.K.P. Salve as Marathi Christians. The Wikipedia entry on her says her father was Hindu and her mother Christian, so the sources differ. Prasadrao Salve was a lawyer and a worker in the independence movement. Wikipedia credits him with a Marathi translation of the Quran and says Cornelia was reportedly the first woman in India to take an honours degree in mathematics. Her brother later became a Congress member of parliament, a Union minister and president of the Indian cricket board.

The movement's biographies say she spent school holidays at Mahatma Gandhi's ashram. She took part in the Quit India campaign of 1942 and was jailed for it, according to her brother H.P. Salve's memoirs as cited on Wikipedia. She studied medicine at the Christian Medical College in Ludhiana and at Balakram Medical College in Lahore.

Shortly before independence in 1947 she married Chandrika Prasad Srivastava, an officer of the Indian civil service. He became joint secretary to Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri, and in 1974 he was elected secretary-general of the International Maritime Organization in London, a post he held until 1989. Queen Elizabeth II gave him an honorary knighthood. The couple had two daughters.

The movement dates its beginning to 5 May 1970. By its account, she was sitting through the night on the shore at Nargol, on the Gujarat coast north of Bombay, and at dawn opened the *sahasrara*, the chakra at the crown of the head, for humanity as a whole. A devotional site quotes her, from talks on the opening of the sahasrara: "I saw the Primordial Kundalini rising like a big furnace." From then on she offered what she called self-realisation to small groups in India. When her husband's post took her to London, she began to teach there, and through the 1970s and 1980s the work spread across Europe, reaching North America by the mid-1980s. She later founded a religious body, Vishwa Nirmala Dharma, and an international school at Dharamsala in 1990.

Italy became a second home. According to the list of honours on Wikipedia, the Italian government declared her personality of the year in 1986, and in 2006 she was given honorary citizenship, an honour the list places at Cabella Ligure. By the same list, she was invited to speak at the United Nations on world peace in four successive years. She died at Genoa on 23 February 2011, aged 87 (Hinduism Today, reporting the trust's statement, gave 88). Her followers address her as Shri Mataji, "revered mother." The Sahaja Yoga trust said in 2011 that it was active in about 120 countries; the movement's foundation now says over 140, and its own online directory lists fewer.

## The teaching

*Sahaja* means "born with" or "spontaneous." Shri Mataji taught that every person carries a dormant kundalini, the energy that tantric texts place at the base of the spine, and that it can rise of its own accord through a central channel in a subtle body of channels and chakras. When it reaches the top of the head, the seeker is "realised." The sign is physical. Practitioners describe a cool breeze on the palms or above the head, which she called *chaitanya*, divine vibrations.

The order is the reverse of classical yoga. In most Indian systems the kundalini rises, if at all, after years of discipline. Shri Mataji gave the awakening first, often to a whole hall in one evening, and asked for daily meditation afterwards to keep the channels clear. That meditation aims at *nirvichara*, thoughtless awareness, a state of alertness with no thinking.

She held that real spiritual work cannot be sold, and the introductory programmes are free. In 2004 her website declared her work complete.

**The Adi Shakti.** Shri Mataji spoke of herself as an incarnation, and most of her followers regard her as the Adi Shakti, the primordial feminine power of Hindu Shakta theology, and as the Holy Spirit of Christian teaching. Sahaja Yoga gatherings are organised around *pujas*, ritual worship, in which she was the object of devotion during her life. The scholar Judith Coney, who studied the British movement in the 1990s, reports that Shri Mataji described herself as having come to save the earth from demonic forces. To members this is the ground of the method, since only the Adi Shakti, in their view, could awaken kundalini in thousands at once.

## Questions raised

**Divinity and control.** Critics have called Sahaja Yoga a cult. The French psychiatrist Jean-Marie Abgrall, in *Soul Snatchers* (2000), described it as having a god-like leader and demanding loyalty and money; he noted the movement's official statement that it is not a cult. Coney's 1999 study recorded ex-members' complaints about unwanted arranged marriages, the limited roles of women and fear of leaving, and a 1999 Austrian government publication described the founder as an authority who could not be questioned. In 2001 *The Independent* reported ex-members who said they were told to cut family ties. The movement's British co-ordinator replied that nobody was forced to contribute money.

**Money.** Introductory meetings are free. Ex-members and Abgrall say that membership costs money once a person is committed, through fees for international pujas, shared rents and gifts to the founder, which *The Guardian* reported as large. The official site charges for attendance at international pujas, which it says covers costs.

**Belgium.** In 2005 the Belgian state advisory centre on harmful sectarian organisations, IACSSO, published an advisory that classed Sahaja Yoga as a syncretic cult based on Hindu tradition and warned that its recruitment techniques posed a risk to the public, and to young people in particular. Sahaja Yoga Belgium sued the centre and won preliminary rulings in its favour, reported by the newspaper *De Morgen* in 2008. According to a 2019 legal study of church and state in Belgium, as summarised on Wikipedia, these rulings were overturned on appeal in 2011, and the final judgment found that the movement had been unable to refute the centre's statements. The rulings themselves were not available to check. No Sahaja Yoga court case in Australia was found in the sources consulted for this entry.

## Lineage

Shri Mataji had no guru and named no successor. She sits on the tree as a branch from Shakta tantra, with a documented line from the Christian faith of her family and a looser one, from the movement's own accounts, to Gandhi.

The tantric vocabulary is plain in her teaching and is documented. The Christian edge rests on her family background and on her own identification with the Holy Spirit. The link to Gandhi is testimony from the movement's biographies.
