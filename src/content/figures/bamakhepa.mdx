## Life

He was born Bamacharan Chattopadhyay in Atla, a village in Birbhum district of western Bengal, near Tarapith. One online source gives the date as 22 February 1837, and others give only the year. His family were poor Brahmins. Devotional biographies name his father as Sarvananda, who died while the boy was young, and his mother as Rajkumari. He had an elder sister, who was a widow, and at least one brother, Ramcharan. The village school was all the education he had.

The stories told about his childhood are the stories told about many Bengali saints. He was no use for ordinary work. A job herding cattle for an uncle ended with his being sent home. He sang well, knew the Ramayana and Mahabharata from his mother and sister, and by one account took neighbours' household images at night to worship them by the river. When his absorption in the goddess Tara grew, the family is said to have confined him at home. He got out, crossed the Dwaraka river and walked to Tarapith.

Tarapith is a Tantric temple town built around an image of Tara, a fierce and protective form of the goddess, beside a cremation ground. Tradition counts it among the Shakti Pithas, the sites where parts of the goddess's body are said to have fallen. Bama is reported to have taken instruction from Kailashpati Baba, a Tantric practitioner living there, and then from a second teacher named Mokshadananda. The same biographies say both later installed him as the leading holy man of the place and moved on. No independent source for the two teachers has turned up beyond these devotional accounts.

From then until his death he lived in and around the cremation ground. Accounts describe him as naked or nearly so, smoking hemp, eating alongside dogs, and ignoring temple rules about who may eat before the goddess is fed. The name Bamakhepa comes from this. *Khepa* is Bengali for mad, and Tantrics and Bauls use it as a term of respect for a person beyond convention.

The best-known story concerns food. After temple priests beat him, or stopped his meals, for eating before the offering was made, the queen of Natore, the temple's patron, is said to have dreamed that Tara wanted her son fed first. From then on he was fed in front of the image before anyone else. Devotees also tell of him swimming a flooded river with his dead mother's body to reach the cremation ground. Both stories are reported as legend, and neither has an outside source.

He died at Tarapith in 1911, at about seventy-four. A small shrine to him stands in front of the Tara temple.

<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Bama is short for Bamacharan. Some writers also hear in it <i>vama</i>, the "left-hand" path of Tantra.</aside>

## The teaching

Bamakhepa left no writings and no organised school, and the sayings credited to him are few. What survives is an attitude and a handful of answers. Asked about his learning, he is reported to have said he was unlearned and that the Mother had shown him everything. Asked why he went naked, he is reported to have said that his Father and Mother, Shiva and Tara, were naked, and that he lived with his Mother in the cremation ground.

Behind this is the Shakta view that the goddess is the whole of reality, present equally in what society calls pure and impure. A cremation ground is where that claim is tested, since it holds the things ordinary religion keeps away: corpses, ashes, fear. Devotional accounts say he refused to separate pure from impure and treated outcastes, animals and women as forms of the Mother, though these summaries of his outlook come from later writers and not from his own words.

Visitors came to him for blessings and cures, and the biographies are full of healing stories. They present these as signs of the goddess working through him. The scholar Imma Ramos has written a 2014 paper on Bamakhepa's visualisation of Tara at the temple.

One account of a wider reach concerns Nigamananda Paramahansa (1880–1935), later a well-known teacher in Assam. His movement's story says that after a dream he searched for a mantra's meaning, was led to Bamakhepa and was initiated by him. By that account Bamakhepa then advised him to study Advaita under a Vedantic teacher, and Nigamananda found Satchidananda Saraswati at Pushkar in 1902.

## Lineage

Bamakhepa belongs to the Shakta Tantra of Bengal, and the tree shows that tradition as his root. His devotional biographies name Kailashpati Baba and Mokshadananda as his teachers. Both edges rest on testimony from the movement and nothing else. He is said to have initiated Nigamananda, a claim from Nigamananda's own tradition.

Ramakrishna of Dakshineswar, also a Bengali devotee of the Mother, was born a year before him. One biography notes the coincidence of dates. No source consulted reports a meeting between the two.
