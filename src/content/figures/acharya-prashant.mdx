## Life

Acharya Prashant was born Prashant Tripathi, reportedly on 7 March 1978 in Agra. He studied engineering at the Indian Institute of Technology Delhi and management at the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad. News profiles agree that he passed the civil services examination; they differ on whether he served, with the *Pioneer* reporting that he left "the IRPS Services" and other accounts saying he chose not to take up the career. Penguin's author interview says he worked for a time as a corporate consultant. He has described the move from that career to teaching as "a process of elevation and not of renunciation."

According to the *Pioneer*, he founded the Adwait Life-Education Foundation in 2006 to teach spirituality to engineering students, and spent several years from 2012 holding retreats and reading scripture at Rishikesh and Puri. In 2015 he founded the PrashantAdvait Foundation in Greater Noida. Its reach since then has come mostly through YouTube, where his Hindi channel has tens of millions of subscribers; the foundation reports some 90 million followers across platforms. It runs a structured online programme on the Gita and Upanishads in Hindi and English, with monthly live sessions and examinations, which it says has had over 150,000 participants. He has published books in Hindi and English with Penguin, HarperCollins and Indian publishers, among them *Karma: Why Everything You Know About It Is Wrong* (2021) and *Truth Without Apology* (2025).

He also campaigns on public questions he regards as spiritual ones: animal suffering and veganism (PETA India named him its "Most Influential Vegan" of 2022), climate change, women's equality and superstition. An interview video recorded at IIT Delhi ahead of the Lok Sabha elections, in which he called politicians who avoid climate change "vote merchants" was shared by both the BJP and the Congress on their official accounts. In April 2026 his foundation said he had received a fresh death threat after a video on B. R. Ambedkar and religion, and that such threats tended to follow his statements on women's rights, superstition and terrorism.

## The teaching

Acharya Prashant teaches Advaita Vedanta through the Gita and the Upanishads, read alongside Buddhism, Sufi poetry, Taoism, the Stoics and existentialist philosophy. His starting point is psychological. The ego, the sense of being a separate doer and enjoyer, is in his account a false centre that acts out of fear and greed and suffers for it. Spiritual life is the honest examination of that centre until it loosens. On action he says that acting rightly "simply means to not act from a personal centre of greed and/or fear."

**Against ritual and superstition.** He draws a sharp line between the ritual portions of the Vedas and Vedanta. In a 2025 essay on the Gita he treats ritual as at best an early stage of religion, and reads the Gita's criticism of rites performed for reward (2.42–2.46) as a rejection of religion used as a transaction to secure wealth or comfort while leaving the ego intact. He writes of the Gita as beginning where "rituals end, and honesty begins." He holds that "there is hardly any concept in spirituality which has not been both misinterpreted and misrepresented," and much of his teaching consists of rereading familiar words, such as karma, maya and dharma, against their popular meanings.

**Spirituality as social conscience.** He treats ethical positions as consequences of self-knowledge: a person no longer driven by craving will not consume animals, the planet or other people. His foundation runs programmes on animal welfare, climate and women's empowerment alongside its courses on scripture.

On the question of this part, his answer is close to Radhakrishnan's in one respect and far from it in another. Like Radhakrishnan he finds the same insight in the Upanishads, the Buddha, the Sufis and Western philosophy. Where Radhakrishnan praised Hinduism as tolerant and experimental, Acharya Prashant's writing on the Gita directs its criticism at religion as it is commonly practised, and the common ground he finds across traditions is their critique of the ego.

        <li>The Neo-Vedanta habit of reading many traditions as one insight.</li>
        <li>Vivekananda's linking of Vedanta to reason and social reform.</li>

    </div>

        <li>Vedanta taught as a sustained critique of popular religion to a mass Hindi-speaking audience.</li>
        <li>A structured online curriculum with examinations, run at very large scale.</li>
        <li>Veganism and climate action presented as direct consequences of self-knowledge.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <p class="final">My verdict: the doctrine is classical Advaita read as psychology; what is new is the polemical edge against ritual and the scale and medium through which he teaches it.</p>

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="acharya-prashant" />

He claims no guru and no initiation. The tree therefore connects him only to Advaita Vedanta as a tradition of texts. His reading of it, psychological and suspicious of ritual, sits closer to the Upanishads as read by modern reformers than to any monastic line,.
