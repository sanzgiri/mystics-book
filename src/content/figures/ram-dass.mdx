## Life

Richard Alpert was born in Boston on 6 April 1931, the son of a lawyer, George Alpert. He took a psychology degree at Tufts in 1952, a master's at Wesleyan in 1954 and a doctorate at Stanford in 1957, and became an assistant clinical professor at Harvard in 1958. In March 1961 he took psilocybin for the first time, and with Timothy Leary he ran the Harvard Psilocybin Project. He was dismissed from Harvard in 1963 (Wikipedia and a wire obituary carried by KMPH agree on the year).

In 1967 he travelled to India. The wire obituary says that Allen Ginsberg's advice sent him there, and Wikipedia says that the young American seeker Bhagavan Das introduced him to the guru Neem Karoli Baba, who gave him the name Ram Dass, "servant of Ram", often rendered "servant of God" for Western readers. Wikipedia says he studied with the guru over the following four years. Neem Karoli Baba died on 11 September 1973, and the chapter on him describes how little he left in writing. Ram Dass carried his name outside India.

The book that did so was *Be Here Now*. It first appeared in 1970 as *From Bindu to Ojas*, a pamphlet distributed by the Lama Foundation in New Mexico, and as a book in 1971. Wikipedia lists four sections. "Journey" is a short autobiography. "From Bindu to Ojas" is the largest section, a free-form set of reflections with illustrations by Lama residents. "Cookbook for a Sacred Life" gives practical techniques of yoga, breath control and meditation along with quotations from many traditions, and "Painted Cakes (Do Not Satisfy Hunger)" holds a Zen commentary and reading lists. Wikipedia reports that it has been in print since 1971 and has sold more than two million copies. The title phrase came from a remark of Bhagavan Das. Baba Hari Dass, Ram Dass's sadhana tutor in these years, is part of the book's story, and Wikipedia notes that Neem Karoli Baba asked for changes to the passages about him.

In 1974 Ram Dass founded the Hanuman Foundation, which ran a prison project and a dying project. In 1978 he co-founded the Seva Foundation with Larry Brilliant and Wavy Gravy, which works to treat preventable blindness in India and Nepal. A stroke in February 1997 left him with expressive aphasia, and he interpreted it as grace. He published *Still Here* in 2000, nearly died of a second stroke in India in 2004, and then moved to Maui, where he died on 22 December 2019, aged 88.

## Why this figure appears here

This part asks whether surrender is the path. Through Ram Dass the answer reached people who had never heard the question. The chapter on his teacher says that Neem Karoli Baba wrote nothing and taught by feeding people. The edge between them is documented by Ram Dass's own books.

He inherited the Hanuman devotion of his guru and the practice of service, and InsightLA singles out his karma yoga, or spiritual service, as the practice through which he reached many others. The new element was the audience and the register. A man trained as a psychologist wrote in the voice of the seeker he had been, with drawings and quotations drawn across traditions, and his book was read as a manual by readers who did not share a Hindu background. Wikipedia calls it one of the first guides for non-Hindus seeking to become yogis. From 1968, according to InsightLA, he also explored Buddhist meditation in the Theravada, Tibetan and Zen schools and Sufi and Jewish mystical study, and presented them next to bhakti, a wider range than the sources record for his guru.

The wire obituary notes his book *Compassion in Action* and quotes him on learning how to get old for the ageing baby boomers, and the stroke became part of the teaching. His practical advice, as given in these sources, is presence and service, which means the command in the title and work among prisoners, the dying and the blind. The sources read do not preserve a fixed method for his own practice.

On the tree he stands as a documented disciple of Neem Karoli Baba, with Bhagavan Das as the introducer and Hari Dass Baba as tutor, and with the Buddhist tradition as a later influence. He also links the Hanuman current in Part 7 to the Western reception of yoga.
