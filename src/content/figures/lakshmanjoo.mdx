<p>1990. Swami Lakshmanjoo, eighty-three, is sitting in front of a video camera, working verse by verse through Abhinavagupta's eleventh-century commentary on the <i>Bhagavad Gita</i>. His American students John and Denise Hughes are in the room, with a third student named Jonathan. Someone asks how people will be initiated into this tradition once he is gone.</p>
<p>He points at the camera. "Through viewing this!" he says, according to the transcript the Lakshmanjoo Academy later published. When the students ask about people who only read the books, he says they "will be initiated by themselves." Then he adds an instruction. The recordings, he tells them, must be transcribed and published.</p>
<p>The exchange is short, and it sums up what made him unusual. He belonged to a teaching tradition that had survived for centuries on palm-leaf and birch-bark manuscripts and in the memory of a few Kashmiri families. He spent the last twenty years of his life making sure it would survive on magnetic tape.</p>

## Life

Lakshman Raina was born in Srinagar on 9 May 1907 into a Kashmiri Pandit family, the Brahmin community of the valley. His father, Narayandas Raina, is remembered in Srinagar as a pioneer of the houseboat trade on Dal Lake. His mother was Aranyamali. Sources disagree about the size of the family. One says he was the fifth of nine children, and a 1991 magazine profile says one of eighteen.

The family's guru was Swami Ram, a Shaiva teacher of Srinagar who, by the tradition's own account, did much to revive the study of the old Kashmiri Shaiva texts in the late nineteenth century. His dates are given as 1852–1914 by the Lakshmanjoo Academy and 1854–1915 elsewhere. It was Swami Ram, according to a biography by the Kashmiri writer G.N. Raina, who gave the boy the name Lakshman and taught him the Gayatri mantra and simple yogic exercises. Before he died he placed the child in the care of his chief disciple, Swami Mahatab Kak (1870–1942), who became Lakshman's teacher in the Shaiva scriptures.

The movement's biography says that from early childhood he would slip into absorbed states that he later described with a Kashmiri phrase meaning "greater than the greatest." He was formally initiated at seventeen. At nineteen, or about twenty in another account, he had what he called his first glimpse of God-consciousness, and he took a vow of lifelong celibacy. G.N. Raina places the experience at four in the morning, the hour Hindus call *brahma muhurta*. Soon after, he left home without telling his family and went to an ashram outside Srinagar. One source names it as Harvan, a village just beyond the Shalimar garden, and another as Handwara, much further north.

His father brought him back, and the family settled into a compromise. Lakshman would not run the houseboats, but he would stay near home. From 1926 to 1933 he studied Sanskrit grammar and Shaiva philosophy with Pandit Maheshwar Nath Razdan, a scholar of the old school. His first book, in 1933, was a Sanskrit and Hindi commentary on Abhinavagupta's interpretation of the *Bhagavad Gita*, the *Gitartha Samgraha*. In 1934 his parents built him a house on the hillside at Ishber, above the spring of Gupta Ganga, between the Nishat and Shalimar gardens, looking down on Dal Lake. Sources differ on when this house became Ishwar Ashram in the formal sense. One gives 1934, and another says he founded the ashram in 1962.

He stayed there for most of the next half-century, reading, meditating and teaching anyone who climbed up to ask. He wore white, kept his vow of celibacy and never took formal monastic orders, so he was a *brahmachari* more than a swami in the strict sense, though everyone called him Swami. A small circle of Kashmiri devotees, many of them women from Pandit families, formed around him.

Scholars found him first. The French Indologist Lilian Silburn came in 1948 and returned for about ten years, and her French translations of Kashmiri Shaiva texts drew on his readings. The Indian scholar Jaideva Singh studied with him and went on to publish English translations of the *Shiva Sutras*, the *Spanda Karikas* and the *Vijnana Bhairava*. The American writer Paul Reps studied the *Vijnana Bhairava* with him in 1957 and published a free rendering of its meditation techniques, under the title "Centering," in his popular anthology *Zen Flesh, Zen Bones*. The Italian Raniero Gnoli, the Oxford scholar Alexis Sanderson, who spent six years in Kashmir studying with him, and the British-born Mark Dyczkowski were regular visitors. Swami Muktananda, the subject of the next chapter, spent several summers near him.

From about 1965, by the Academy's account, a steady stream of visitors from India and abroad came up the hill. The most important for his legacy were John and Denise Hughes, Americans who arrived in 1971. John Hughes had come to Kashmir to write a doctoral thesis. He stayed fifteen years and began recording Swamiji's English translations and commentaries of the core texts, first on audio and later on video. The violence that spread through the valley in the late 1980s ended this life. The Hugheses left in 1986, and the Kashmiri Pandit community, including most of his devotees, fled the valley in 1989 and 1990.

In 1991 Lakshmanjoo travelled to the United States and stayed with the Hugheses in Los Angeles, where his birthday was celebrated. That year he set up the Universal Shaiva Fellowship there to publish his teachings. A visiting correspondent for *Hinduism Today* reported that he expected to live fifteen more years and to leave his body in 2006. He died on 27 September 1991 in Noida, near Delhi, aged eighty-four. His devotees in India formed the Ishwar Ashram Trust, which still holds an annual fire ceremony for him at Ishber.

## The teaching

Kashmir Shaivism is the name modern writers give to a family of non-dual Shaiva schools that flourished in the Kashmir valley between about the ninth and twelfth centuries. Its greatest figure was Abhinavagupta, who lived around 1000 CE and wrote the *Tantraloka*, an encyclopedia of Shaiva practice and thought. Lakshmanjoo taught this system from inside, as a practitioner, and he taught it whole: the philosophy, the yoga and the devotion together.

### Consciousness and its play

The starting point is the first of the *Shiva Sutras*, a short text that tradition says was revealed to the sage Vasugupta in the ninth century. It reads *chaitanyam atma*: "consciousness is the Self." For Kashmir Shaivism this consciousness is Shiva, and it is active. It knows, it wills and it creates. The world is its self-expression, real as an expression of Shiva and never a mistake or an illusion to be seen through. "This whole universe is the expansion of your own nature," Lakshmanjoo told his students.

This is where the tradition parts company with Shankara's Advaita, which treats the world as an appearance on the screen of a changeless Brahman. In Kashmir Shaivism the creative power of consciousness, which it calls *shakti* or *spanda* (the vibration or pulse of awareness), belongs to the Absolute itself. Shiva without Shakti, Lakshmanjoo liked to say, would be inert. The universe is laid out in thirty-six levels, or *tattvas*, running from Shiva and Shakti at the top down through the limited individual to the elements of earth, and the student learns to trace each level back to the consciousness that projects it.

### Recognition

The school of Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta is called *Pratyabhijna*, "recognition." Liberation, in this view, is recognising something you already are. The old illustration is a woman who has fallen in love with a man by reputation and then meets him without knowing who he is. He means nothing to her until someone tells her his name. Nothing about him changes when she recognises him, and everything about her experience does. In the same way, Lakshmanjoo taught, the individual is already Shiva, and liberation is the moment of recognition.

### Grace

The question this part of the book asks is whether awakening can be transmitted. Lakshmanjoo's answer comes from the doctrine of Shiva's five acts. Shiva creates, sustains and dissolves the world. He also conceals his own nature, which is how limited beings come about, and he reveals it again, which is grace, *anugraha*. In Kashmir Shaivism grace is also called *shaktipata*, the "descent of power," and the texts describe it in nine grades from the faintest stirring of interest to an awakening so complete that, in Lakshmanjoo's reading, the body cannot hold it and falls away.

Grace in this system comes from Shiva, and its distribution owes nothing to merit. "There is no question of deserving," he said in the talks published as *The Secret Supreme*. "Grace can shine on anybody." A teacher can be its channel, and he spoke of realised masters who go on working "secretly in deserved hearts of disciples." He did not, however, present himself as someone who handed out awakenings. Students who came to him expecting a jolt of energy found a teacher who expected them to learn Sanskrit, meditate daily and read the commentaries line by line.

### The four means

The tradition grades its paths by how much effort they involve, and Lakshmanjoo taught all four. The lowest, *anavopaya*, the individual means, works with the breath, the body, mantra and concentration. The middle, *shaktopaya*, the means of energy, works with thought itself, refining a single thought until it becomes awareness. The higher, *shambhavopaya*, the means of Shiva, needs only a moment of pure awareness before thought begins. Above them is *anupaya*, "no means," for those in whom a single word from the teacher is enough.

He did not rank people by these paths so much as he treated them as entry points. The *Vijnana Bhairava*, his favourite practical text, gives 112 brief techniques, many of them a sentence long: attend to the gap between two breaths, to the beginning of a sneeze, to the moment of falling asleep. Each, he said, is a door into the same awareness.

<aside class="note"><strong>Trika.</strong> "Threefold." The name of Abhinavagupta's school, after its three goddesses and its triad of Shiva, Shakti and the individual. Lakshmanjoo used the word almost interchangeably with Kashmir Shaivism.</aside>

I came to Lakshmanjoo through the scholars who thank him in their prefaces, and I expected a learned pandit. Listening to the recordings changed that. He laughs a great deal, interrupts himself to tell stories about his gurus, and every so often says something so plain about awareness that the commentary he is explaining seems to fall away. Most of this book's figures had to build a teaching. He had one handed to him and spent his life passing it on intact, which is a rarer skill than it sounds.

## Lineage

Lakshmanjoo's line, as his own organisation presents it, runs back through Mahatab Kak and Swami Ram to Swami Manakak in the mid-nineteenth century and, beyond him, to Abhinavagupta's teachers Lakshmanagupta and Somananda. The Academy calls him "the last in an unbroken chain of Masters." The nineteenth-century links are documented. The connection between them and the medieval masters rests on the tradition's own account.

His students divide into two groups. The scholars, among them **Lilian Silburn**, **Jaideva Singh**, **Alexis Sanderson** and **Mark Dyczkowski**, carried his readings into the academic study of Kashmir Shaivism. The practitioners, led by **John and Denise Hughes**, have kept his recorded teaching in print and online through the Universal Shaiva Fellowship and its Lakshmanjoo Academy. His influence on **Muktananda**, who made Kashmir Shaivism the philosophical frame of Siddha Yoga, is shown as a lighter branch.

## The practice

He taught many methods. This sequence follows the outline he gave in his recorded talks on the *Vijnana Bhairava* and *The Secret Supreme*.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Study.</strong> Learn the map first: the thirty-six levels of reality and Shiva's five acts. He held that understanding was itself a means of liberation.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Work with the breath.</strong> Watch the points where the in-breath ends and the out-breath begins, and rest attention in that pause.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Use a mantra or a single thought.</strong> Repeat it until it thins out into the awareness that is thinking it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Catch the first instant.</strong> Notice the moment before a perception or a thought takes shape, where the texts locate the pulse of consciousness.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Carry it into ordinary life.</strong> The goal of the tradition is to find the same awareness in waking activity, which he called the state of Shiva in the world.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

Unusually for a teacher of his generation, Lakshmanjoo can be heard at length in English. John Hughes recorded his translations and commentaries from the 1970s onward, and the Universal Shaiva Fellowship says it has published eight books and thirty-five hours of video from these sessions. Most of the books in print under his name are edited transcripts of the tapes.

## Critiques and questions

**The unbroken chain.** The claim that he was the last link in a line reaching back to Abhinavagupta comes from his followers. The nineteenth-century teachers are documented, and the medieval texts are well known, but the centuries between them are thin on evidence. Alexis Sanderson, who knew him well, has written a study of his place in the Kashmiri Shaiva tradition, and readers who want the scholarly picture should start there.

**Two organisations.** His teaching is represented by the Ishwar Ashram Trust in India and by the Universal Shaiva Fellowship and Lakshmanjoo Academy in the United States. Both are devoted to him. The recordings and their transcripts are copyrighted by John Hughes, which has kept the archive coherent and has also meant that most of his spoken teaching reaches the public through one family's editorial choices.

**Forecasts.** In 1991 he told *Hinduism Today* that he would live until 2006 and that the coming fifteen years would bring peace and prosperity to Hinduism. He died that September. Within the tradition such statements are read in several ways, and I report the forecast without interpretation.

**Endorsements withheld.** He was cautious about others who claimed kundalini experiences. The same 1991 report says that Gopi Krishna, whose account of a spontaneous kundalini awakening was widely read in the West, sought his confirmation and did not get it. He also claimed the scholar Gopinath Kaviraj as a secret disciple, which Kaviraj's own circle does not appear to have said.

**Transmission by video.** His statement that people could be initiated by watching the recordings is taken literally by some followers today. Traditional Shaiva practice requires initiation, *diksha*, from a living teacher, and the question of whether a recording can serve is open even among his admirers.

## Descendants

Lakshmanjoo named no institutional successor in the usual sense. His legacy lives in three places. The scholarly field of Kashmir Shaiva studies, in Europe, India and North America, owes much to the readings he gave Silburn, Singh, Sanderson and Dyczkowski. The Universal Shaiva Fellowship and Lakshmanjoo Academy run study groups, online courses and a weekly online gathering built around the recordings. And the wider Western interest in "non-dual Tantra," which runs through many modern yoga schools, draws heavily on texts he helped bring into English, the *Vijnana Bhairava* above all.
