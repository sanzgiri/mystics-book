## Life

Nani Bala Barua was born in 1911 in a village on the Chittagong plains. Wikipedia gives 25 March. She belonged to the Barua, a Buddhist community of the region whose households kept Buddhist rituals and customs, although meditation had largely dropped out of lay life and was left to monks and nuns. At twelve she married Rajani Ranjan Barua, an engineer about twice her age, who left for work in Burma a week after the wedding. After two years in her in-laws' home she joined him in Rangoon. Encyclopedia.com reports that he told her to put off meditation until she was older.

She lost her mother early in the marriage and later lost two infants, and the couple raised her much younger brother, Bijoy. Her daughter, Dipa, was born after some twenty-seven years of marriage, according to Encyclopedia.com (Wikipedia gives 1950), and the name Dipa Ma means "mother of Dipa."

Her husband died suddenly, in 1957 according to Wikipedia, when she was in her mid-forties. A Lion's Roar profile by Amy Schmidt and Sara Jenkins says she was left grief-stricken and, for several years, bedridden with heart disease and high blood pressure. She dreamed that the Buddha recited verses from the *Dhammapada*, among them the line "Clinging to what is dear brings sorrow," and resolved to learn meditation. On her first day at the Kamayut Meditation Center in Rangoon a dog bit her leg and she felt no pain, so deep was her concentration. She had rabies treatment, and her daughter would not let her go back. She went on practising at home for several years.

Around the age of fifty-three, the Lion's Roar profile says, Munindra, a family friend, urged her to come to the center where he was studying with the Burmese monk Mahasi Sayadaw. Her sister Hema joined her, and the two kept strict retreat rules for almost a year: silence, no eye contact and no food after noon. Wikipedia says she went on to a retreat at Thathana Yeiktha, Mahasi's centre, and reports a first stage of awakening there. That is the report of a source with several unsourced passages.

Burma ordered foreign nationals out in 1967. Monks offered to help her stay, according to the dipama.com biography, but she chose Calcutta for her daughter's schooling and prospects. They lived in a small room above a metal-grinding shop, with no running water, a charcoal stove and a shared toilet. She taught her neighbour Malati Barua, a widow with six children, and Sudipti, who ran a business and cared for relatives, and then others from the same room. By her death she had several hundred students in Calcutta and a following in the West. The Insight Meditation Society (IMS) in Barre, Massachusetts, says she taught there in 1980 and again in 1984. Her American students included Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg and Jack Kornfield, who had founded IMS in 1975, and also Sylvia Boorstein and Michele McDonald-Smith. She died in Calcutta in September 1989; Wikipedia gives 1 September. Encyclopedia.com repeats the account of a neighbour who was with her, that she folded her hands and bowed to a statue of the Buddha.

## The teaching

Dipa Ma taught that family life and practice are one. The Lion's Roar profile says she applied mindfulness to work, cooking, parenting and conversation, and taught a nursing mother to be mindful while feeding her child. Students called her the patron saint of householders. Meditation teachers in her tradition were mostly male monks, and she taught lay women in their own homes.

Her words survive through students. A student, Sharon Kreider, recalled her saying, "Being a wife, being a mother—these were my first teachers," according to Testimonies of Awakening; dipama.com gives the same words. Other sayings gathered on the same site include "You cannot separate meditation from life" and "If you are busy, then busyness is the meditation." To a student with little time she said, "If you can manage five minutes a day, then do that." She also said, "If your life is in trouble, do metta," using the Pali word for loving-kindness, and "Meditation is love." A student, Michele Levey, quotes her as saying that her own mind had three qualities, concentration, equanimity and loving-kindness.

Behind the easy sayings lay a severe discipline. The Lion's Roar profile records the five precepts, about four hours of sleep and several hours of daily sitting for serious students, and Goldstein recalled being told to sit for two days straight. She blessed students each morning, often by blowing on them and stroking their hair. When her daughter urged her to rest from the visitors who came to her door, she said, "They are hungry for the dhamma, so let them come." The phrase appears in the IMS, Lion's Roar and dipama.com accounts.

Wikipedia reports that she studied supernormal powers with Munindra in 1963 and claimed to have them, and adds that the claim was never independently verified. It is her claim as reported there, and the sources read for this entry do not describe it further.

Her own path fits the emphasis. She came to meditation through grief and illness, practised at home for several years after her first retreat, and went to Mahasi's centre only when she was past fifty. She asked students for the same attention at whatever scale their lives allowed. The framework she worked within was the four stages of awakening described in the Visuddhimagga, a text that testimoniesawakening.com cites for her progress.

## Lineage

She received her training in the Burmese Mahasi tradition through Munindra and through Mahasi Sayadaw's centre in Rangoon. Munindra, born in Chittagong in 1915 or 1914 (Wikipedia gives both) and died in 2003, had himself been a close disciple of Mahasi from 1957 to 1966.

The Munindra link is documented by the biographies read for this entry. The Mahasi link is documented for the retreat at his centre and is less specific about what he taught her. Her teaching of Goldstein and Salzberg, and of Kornfield as one of his teachers, is stated in the IMS and Lion's Roar accounts. Through them her sayings entered the retreat talks and books of the American insight movement, where "if you are busy, then busyness is the meditation" is still quoted.

## The practice

Dipa Ma left no manual, and the practice below is assembled from the sayings and student accounts cited here. It is the Mahasi lineage's ordinary insight practice, scaled to a household.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Know what you are doing.</strong> She told students, "Know your intention in every action" (dipama.com). A rushed journey to the office becomes a subject of mindfulness: "If you are rushing to the office, then you should be mindful of rushing."</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Sit for the time you have.</strong> Five minutes a day counts. She added, "Practice now. Don't think you will do more later."</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Settle on the breath.</strong> Levey, who learned loving-kindness practice from her, describes resting attention on the breath rising and falling in the chest and noticing warmth, tingling or movement near the heart (Snow Lion article).</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Offer loving-kindness.</strong> In the same account the phrases begin with oneself ("May I be free from enmity"), pass to a loved teacher or friend ("May you...") and then to all beings.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Bring it into the day.</strong> She told a student with only five minutes a day, "The only way is to bring mindfulness to whatever you are suffering" (Lion's Roar).</div></li>
</ol>
