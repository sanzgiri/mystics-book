## Life

Nani Bala Barua was born in 1911 in a village on the Chittagong plains. Wikipedia gives 25 March. She belonged to the Barua, a Buddhist community of the region. At twelve she married Rajani Ranjan Barua, an engineer about twice her age, and after two years in her in-laws' home she joined him in Rangoon. She lost two infants, and the couple raised her younger brother, Bijoy. Her daughter, Dipa, was born later in her life, and the name Dipa Ma means "mother of Dipa."

Her husband died suddenly in 1957, when she was in her mid-forties. A Lion's Roar profile by Amy Schmidt and Sara Jenkins says she was left with heart disease and high blood pressure, and that a dream of the Buddha reciting verses on grief led her to learn meditation. She sat her first retreat at the Kamayut Meditation Center in Rangoon. Around the age of fifty-three, the same profile says, Munindra, a family friend, urged her to come to the center where he was studying with the Burmese monk Mahasi Sayadaw. Wikipedia says she went on to a retreat at Thathana Yeiktha, Mahasi's centre, and reports a first stage of awakening there. That is the report of a source with several unsourced passages.

Burma ordered foreign nationals out in 1967. Monks offered to help her stay, according to the dipama.com biography, but she chose Calcutta for her daughter's schooling and prospects. In Calcutta she taught her neighbour Malati Barua, a widow with six children, and then others from her flat. The Insight Meditation Society (IMS) in Barre, Massachusetts, says she taught there in 1980 and again in 1984. Her American students included Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg and Jack Kornfield, the founders of IMS, and also Sylvia Boorstein and Michele McDonald-Smith. She died in Calcutta on 1 September 1989, according to Wikipedia.

## The teaching

Dipa Ma taught that family life and practice are one. The Lion's Roar profile says she applied mindfulness to work, cooking, parenting and conversation, and that she asked busy householders for as little as five minutes a day. It also records periods of strict discipline: the five precepts, about four hours of sleep and several hours of daily sitting. She blessed students, often by blowing on them and stroking their hair.

Her words survive through students. Sharon Kreider recalled her saying, "Being a wife, being a mother—these were my first teachers," according to the dipama.com biography. To the many visitors who came to her door she said, "They are hungry for the dhamma, so let them come." The phrase appears in the IMS, Lion's Roar and dipama.com accounts.

Wikipedia reports that she studied supernormal powers with Munindra in 1963 and claimed to have them, and adds that the claim was never independently verified. It is her claim as reported there, and the sources read for this entry do not describe it further.

## Lineage

She received her training in the Burmese Mahasi tradition through Munindra and through Mahasi Sayadaw's centre in Rangoon. Munindra, born 1915 in Chittagong according to Wikipedia and died in 2003, had himself been a close disciple of Mahasi from 1957 to 1966.
