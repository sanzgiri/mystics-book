## Life

Saradamani was born on 22 December 1853 in Jayrambati, a village in western Bengal a few miles from Kamarpukur, the home of Ramakrishna's family. Her parents, Ramachandra Mukhopadhyay and Shyamasundari Devi, were Brahmins of modest means. She had no formal schooling, and the biographies describe a childhood spent on household and farm work.

In 1859, when she was about five, she was married to Gadadhar Chattopadhyay, the young priest of the Kali temple at Dakshineswar who would become known as Ramakrishna. The official account of the women's order she inspired calls it more a betrothal than a marriage. She stayed with her parents. Reports reached Jayrambati that her husband had gone mad, and in 1872, at about eighteen, she walked to Dakshineswar to see for herself and, in her own later telling, to serve him.

She found him sane and kind, and she stayed. They lived as husband and wife without a sexual relationship, a fact that both of them and all the early sources affirm. In 1872, on the night of the Phalaharini Kali Puja, Ramakrishna worshipped her in his room as the goddess Shodashi, a form of the Divine Mother, offering at her feet the fruits of his practice. Belur Math still marks the occasion every year. He taught her mantras and, according to the movement's biographies, how to initiate others, and the Ramakrishna Order counts her as his first disciple.

For the next thirteen years she lived at Dakshineswar for long stretches in a tiny room in the music tower, the *nahabat*, cooking for her husband and for the young men who came to him, with spells at home in Jayrambati. When he was dying of throat cancer at Cossipore in 1886 she nursed him.

After his death in August 1886 she went on pilgrimage to Benares, Ayodhya and Vrindavan. Devotees say that at Vrindavan she passed into deep samadhi and that Ramakrishna appeared to her and told her to take up the work of guiding disciples. She then lived alone at Kamarpukur for about a year in great poverty, as a Brahmin widow without means, until her husband's monastic disciples brought her to Calcutta in 1888. From then on she divided her time between Calcutta and Jayrambati. In Calcutta she lived in a series of rented houses until 23 May 1909, when she moved into a house in Baghbazar that Swami Saradananda had built for her. It also housed the order's Bengali journal *Udbodhan*, and locals still call it Mayer Bari, "Mother's house."

She initiated a large number of disciples, monks and householders, men and women, and the young monks of the order treated her as their guru as much as Ramakrishna's widow. Sister Nivedita, the Irishwoman Margaret Noble, was received by her in 1898 and became close to her. She died in Calcutta after a long illness, recurring malaria in the Math's account and kala-azar (black fever) in others, in July 1920. The date is given as 20 or 21 July in different sources, since she died in the night. She was cremated at Belur Math.

## The teaching

She gave no lectures and wrote nothing. What survives are conversations that her disciples wrote down in Bengali, later collected as *Sri Sri Mayer Katha*, "the words of the Holy Mother," and translated by Swami Nikhilananda as *The Gospel of the Holy Mother*. They are short, practical and mostly about how to live.

**Steady practice.** She told disciples to repeat their mantra every day, in large numbers. The Sri Sarada Math's biography quotes her advice to repeat it "at least 10,000 to 15,000 times a day," and says she herself rose at about two in the morning to do japa on behalf of disciples who were too lax to do their own. She treated practice as ordinary daily work, to be done whether or not one felt inspired.

**Motherhood as the ground of acceptance.** Her central idea, lived more than argued, was that she was mother to anyone who came. She is reported to have said that she could not refuse anyone who called her mother. The most cited story concerns Amjad, a Muslim labourer from a neighbouring village with a reputation as a thief, whom she fed in her house in Jayrambati and served herself, against the objections of her relatives. The Math records her saying, "Amjad is my son, exactly in the sense that Sarat (Swami Saradananda) is."

**Look at your own faults.** Her last recorded instruction, given to a woman devotee a few days before she died, is the one most often repeated in the movement: "if you want peace of mind, do not find fault with others. Rather, see your own faults." It continues: "No one is a stranger, my child: this whole world is your own!"

**Work as worship.** She cooked, swept and washed clothes until late in life, and she held that household work done for others was a form of spiritual practice. In this she gave the movement's ideal of service a domestic and female face.

Vivekananda wrote that "Mother has been born to revive that wonderful Shakti in India," and the order treats her as the incarnation of the Divine Mother and the partner of Ramakrishna's mission. In 1954, a century after her birth, the order began the Sri Sarada Math, a separate monastic order for women, which it describes as fulfilling Vivekananda's wish.

Scholars have begun to ask how much of this image belongs to her and how much to her admirers. Narasingha Sil's *Divine Dowager* (2003) sets out to show her as a real person, hardworking and unpretentious, shaped by her husband's status as a god-man, without trying to debunk the hagiography. A 2010 University of Manitoba thesis by Trishia Goulet argues that both devotees and scholars wrote her up mainly as Ramakrishna's support, and that her idealisation as the mother of India by the Bengali middle class hides her own life as a renouncer and a guru.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="sarada-devi" />

On the tree she hangs from Ramakrishna, as his wife and, in the order's account, his first disciple. The relationship is documented; his worship of her as Shodashi is known from his own words and the early biographies. Below her stand the people she guided, among them Swami Saradananda, who served as her guardian for decades and built the Udbodhan house, and Sister Nivedita. The Sri Sarada Math, founded 34 years after her death, is the women's monastic order of the Ramakrishna tradition, and it takes her as its ideal.
