## Life

Margaret Noble was born on 28 October 1867 in Dungannon, County Tyrone, the eldest of six children of Samuel Noble, a linen draper who became a Methodist minister, and Mary Hamilton. After her father died in 1877 she was educated at a Congregationalist school in Halifax. She began teaching in 1884 and taught in Keswick, Rugby, Wrexham and Chester before opening her own school in London in 1892, where she followed the child-centred methods of Pestalozzi and Froebel. She was a member of the Sesame Club in London. Wikipedia says that after years of religious doubt she turned to natural science and then to Buddhism. She also worked as secretary to the planner and biologist Patrick Geddes, according to the Encyclopedia.com entry.

She heard Vivekananda lecture in London in November 1895. The Sri Sarada Math site quotes her saying his teaching was like living water to men perishing of thirst. She reached Calcutta in January 1898 and met Sarada Devi on 17 March. On 25 March Vivekananda initiated her as a brahmacharini and gave her the name Nivedita, meaning the dedicated one. Her school opened at Bosepara Lane in Bagbazar, north Calcutta, on 13 November 1898, and Sarada Devi blessed it. Orthodox families hesitated to send daughters to a school run by a foreign woman, and money was scarce. The Sri Sarada Math says the school closed after a few months for lack of funds and reopened for good in February 1902. Sister Christine started a section for women there in November 1903, which may explain why Encyclopedia.com dates the school to 1903.

In March 1899 she organised volunteers to clean streets and nurse patients during Calcutta's plague outbreak for about a month. She toured Britain and America in 1899 to raise funds. After Vivekananda's death in 1902 she carried on his mission in her own way, lecturing across India from 1902 to 1904. Her books included *Kali the Mother* (1900), *The Web of Indian Life* (1904) and *The Master as I Saw Him* (1910). She helped the scientist Jagadish Chandra Bose with his research and backed artists of what became the Bengal school, among them Abanindranath Tagore and Nandalal Bose, and the critics Ananda Coomaraswamy and E. B. Havell. The Sarada Math site says she financed the artists' study of the caves at Ajanta and Ellora. She left her property and copyrights to Belur Math for the education of Indian women and died in Darjeeling in October 1911, aged forty-three. Her memorial there, in the wording that sources give in slightly different forms, says that she gave her all to India.

## Why this figure appears here

Part 2 asks whether all religion is one. Vivekananda answered with a doctrine, Vedanta as the common ground, and with the Ramakrishna Mission as a body that would put it to work. Nivedita is the test of whether the doctrine could be lived by someone from outside it. She was a Protestant-raised Irishwoman who took Hindu vows, took the Mother as her text in *Kali the Mother*, and made Calcutta her home from 1898 until her death.

What she inherited came in two parts. From Vivekananda she took the idea that service is worship and that education for women was a precondition of any national renewal. From her European training she brought the kindergarten method of Froebel and Pestalozzi. What looks new is the use she made of both. She treated Indian art and history as a subject to be defended in print against colonial assumptions, and she argued that a school should start from the pupil's own culture. Wikipedia reports that she supported Abanindranath Tagore and Nandalal Bose in just this way, and Encyclopedia.com credits her with inspiring Havell's defence of Indian art. The Sarada Math site lists Tagore, Gokhale and Aurobindo among those her writings and speeches moved.

On the tree two edges lead to her, from Vivekananda by initiation and from Sarada Devi, who opened the school. Both are documented in the biographical sources read. The line to Aurobindo is a friendship: by the Aurobindo ashram's account they met at Baroda in 1902, and in 1910 she edited his weekly *Karmayogin* at his request. Wikipedia counts Tagore among her friends. The claim that she inspired Tagore and Bose rests on later accounts, and no initiation lies behind any of these lines.

She is a case of devotion carried into institutions. The Ramakrishna lineage passed in her hands from monastic life into a schoolroom and a studio, and the main thing she added was the claim that a nation's art and its girls' education belong to the same project.
