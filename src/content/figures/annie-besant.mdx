## Life

Annie Wood was born in London in 1847, married the clergyman Frank Besant in 1867 and separated from him in 1873. She then made her name as a freethought lecturer and writer for the *National Reformer*. By the late 1880s her interests had moved to socialism, and she joined the Fabian Society in 1885, took part in the matchgirls' strike of 1888 and sat on the London School Board. In 1889 she was asked to review Blavatsky's *The Secret Doctrine*, asked to meet its author, called on her at Lansdowne Road in London and joined the Theosophical Society on 21 May 1889. Blavatsky made her a favourite pupil and co-editor of *Lucifer*.

She reached India for the first time in 1893 and went on to Chicago to represent the Society at the World's Parliament of Religions. In India she immersed herself in Hindu texts with the Theosophist Bhagavan Das. Together they produced a translation of the *Bhagavad Gita* in 1895. In July 1898 she founded the Central Hindu College in Benares, whose aim, in Theosophy Wiki's summary, was sound secular education combined with moral and religious instruction on Hindu lines. George Arundale became its headmaster in 1908. A girls' college followed in 1904, and her *Sanatana Dharma*, a textbook of Hindu religion and ethics, appeared in Benares in 1903. In 1911 she agreed with Madan Mohan Malaviya to work toward a university in Benares, and the college became a constituent of the new Banaras Hindu University in October 1917.

After Colonel Olcott died she was elected president of the Theosophical Society in 1907, an office she held until she died. Wikipedia places the discovery of Krishnamurti in 1909, when C. W. Leadbeater proclaimed him the best vehicle for the World Teacher. She became his guardian in March 1910 and in 1911 created the Order of the Star in the East around him. When Krishnamurti dissolved the Order in 1929 she continued to say that she believed in him. From 1913 she entered Indian politics. She started the papers *The Commonwealth* and *New India* in 1914, founded the All India Home Rule League in 1916, the year Bal Gangadhar Tilak started a league of his own, and in December 1917 became the first woman president of the Indian National Congress. She died at Adyar on 20 September 1933.

## Why this figure appears here

Part 12 asks whether the guru is the obstacle. Besant held the opposite view in its strongest form: that a living teacher could be identified in advance, trained and presented to the world. She and Leadbeater built an institution around that expectation, the Order of the Star in the East, and the boy at its centre dissolved it in 1929. Her own path shows the same habit of commitment. Christianity gave way to freethought, freethought to socialism, socialism to Theosophy, and each stage brought public work.

What she inherited was Blavatsky's Theosophy: the Masters of the Ancient Wisdom, the unity of religions and the Society's commitment to the comparative study of religion. What she added in India is the Hindu strand. Her Gita translation, her textbooks and her college gave Theosophical ideas the form of Hindu religious instruction for schoolboys, at an institution that later became a constituent college of the Banaras Hindu University. Her Home Rule work belongs to the same turn toward India, though the sources read here say little about how she tied it to her teaching.

The tree shows three edges. The Theosophical Society made her president, an institutional edge that is documented. Her edge to Krishnamurti is documented as guardianship, dated March 1910 in the book's Krishnamurti chapter. Blavatsky stands behind her as the teacher who converted her.

The book asks what she took a teacher to be. She took him to be a vehicle chosen and trained from outside, and Krishnamurti answered differently in public.
