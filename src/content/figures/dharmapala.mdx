## Life

David Hewavitarne was born on the night of 17 September 1864 in the Pettah district of Colombo, the son of Mudaliyar Don Carolis Hewavitarne and his wife Mallika. The family was Buddhist and well off, but in colonial Ceylon the good schools were Christian, and he was educated at Catholic and Anglican schools.

In May 1880 two Theosophists, the American colonel Henry Steel Olcott and the Russian writer Helena Blavatsky, arrived in Ceylon. The sixteen-year-old heard Olcott lecture. In early 1884 he joined the Theosophical Society (one account gives January, another 3 February), and in December he went to its headquarters at Adyar, near Madras, over his family's objections. Blavatsky, by Sangharakshita's account, told him that he "should study Pali, where all that was needed could be found." He became Olcott's translator on tours of the island, took the name Dharmapala, "guardian of the Dhamma," and called himself *anagarika*, "homeless one."

He also taught himself to meditate, from a palm-leaf manual found in a village temple (later published as *The Manual of a Mystic*) and the classical texts, since he could find no living teacher. In 1889 he lectured in Japan with Olcott.

On 22 January 1891 he reached Bodh Gaya, the place of the Buddha's awakening, with a Japanese priest, Kozen. The great temple was in the hands of the mahant, the abbot of a Shaiva monastery whose line, by the temple committee's account, goes back to an ascetic who settled there in 1590. Broken statues lay about the grounds. In his diary Dharmapala wrote, "We both solemnly promised that we would stop here until some Buddhist priests came to take charge of the place." That May, in Colombo, he founded the Maha Bodhi Society with the monk Hikkaduwe Sumangala as president and Olcott as director (31 May in Sangharakshita, 3 May in Banglapedia). In 1892 the office moved to Calcutta and the journal *The Maha Bodhi* began.

In 1893 the organisers of the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago invited him to speak for the Buddhists of South Asia, at the same meeting where Vivekananda spoke. He brought, in his words, "the good wishes of four hundred and seventy-five millions of Buddhists," and his paper, "The World's Debt to Buddha," was read on 18 September. On the way home, in Honolulu, he met Mary Foster, who became his chief patron. He lectured in America again from 1896, at the invitation of the writer Paul Carus.

The Bodh Gaya campaign went to court. On 25 February 1895 Dharmapala placed a Japanese image of the Buddha in the temple, and the mahant's men removed it. A magistrate convicted three of them in July. The sessions judge kept the fines and suspended the jail terms, and on 22 August 1895 the Calcutta High Court set the convictions aside. The government refused in 1897 to treat the temple as a purely Hindu shrine, but the mahant kept control for the rest of Dharmapala's life. In 1905 Olcott resigned from the Maha Bodhi Society and Dharmapala left the Theosophical Society. The Theosophists' own history puts the cause in their priorities, Bodh Gaya for Dharmapala and general Buddhist teaching for Olcott, and the scholar David McMahan links the break to Dharmapala's rejection of Theosophy's universalism.

In 1915 riots broke out between Sinhalese Buddhists and Muslims in Ceylon. The British banned his Sinhala newspaper, *Sinhala Bauddhaya*, and kept him under restriction in Calcutta until 1920 (the Sri Lankan National Archives say six years). His brother Edmund was detained during the riots and died while held; the National Archives exhibit does not give the cause or place. In his diary Dharmapala called it "cold-blooded murder." In the 1920s he founded a branch in London and built the Mulagandhakuti Vihara at Sarnath, where the Buddha first taught. It opened on 11 November 1931. That year he took novice ordination at Sarnath as Sri Devamitta Dhammapala, and in January 1933 monks from Ceylon gave him full ordination there. He died at Sarnath on 29 April 1933.

In Dharmapala's use, a celibate lay worker who lives like a renunciant and works in the world. Later Theravada teachers, Anagarika Munindra among them, used the same title.</aside>

## The teaching

Dharmapala wrote no system. At Chicago he presented the Buddha as a teacher of reason who asked for inquiry before belief: "The strongest emphasis has been put by Buddha on the supreme importance of having an unprejudiced mind." His farewell to the Parliament began, "Learn to think without prejudice," and asked his hearers to set aside dogma that stood in the way of truth. His own life as an anagarika made the case that serious practice was open to lay people as well as monks.

The anthropologist Gananath Obeyesekere called this new style "Protestant Buddhism," and he treated Dharmapala as the key figure of the revival. In his account the revival borrowed forms from the Christian missions it opposed, such as the Sunday school, and moved Buddhism from the village temple to the town. Steven Kemper notes that the term carries both meanings, a protest against Christianity and against Western rule. The philosopher Amod Lele objects that neither Olcott nor Dharmapala was a Protestant and that the label sounds like an accusation.

The other side of the teaching was national. Dharmapala wrote and published in Sinhala for a Sinhala Buddhist public, and he was a leader of the temperance movement. In a letter of 15 June 1915 to the Secretary of State for the Colonies (printed in Ananda Guruge's *Return to Righteousness*), he wrote, "The Muhammedans, an alien people, who in the early part of the 19th century were common traders, by Shylockian methods became prosperous like the Jews." Later quotations of the same letter have him compare the Muslim's place among the Sinhalese to the German's among the British. In *Leveling Crowds* the anthropologist Stanley Tambiah quotes Dharmapala's paper *Sinhala Bauddhaya* complaining in 1912 that "the Sinhalese are obliged to fall at the feet of the Coast Moors and Tamils." Reviewing H.L. Seneviratne's *The Work of Kings* in 2001, Kemper wrote that the later monk Yakkaduve Pragnarama "spoke of Tamils in the same racist terms as Dharmapala." In *Rescued from the Nation* (2015), drawing on nearly a hundred diaries and notebooks, Kemper reads him, in the publisher's words, as "a world-renouncer first and a political activist second," whose lifelong aim was a unified Buddhist world. In Sri Lanka he is honoured as a figure of the independence struggle, and the National Archives exhibit him in those terms.

## Lineage

Dharmapala stands where Sri Lankan Theravada meets the Theosophical Society. Olcott and Blavatsky shaped his first years of work, and he broke with them over Bodh Gaya.

<MiniTree id="dharmapala" />

His importance for this part is practical. He put Buddhist monks, money and a journal back into India, and he built at Sarnath a temple where Indian Buddhists could gather. He did not live to see Bodh Gaya change hands. The Bodh Gaya Temple Act of 1949 placed the temple under a committee of four Buddhists and four Hindus chaired by the district magistrate, and the mahant handed over management in May 1953. Buddhist protesters in 2025 were still asking for the Act's repeal. The Buddhism he brought home was the reformed Theravada of Ceylon. In 1950 the society's journal printed Ambedkar's essay "The Buddha and the Future of His Religion," and Ambedkar went on to read the Buddha in his own way. The edges to Olcott and the Theosophical Society are documented in Sangharakshita's biography and the Theosophical Society's own history. The link to Ambedkar runs through the society's journal, seventeen years after Dharmapala's death.
