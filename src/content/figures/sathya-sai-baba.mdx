<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> <i>Sathya</i> means truth, and was the short form of his birth name, Sathyanarayana. <i>Sai Baba</i> he took from the fakir of Shirdi, whom he said he had been in his previous life. Devotees call him Swami or Bhagavan.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>The movement's own account sets the day as 20 October 1940, in the small town of Uravakonda, where the boy is living with his elder brother Seshama Raju and going to school. The family has just come back from a trip to Hampi. In the morning Sathyanarayana sets out for school as usual and comes back within minutes.</p>
<p>On the front step he throws down his books. When his brother's wife comes to the door, he tells her, as the movement's biographers record it, "I am no longer your Sathya. I am Sai." He says that maya, illusion, has left him, that his devotees are calling him, and that he has his work. His brother arrives and is told to stop trying to cure him. The boy will not go back into the house. He goes instead to the garden of a neighbour, an excise inspector named Anjaneyulu, sits under the trees, and people begin to come and sing with him.</p>
<p>A few days later he leaves Uravakonda for Puttaparthi, the village where he was born. The Sathya Sai movement keeps 20 October as Avatar Declaration Day. Everything in this scene comes from devotional sources, written by people who believed what the boy said.</p>
</section>

## Life

Sathyanarayana Raju was born in Puttaparthi, a village in the dry Anantapur district of what is now Andhra Pradesh. The movement gives the date as 23 November 1926; the journalist Vishal Arora, writing in *The Caravan* in 2010, noted that independent proof of the date is hard to find. His family were Bhatrajus, a Telugu community of balladeers and religious singers. The movement's biographies tell of signs around his infancy, such as instruments playing by themselves and a cobra under his cradle. These are devotional stories, with no independent witness.

The decisive year was 1940. According to the movement's account, on 8 March 1940, at Uravakonda, a large black scorpion stung him at dusk. He seemed well that night. The next evening he fell unconscious and stiff, and over the following weeks he behaved in ways his family could not explain: long silences, then songs, poems, Sanskrit verses and talk of Vedantic philosophy. His brother brought a doctor and then an exorcist, who shaved the boy's head, cut marks into his scalp and poured stinging juices on the wounds. The parents stopped the treatment.

On 23 May 1940, the movement says, he called the household together, handed out sugar candy and flowers that seemed to come from nowhere, and said, "I am Sai Baba." His father, by this account, suspected that a Muslim spirit was speaking through the boy and asked what they were to do with him. The answer was "Worship Me!" Five months later came the doorstep scene, the break with family and school, and the move to Puttaparthi. He was thirteen. The movement's own pages say fourteen, counting in the Indian way.

<aside class="note"><strong>Shirdi.</strong> The man he claimed to have been is the subject of an earlier chapter: <a href="/figures/shirdi-sai-baba/">Sai Baba of Shirdi</a>, the fakir who lived in a ruined mosque in Maharashtra and died in 1918, eight years before Sathyanarayana was born.</aside>

The claim to be Shirdi Sai Baba reborn was his own, made as a boy, and it has been accepted by his followers and disputed outside the movement. The Shri Saibaba Sansthan, the trust that runs the shrine at Shirdi, repeatedly told the scholar Antonio Rigopoulos during fieldwork in October 1985 that "Sai Baba has neither successors nor heirs," while rejecting a different claimant, Narayan Baba. In the same diary entry Rigopoulos wrote that Sathya Sai Baba's claimed connection to Shirdi "requires a thorough investigation," and that to his knowledge he never came to Shirdi. The book's tree records the link as a claim, made by Sathya Sai Baba and held by his followers.

Through the 1940s the young Baba lived and held gatherings in Puttaparthi, singing <i>bhajans</i>, devotional songs, with visitors, and the crowds grew. Construction of an ashram at the edge of the village began in 1948, and it was inaugurated on his birthday, 23 November 1950, as Prasanthi Nilayam, the "abode of supreme peace." In 1948, in Bangalore, he met Narayana Kasturi (1897 to 1987), who became his biographer, translator and editor. Kasturi's *Sathyam Sivam Sundaram*, Part I, appeared in 1960 and covers the first thirty-four years of the life. Almost every later account of Sathya Sai Baba's childhood rests on it. From 1958 Kasturi also edited the movement's monthly, *Sanathana Sarathi*.

His first public discourse, according to Rigopoulos, was given at Prasanthi Nilayam on 17 October 1953, the last day of the Dasara festival. From then on he spoke in Telugu to large audiences on festival days, usually with a translator at his side. The talks were collected in the series *Sathya Sai Speaks*.

In 1963 he fell gravely ill. His followers describe a stroke and heart attacks that left one side paralysed for some days. On 6 July 1963, Guru Purnima, he spoke to the crowd and said the illness was one he had taken on to save a devotee. In the same discourse he set out the theology that frames his whole career. Shiva and Shakti, he said, had promised the sage Bharadwaja that they would be born three times in his lineage: "Shiva alone as Shirdi Sai Baba," "Shiva and Shakthi together at Puttaparthy as Sathya Sai Baba," and "Shakthi alone as Prema Sai, later." He told the audience, "I belonged to the Apasthamba Suthra and the Bharadwaja lineage." The prophecy of a third Sai, Prema Sai, still to come, is part of the movement's expectation today.

The organisation grew in the 1960s. The movement dates its seed to 1961, when he announced a service committee, and the first Sri Sathya Sai Seva Samithi was formally set up in Bombay on 5 July 1965. A first all-India conference of office-bearers met in Madras in April 1967, and the first World Conference in Bombay in May 1968. In July 1968 he visited Kenya and Uganda. Children's classes in values, first called Bal Vihar, were renamed Bal Vikas in December 1971. The Sri Sathya Sai Central Trust was set up in 1972 to hold the movement's property and run its institutions.

In the 1970s and 1980s the ashram drew visitors from Europe, North and South America, Australia and Southeast Asia, many of them middle-class people with no Hindu background. Visitors describe the shape of his public life: <i>darshan</i>, when he walked slowly through the seated crowds and sometimes waved his hand and gave out <i>vibhuti</i>, sacred ash; private "interviews" with chosen individuals or groups; festival discourses; and a yearly round of holy days at Puttaparthi and at his second ashram at Whitefield near Bangalore. Presidents, prime ministers, judges and industrialists visited him. So did sceptics.

Through these decades the institutions multiplied, each presented as the teaching put into practice. A college for women at Anantapur, opened in 1962, grew into the Sri Sathya Sai Institute of Higher Learning, founded on 22 November 1981, with the motto *Satyam Vada, Dharmam Chara*, "speak the truth, act rightly." A free general hospital had opened in Puttaparthi in 1954. The Sri Sathya Sai Institute of Higher Medical Sciences, a large specialist hospital outside the village, opened on 22 November 1991, and the movement says it is "entirely free," charging nothing for surgery, doctors or beds. In 1995 a drinking water project began in Anantapur district, later extended to other districts and to Chennai. The movement treats all of these as <i>seva</i>, service, and service as worship.

In 2000 he told a gathering at Puttaparthi that he would live to ninety-six. He used a wheelchair in his last years. On 28 March 2011 he was admitted to the hospital he had built, after giddiness and a slowing heartbeat, and he died there on the morning of 24 April 2011, after several weeks in intensive care, of multiple organ failure. He was eighty-four by the movement's birth date; some reports, including the AFP obituary, gave eighty-five. Andhra Pradesh declared four days of state mourning. He was buried with state honours on 27 April in Sai Kulwant Hall, the darshan hall at Prasanthi Nilayam, in the presence of the prime minister, Manmohan Singh.

## The teaching

Sathya Sai Baba's teaching is simple in its statements and large in its claims. It has three layers, and devotees hold all three together: a moral teaching anyone can follow, a universalist account of religion, and the claim that the teacher is God in human form.

**The five human values.** The moral core is a list of five Sanskrit words: <span class="script" lang="sa">सत्य</span> *sathya*, truth; *dharma*, right conduct; *shanti*, peace; *prema*, love; and *ahimsa*, non-violence. The movement presents them as the values common to all religions and as the curriculum of its schools and its children's classes, where they are taught through stories, songs, group activity and silent sitting. Of the five, love carries the weight. The best-known summary of his teaching is a pair of slogans the movement still uses: "Love All, Serve All" and "Help Ever, Hurt Never." Another favourite, quoted on the international organisation's website, runs: "Start the day with love. Fill the day with love. End the day with love."

**Service as spiritual practice.** He told his followers that helping others was a way of reaching God, and gave the organisation the task of service. In his own words, as the Indian organisation quotes them, "Seva is saadhana, not a pastime of the rich and well placed." Members visit hospitals, feed the poor, run medical camps and clean temples, and they are taught to do this as worship of the God who lives in the person served. The organisation, he said, was "set up to develop and foster Love."

**One God, many religions.** He did not ask anyone to convert. He is most often quoted as saying that he had come so that a Hindu becomes a better Hindu, a Muslim a better Muslim and a Christian a better Christian. The wording varies between versions, and the original talk was not traced. The movement's emblem, the Sarva Dharma symbol, puts this in a picture: a pillar topped by a lotus and a flame at the centre, with the Hindu Om, the Buddhist wheel, the Zoroastrian fire, the Islamic crescent and star and the Christian cross around it. The movement presents the symbol as an emblem of the harmony of religions and the unity of all faiths. The movement's children's classes teach <i>sarva dharma</i> bhajans, songs drawn from several faiths.

Underneath the universalism the theology is Vedantic. The God in all religions is the one Self in all beings, and the movement's own summary has him teaching that the true dwelling place of God is "the heart of man, and the same God dwells in all beings." Liberation means seeing that unity, and love is the way to it. In a 1976 interview, as later summaries report it, he described his aim as re-establishing Sanathana Dharma, the eternal religion of one God that, in his view, stands behind the historical ones. Scholars who have studied the movement, including Lawrence Babb and Deborah Swallow, have read its ritual and theology as recognisably Hindu in form, whatever its universal language.

**The avatar and the miracles.** The third layer is the claim about himself. In the 1963 discourse he said he was Shiva and Shakti together. Devotees call him an avatar, God descended in human form, and many credit him with knowing their thoughts, appearing in dreams, healing illness and producing objects out of the air. The most familiar of these was vibhuti, sacred ash, which devotees say poured from his moving hand at darshan and which they keep as a blessing. They also describe rings, watches, necklaces, small images and sweets produced with a wave of the palm, and, at the festival of Mahashivaratri, small stone or metal <i>lingams</i>, emblems of Shiva, that came out of his mouth after visible struggle. The movement reports that this public rite was stopped in 1977 and revived in 1999.

He gave these acts a teaching purpose. Movement writers report that he spoke of miracles as *nidarshana*, signs to establish divinity, as opposed to *pradarshana*, display. The anthropologist Lawrence Babb, writing in 1983, singled out the very strong emphasis on the miraculous as the movement's most striking feature. For devotees, the miracles and the moral teaching belong together, because the one who asks them to love and serve is, in their eyes, the one who made the world.

Babb judged the teachings simple, eclectic and largely unoriginal, and on the content of the doctrine I think he was mostly right. The five values, the unity of religions and service as worship had all been said before, and said by people who are in this book. What I find new is the packaging. He took the universalism of the Ramakrishna movement, gave it the warmth of a village bhajan, tied it to a three-life story about himself, and set up an organisation that could run children's classes in Nairobi or Kuala Lumpur without ever mentioning a Hindu god. Whether that counts as originality depends on how much weight one gives to form, and here I give it some.

## Lineage

He had no guru and acknowledged none. His place on the tree comes from the claim he made about himself, and from the people and institutions he shaped.

The edge from Sai Baba of Shirdi is marked as a claim, graded hagiographic: it rests on his own statements of 1940 and 1963 and on the movement's biographies, and the trust that runs the Shirdi shrine holds that Sai Baba has no successors. The edge to the coming Prema Sai is a prophecy, recorded in the 1963 discourse. The documented edges run outward: to Narayana Kasturi, his biographer from 1948, and to the Sathya Sai Organisation, whose first committee was formed in 1965. The influence of Advaita Vedanta is visible in the discourses and is marked as documented.

## The practice

The organisation asks members to follow a short code of practice. The steps below are drawn from the movement's own descriptions.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Daily prayer and meditation.</strong> Members are asked to pray and meditate each day.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Bhajan singing.</strong> Weekly devotional singing with the family, and group singing at the local centre.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Study.</strong> Reading the discourses, and teaching the five human values to children in Bal Vikas classes.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Seva.</strong> Regular service in hospitals, villages and relief work, done as worship and without payment.</div></li>

## Critiques and questions

**The miracles.** Rationalists argued from the 1970s that the materialisations were sleight of hand. In 1976 H. Narasimhaiah, a physicist and vice-chancellor of Bangalore University, set up a committee to investigate miracles and other claims that could be tested. It wrote to Sathya Sai Baba three times over about six weeks, asking him to perform under controlled conditions. He did not reply, and the letters were released to the press. In an interview with the editor of *Blitz*, R. K. Karanjia, he argued, in the reported paraphrase, that scientific method could not grasp his powers. The rationalist Basava Premanand campaigned against him for decades and in 1986 petitioned the Andhra Pradesh High Court, arguing that materialising gold without a licence broke the Gold Control Act; the petition failed. A Channel 4 film, *Guru Busters* (1995), showed rationalists reproducing such feats as tricks. In 1992 the *Deccan Chronicle* published stills from state television footage that it said showed an assistant passing him a chain; the Icelandic psychologist Erlendur Haraldsson and the British psychologist Richard Wiseman examined the footage and concluded in 1995 that it "offers no unequivocal evidence of fraud." Haraldsson, who had observed him in 1973 and 1974 with Karlis Osis, reported events he could not explain and also recorded that Sathya Sai Baba declined controlled tests. No independent test of the materialisations was ever carried out.

**The 1993 killings at Prasanthi Nilayam.** On the night of 6 June 1993, four young men armed with knives entered his residence in the ashram. Two of his attendants were stabbed to death and two others were injured; Sathya Sai Baba was unhurt. The four locked themselves in a room. Police climbed to the first floor, tried to break down the door and shot all four dead, saying the men had attacked them when the door opened. Six people died in all. The motive has never been established. The intruders were described as devotees; two others said to be involved escaped and were caught later, and said they had meant to warn him about trust members, not to harm him. Indulal Shah, a leading trustee, is reported (by *The Caravan* in 2010 and by Wikipedia, which cite *The Hindu* of 10 June 1993; the original was not seen) to have said that the matter was purely internal and that the trust did not want any law enforcement agency to investigate it. In December 1993 the state CID registered a case against the Puttaparthi police over the shooting, and on 30 December told a local court that the police had tampered with the scene, moving the bodies to fit their version; some policemen were suspended. Accounts of the outcome differ in detail. The BBC film of 2004, as summarised on Wikipedia, said some officers were arrested but never charged and the case was eventually dropped; *The Caravan*, citing the same film, reported that it was dropped after intervention from the central government. Premanand's writ petitions about the killings were dismissed. V. P. B. Nair, a former senior Andhra Pradesh official (described variously as a retired director general of police and as a former home secretary), later said publicly that the police account was untrue. The reports consulted record no conviction over the six deaths, and Sathya Sai Baba later said in a discourse that there had been no attempt on his life.

**Allegations of sexual abuse.** From the 1970s some former followers alleged that he made sexual approaches to young men and boys in private interviews. The American Tal Brooke, who had been a devotee, published a hostile account, *Lord of the Air* (1976), later reissued as *Avatar of Night*, which other ex-devotees cited when they came forward. Around 2000 the allegations multiplied. By 2002 the Swedish actor Conny Larsson, a former devotee, was saying publicly that he had been molested, and former followers had circulated written testimonies. In 2000, as the AFP obituary reported, Sathya Sai Baba told followers, "Some people out of their mean-mindedness are trying to tarnish the image of Sai Baba." In December 2001 an open letter signed by the prime minister, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, the former chief justices P. N. Bhagwati and Ranganath Misra, and others said, in the wording later reports give, that they were pained by allegations they called wild, reckless and concocted. On 17 June 2004 BBC Two broadcast *The Secret Swami* in its *This World* series, in which former devotees, including the American Alaya Rahm and the Briton Mark Roche, described sexual abuse in interviews. Rahm filed a civil suit in the Superior Court of Orange County, California, in 2005 against the Sathya Sai Baba Society of America and its California leaders. Accounts from both sides agree that he withdrew it himself in 2006, before trial; no court record or neutral news report of the suit was found for this entry. Defenders of Sathya Sai Baba presented this as a collapse of the case, while a statement issued for the Rahm family said his lawyer had advised that liability could not be established against the society and cited the statute of limitations. The suit made no finding on the allegations. The United States State Department issued a travel warning about "inappropriate sexual behaviour by a prominent local religious leader," which officials confirmed referred to him. In 2006 the movement's UK national youth coordinator, Shitu Chudasama, told <i>The Guardian</i> the claims were "totally unfounded." Sathya Sai Baba denied the allegations and was never charged with any offence in India or elsewhere, and no court made any finding on them.

**Money and succession.** He named no successor, and the movement expects the next Sai to come as Prema Sai. His death left the Sri Sathya Sai Central Trust, whose members included his nephew R. J. Ratnakar Raju, the former chief justice P. N. Bhagwati, the chartered accountant Indulal Shah, the industrialist V. Srinivasan, and others, in charge of assets that press estimates put in the tens of thousands of crores of rupees. On 16 and 17 June 2011 trustees opened Yajur Mandir, his private residence, in the presence of two retired judges and a government-approved valuer. The trust said it found cash, gold, silver and jewellery worth about Rs 30 crore, deposited it in the bank and paid advance tax on it; press reports gave 98 kg of gold, 307 kg of silver and Rs 11.56 crore in cash. On the night of 18 June police seized about Rs 35 lakh in cash from a trust vehicle travelling toward Bangalore, at the Kodikonda checkpost. The trust said the money had been given by devotees for building his tomb, and the trustees R. J. Ratnakar and V. Srinivasan, asked by police for statements, denied that trust money was being moved out illegally. Three men were arrested and released on bail; the reports consulted record no conviction. *Open* magazine reported that the trust's legal adviser had confirmed finding a handwritten will asking that his caregiver, Satyajit, be given a prominent place in the trust, while some trustees denied that any will existed; the trust's press release disputed reports of a will, noting "a reference to an 'oral will', a concept which is unknown to law." Some family members criticised the caregiver's handling of his last illness. Ahead of a later anniversary of his death, probably in 2012, a relative, Ganapathi Raju, sent a memorandum to the state's chief minister and governor seeking Ratnakar's removal as a trustee and alleging financial misconduct; sources at the ashram dismissed the allegations. The trust said the institutions "will be administered well and transparently."

## Descendants

The Sri Sathya Sai Central Trust still runs Prasanthi Nilayam, the university and the hospitals, and the Sri Sathya Sai International Organisation, chaired since 2011 by Narendranath Reddy, coordinates centres abroad. The organisation reports about 1,200 centres in well over a hundred countries; estimates of the number of devotees range from about six million to many times that, and none is independent. Most devotees are of Indian origin, in India and in Indian communities abroad, with smaller groups of Western followers. Many wait for Prema Sai, whom he said would be born after him. Beyond the movement's own centres, his influence shows in the values-education programmes his followers run, which the movement says have founded schools in more than thirty countries.
