## Life

Krishna Pattabhi Jois was born on 26 July 1915 at Kowshika, a village near Hassan in what is now Karnataka. His father was an astrologer, priest and landholder, and he was the fifth of nine children. His father taught him Sanskrit and ritual from the age of five, and no one else in the family took up yoga.

In 1927, at about twelve, he saw a lecture and demonstration by Tirumalai Krishnamacharya at the Jubilee Hall in Hassan and became his student the next day. He studied with him in Hassan for about two years. In 1930 he left home for Mysore to study Sanskrit. One account has him walking some 150 kilometres with very little money, and Wikipedia says he had two rupees. Krishnamacharya had also moved to Mysore, and the two met again about two years later. The Maharaja supported Krishnamacharya's yoga school in the Jaganmohan Palace, and Jois took part in its demonstrations and helped with the teaching. In 1933 he married Savitramma, who was fourteen when she first saw him demonstrate and asked to marry him, according to the Ashtanga history site ashtangayoga.info. They had three children between 1941 and 1944, Saraswati, Manju and Ramesh.

In March 1937, at twenty-one, he began to teach yoga at the Sanskrit College on a small stipend, reportedly ten rupees a month, and he held the post until 1973. He became a *vidwan*, a professor, in 1956. When Krishnamacharya moved to Chennai, a move complete by 1956, he left Jois, his oldest and most experienced pupil, to carry on the teaching in Mysore. From 1976 to 1978 he was honorary professor of yoga at the Government College of Indian Medicine. In 1948 he set up the Ashtanga Yoga Research Institute in his home in the Lakshmipuram neighbourhood. His book *Yoga Mala* appeared in Kannada in 1958 and in English in 1999.

Foreign students began to arrive in the 1960s. The Belgian André Van Lysebeth spent two months with him in 1964. In the 1970s a small group of Americans followed, and in 1975 Jois spent four months teaching in Encinitas, California, the start of Ashtanga's spread in the United States. In later decades he taught large workshops in America and Europe, and thousands came to Mysore each year. The institute later took the name K. Pattabhi Jois Ashtanga Yoga Institute. His daughter Saraswathi Rangaswamy and her son R. Sharath Jois carried on the teaching. Pattabhi Jois died in Mysore on 18 May 2009, aged 93.

## The teaching

Jois called his method Ashtanga yoga, the "eight-limbed" yoga of Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*, and said that posture practice was the door to the other limbs. The practice is a set of fixed series: a primary series, an intermediate series and advanced series. Each posture is joined to the next by *vinyasa*, a counted sequence of movements matched to inhalation and exhalation, so that the practice flows without pause and builds heat.

The method rests on *tristhana*, "three places" of attention: the breath, the posture and the *drishti*, a fixed point of gaze. Students breathe with an audible sound and hold the bandhas, the locks at the pelvic floor and the lower belly. Manju Jois recommended *ujjayi* breathing, while his grandson Sharath later said ujjayi as such is not used and that normal breath with an audible sound is enough. Jois recommended holding each posture for five to eight breaths, or as long as possible. The Jois Yoga archive quotes him: "When vinyāsa is perfect, the mind is under control." In the Mysore style each student practises the series at their own pace in a shared room, and the teacher gives each person new postures only when the earlier ones are steady. Teacher-led classes were added in his later years. Hands-on adjustment by the teacher was a central and expected part of the class.

**The series.** The first series is called *yoga chikitsa*, yoga for health or therapy. The second, *nadi shodhana*, is the "nerve purifier," and the advanced series is *sthira bhaga*, "centring of strength." There were originally four series, and a fifth could be attempted after mastering them. The sequences changed over his life. Jois said he had changed nothing, but his first Western students in 1975, David Williams and Nancy Gilgoff, learned three series, and before his death he taught six. Postures were added and removed, breath counts altered and the transitions became longer and more dynamic. His own book, *Yoga Mala*, tells readers to practise the postures in the order given, because in that order they purify the body methodically, and to learn the breathing and vinyasa from a teacher, not from books or photographs.

**The Yoga Korunta.** Jois taught that the system came from Krishnamacharya, who said he had learned it from an ancient text called the *Yoga Korunta*, attributed to a sage named Vamana and taught to him by his Himalayan guru. Krishnamacharya said the manuscript had been eaten by ants. Jois said he never saw it. The text is not cited in Krishnamacharya's own books of 1934 and about 1941, and no copy has been examined by scholars. Historians such as Mark Singleton connect the sequences with the gymnastics and physical culture taught at the Mysore palace in the 1930s. Manju Jois, Pattabhi Jois's son, has said that Krishnamacharya created the sequences. Singleton has also suggested that the name *ashtanga* may come from *ashtang dand*, an older gymnastic exercise, and not from Patanjali's eight limbs. Accounts differ on the manuscript. According to B. N. S. Iyengar, a fellow student, Krishnamacharya handed Jois the text at the palace temple, and Jois later said ants had eaten it.

## Lineage

Jois is the second great branch from Krishnamacharya, beside B.K.S. Iyengar. Where Iyengar slowed the postures down and added props, Jois kept the flowing sequences of the palace years.

<MiniTree id="pattabhi-jois" />

The edge from Krishnamacharya is documented from 1927. The claimed descent from the *Yoga Korunta* rests on Krishnamacharya's account alone. The family line continues through Saraswathi Rangaswamy and Sharath Jois.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Find a Mysore room.</strong> Schools that teach in the Mysore style advise watching a class for about half an hour before starting.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Learn the opening.</strong> The first lesson covers the breath and movement of vinyasa and the opening and closing sequences, and may last only fifteen or twenty minutes.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Open with an invocation.</strong> Practice begins with an invocation to Patanjali and five rounds each of the two sun salutations, followed by the standing postures.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Memorise before adding.</strong> The student practises what has been learned alone, at their own pace, and the teacher adds postures only when the earlier ones are steady.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep a rhythm.</strong> Five or six days a week at the same time of day is the usual advice, with one or two rest days. Sessions end with about five minutes of rest and the closing mantra.</div></li>
</ol>
