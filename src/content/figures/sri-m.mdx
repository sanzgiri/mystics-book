<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> The journalist Ullekh N.P. reports that a friend began calling him "Mr M" and that her niece suggested "Sri M," which stuck. In the Natha tradition his name is Madhukarnath.</aside>

## Life

Sri M was born Mumtaz Ali Khan in Thiruvananthapuram, the capital of Travancore (part of Travancore-Cochin from July 1949). Wikipedia and his publisher Penguin give the date as 6 November 1948. A biography on the Theosophical Society's convention site and a profile on the interview series *Buddha at the Gas Pump* give 6 November 1949. Ullekh N.P., writing in *Open* in 2014, says the family descended from migrants from the North West Frontier who came to Travancore in the early eighteenth century, and spoke a form of Deccani Urdu at home. Penguin describes the household as affluent and liberal.

The rest of his early story comes from his autobiography, *Apprenticed to a Himalayan Master: A Yogi's Autobiography*, published by Magenta Press; Wikipedia and library catalogues date it to 2010, and the Theosophical Society to 2011. By his account, when he was nine a young-looking stranger with matted hair appeared under a jackfruit tree in the family compound, spoke with him briefly and left. He later identified the stranger as his guru, Maheshwarnath Babaji. At nineteen he left home for the Himalaya, travelling by way of Delhi, Haridwar and Rishikesh to Badrinath. Penguin's summary of the book says that he was about to jump into the Alaknanda river when his guru appeared and initiated him into the Natha tradition. He says he met Maheshwarnath at the Vyasa cave near Badrinath and lived and travelled with him for about three and a half years. In his telling the training covered the Upanishads, Kriya Yoga and meditation, an awakening of kundalini, and a hard journey to the monastery at Tholing in western Tibet. He writes that Maheshwarnath took him to meet Sri Guru Babaji, whom he identifies with the Mahavatar Babaji of Lahiri Mahasaya's line, and that he recognised Babaji as his master in a former life. These events are known only from his account.

His guru then sent him back to the plains. He married Sunanda, from a Saraswat Brahmin family, and they have two children. *Open* lists brief jobs in journalism, including a traineeship at *India Today*, and says he worked in institutions run by J. Krishnamurti and became one of his confidants. Wikipedia adds long periods with the Ramakrishna Mission and the Krishnamurti Foundation, and a list of teachers he met, among them Bhagawan Nityananda of Ganeshpuri, Neem Karoli Baba, Lakshmanjoo and Krishnamurti himself. *Open* also reports that he wrote a book, *How to Levitate*, under the pen name James Talbot, explaining tricks used by some godmen.

According to the Buddha at the Gas Pump profile, he began teaching in 1998, a few years after his guru's death, and the Satsang Foundation grew out of that work. The Foundation calls itself a meeting point for seekers of all persuasions. Its ashram is at Madanapalle in Andhra Pradesh. It runs the Peepal Grove School, which A.P.J. Abdul Kalam inaugurated in 2006, a free school called the Satsang Vidyalaya and a health centre, and in 2020 it started a yoga teacher training programme. In 2015 and 2016 he led the Walk of Hope, a walk of about 7,500 km from Kanyakumari to Srinagar through eleven states, which began on 12 January 2015 and ended on 29 April 2016. Wikipedia and Penguin describe its aim as restoring the country's spirituality, and *Open*, before it began, reported his plan to promote communal amity through multi-faith prayer meetings. He received the Padma Bhushan in 2020 for service in the field of spirituality.

## The teaching

*Open* describes him as Muslim by birth and Hindu by initiation, and the centre of his teaching is the claim that the religions share a mystical core beneath their forms. His advice, as quoted by the Theosophical Society and *Buddha at the Gas Pump*, is "Go to the core. Theories are of no use." He admires Kabir, who, as he told *Open*, was born into a Muslim family and called the Supreme Being Ram.

His own practice is Kriya Yoga, received, by his account, through Maheshwarnath from Babaji. *Open* reports that he initiates students into it. He does not present it as the only road. "Though my parampara is kriya yoga, I don't think it suits all aspirants," he told *Buddha at the Gas Pump*. Parampara means a line of teachers. He gives as much weight to satsang, the company of people who share a search, which he says can happen between two people of any caste or creed, and to study of the Upanishads, on which he has published commentaries. *On Meditation* (2019), Penguin says, sets out a simple daily method that working people of any age can follow.

The life he recommends is a householder's. In the *Buddha at the Gas Pump* interview he described spiritual growth as part of ordinary days, with solitude as a help and complete withdrawal as unnecessary. His autobiography and its sequel also contain claims of miracles and, in *The Journey Continues*, memories of past lives. He presents these as his own experience.

## Lineage

Sri M's place on the tree depends almost entirely on his own book. No independent record of Maheshwarnath Babaji has been found, and his link to Babaji is a claim of the same kind as Lahiri Mahasaya's and Yogananda's.

<MiniTree id="sri-m" />

The edges from Maheshwarnath and from Babaji are marked as his testimony and as hagiography. His years around J. Krishnamurti and the Krishnamurti Foundation are reported in the press and by Wikipedia, and appear as an influence. Through the Satsang Foundation he has students of his own.
