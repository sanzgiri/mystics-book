## Life

Uppaluri Gopala Krishnamurti was born on 9 July 1918 in Machilipatnam on the Andhra coast. His mother died a week after his birth, and his maternal grandfather, a wealthy Brahmin lawyer and Theosophist, raised him in nearby Gudivada. He took a BA at the University of Madras, studied psychology, philosophy and mysticism, and left the master's course. From 1941 he worked at the Theosophical Society in Adyar, lectured for it abroad, and became joint general secretary of its Indian section. His association ended in the early to mid 1950s.

He married Kusuma Kumari in 1943, and they had four children. In 1955 the family went to the United States for five years for treatment of their eldest son. By 1961 he had separated from them and ended the marriage.

He listened to Jiddu Krishnamurti's Madras talks from 1947 to 1953 and began direct conversation with him in 1953. By his own account the exchange ended when he told Jiddu Krishnamurti that he had no way of knowing what he claimed, and the two met again in London. In July 1967, at Saanen in Switzerland, friends took him to hear Jiddu Krishnamurti speak. He said afterwards that the man was describing U.G.'s own state, not his own. He walked out of the tent. The next day he asked himself, "How do I know that I am in that state?" and found the question had no answer.

What followed he called "the calamity." In his account, and in the account of Douglas Rosestone, the one person present for part of it, his body appeared to die, with cold, stiffness and slowed breathing. Sources give the period of physical change as seven days or eight, and the biography at the well.com archive gives forty-nine minutes for the dying itself. He said the search had ended and what remained was the "natural state."

From then he had no home or organisation. He travelled between India, Europe and the United States and talked with whoever came. He died in Vallecrosia, Italy, on 22 March 2007 after about seven weeks in bed following a fall. Another report dates it 25 March. He asked for no rites, and his friend Mahesh Bhatt arranged the cremation.

## The teaching

U.G. denied that enlightenment exists. He called the goal a story that Indian and Western traditions sell, and called gurus "sellers of shoddy goods." On this he went further than Jiddu Krishnamurti, who still pointed to a freedom from the known. For U.G. every search was the problem. He held that the self is a construction of thought and memory, that nobody has a mind of their own, and that what each person calls a private mind is a shared "thought sphere."

The natural state was his name for what remained when thought stopped interpreting experience. It had no inner witness and no sense of progress. He described bodily changes with it, including altered senses and, in one account, ash on the skin he called burnt thoughts. He did not offer a method to reach it. He said it arrives by chance, and only after all effort to attain it has stopped.

He rejected the label of teacher. Quoted in the collected sayings, he said, "I have no teaching. There is nothing to preserve." His talks were meant to take apart whatever listeners made of them, which suits their blunt, sometimes abusive style.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="ug-krishnamurti" />

The tree draws two lines into U.G. He grew up inside the Theosophical Society, which also produced Jiddu Krishnamurti, and he heard Jiddu Krishnamurti for years before turning against him. Both edges are documented. U.G. named no successors and said he had no disciples.

## Critiques and questions

Admirers called him an honest voice against spiritual commerce. Critics, according to Wikipedia, called him a charlatan, and some pointed out that the sayings are consistent with a man who made a career of saying he had none. His account of the calamity rests mainly on his own telling and on one witness, and the sources differ on its duration.
