## Life

Uppaluri Gopala Krishnamurti was born on 9 July 1918 in Machilipatnam on the Andhra coast. His mother died a week after his birth, and his maternal grandfather, a wealthy Brahmin lawyer and Theosophist, raised him in nearby Gudivada. He took a BA at the University of Madras, studied psychology, philosophy and mysticism, and left the master's course. From 1941 he worked in C.W. Leadbeater's library for the Theosophical Society, went on a lecture tour for it, and became joint general secretary of its Indian section. His association ended in the early to mid 1950s.

He married Kusuma Kumari in 1943, and they had four children. In 1955 the family went to the United States for five years for treatment of their eldest son. By 1961 he had separated from them and ended the marriage.

He listened to Jiddu Krishnamurti's Madras talks from 1947 to 1953 and began direct conversation with him in 1953. He recalled that the conversations ended in a heated exchange in which Jiddu Krishnamurti told him, in U.G.'s retelling, that he had no way of knowing it for himself. They met again later in London. In July 1967, at Saanen in Switzerland, friends took him to hear Jiddu Krishnamurti speak. He said afterwards that the man was describing U.G.'s own state, not his own. He walked out of the tent. The next day, by his own account, he asked himself how he could know that he was in the state of the Buddha that he had so long wanted, and found the question had no answer.

What followed he called "the calamity." In his account, and in the account of Douglas Rosestone, the one person present for part of it, his body appeared to die, with cold, stiffness and slowed breathing. Mahesh Bhatt's biography describes a change each day for seven days, and gives forty-nine minutes for the dying itself, on the seventh day. Wikipedia describes the changes over about a week, with the most intense phase on the eighth day. He said the search had ended and what remained was the "natural state."

From then he had no home or organisation. He travelled between India, Europe and the United States and talked with whoever came. He died in Vallecrosia, Italy, on 22 March 2007 after about seven weeks in bed following a fall. He asked for no rites, and his friend Mahesh Bhatt arranged the cremation.

## The teaching

U.G. denied that enlightenment exists. He called the goal a story that Indian and Western traditions sell, and in one interview dismissed gurus as sellers of shoddy goods. A memorial account compiled by his friends says he came to dismiss Jiddu Krishnamurti's philosophy as a bogus, pre-charted journey. For U.G. every search was the problem. He held that the self is a construction of thought and memory, that nobody has a mind of their own, and that what each person calls a private mind is a shared "thought sphere."

The natural state was his name for what remained when thought stopped interpreting experience. It had no inner witness and no sense of progress. He described bodily changes with it, including altered senses and, in one account, a white ash on the skin that he called burnt thoughts. He did not offer a method to reach it. He said it arrives by chance, and only after all effort to attain it has stopped.

He rejected the label of teacher. He said repeatedly that he had no teaching and that there was nothing in what he said to preserve, and he refused any copyright on his words. One admirer's essay says he meant his talks to stop listeners from building conclusions out of them.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="ug-krishnamurti" />

The tree draws two lines into U.G. He grew up inside the Theosophical Society, which also produced Jiddu Krishnamurti, and he heard Jiddu Krishnamurti for years before turning against him. The break with Jiddu Krishnamurti is documented from U.G.'s own accounts. U.G. named no successors and said he had no disciples.

## Critiques and questions

Admirers called him an honest voice against spiritual commerce. Critics, according to Wikipedia, called him a charlatan. His account of the calamity rests mainly on his own telling and on one witness, and the sources differ on its duration.
