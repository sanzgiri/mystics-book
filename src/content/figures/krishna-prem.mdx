## Life

Ronald Nixon was born in Cheltenham on 10 May 1898 and went to school in Taunton. Wikipedia says his mother was a Christian Scientist. At eighteen he became a fighter pilot in the Royal Flying Corps, commissioned in 1917, and later wrote of a sense of futility and meaninglessness left by the war. At King's College, Cambridge, he read English and philosophy and met people interested in Theosophy and Buddhism, among them Christmas Humphreys. In 1921 he accepted a post at the University of Lucknow. The vice-chancellor, Gyanendra Nath Chakravarti, was a Theosophist who had represented the Indian Theosophists at the Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893. The Quest article mentions a photograph of dignitaries that includes him and Annie Besant.

Nixon became part of the Chakravarti family. Mrs Chakravarti taught him Hindi from a simplified *Bhagavata*, and he concluded that her accounts of Krishna came from direct vision, which made her the teacher he had been looking for. When her husband retired to Banaras he took a lower-paid post at Banaras Hindu University to stay near them. In 1928 she took the vows of a renunciant as Sri Yashoda Ma and initiated him with the name Krishna Prem. Friends called him Gopalda. The deed of trust for the Temple and Ashram of Uttar Brindaban at Mirtola, a sixty-acre farm about eighteen kilometres from Almora, is dated 4 July 1929, and the temple was built between 1929 and 1931. Yashoda Ma died in 1944 and he became head. In 1948 he visited South India and met Ramana Maharshi, Sri Aurobindo and the Mother. He died at Mirtola on 14 November 1965. President Radhakrishnan called him a great soul.

His successor was Alexander Phipps, born in Edinburgh in 1920, an aeronautical engineer who was posted to India with the RAF. During a holiday in 1944 he visited Ramana Maharshi's ashram at Tiruvannamalai, which began his search. He reached Mirtola after the war, took the name Madhava Ashish and became head in 1965. He turned the ashram into a farm that fed itself, wrote on agriculture and ecology, received the Padma Shri in 1992 and died there of cancer on 13 April 1997. His disciple Dev Ashish succeeded him as head.

## Why this figure appears here

Part 7 asks whether surrender is the path. Krishna Prem's answer was taken from Yashoda Ma, who held the Bhagavata vision of Krishna as a living presence, and from the Gaudiya Vaishnava tradition with which he was affiliated. Scholars quoted on Wikipedia call him perhaps the first Westerner on the path of Krishna devotion (David Haberman) and the first European to embrace Vaishnavism in India (Ferdinando Sardella). The Quest article traces Yashoda Ma's own initiation to Bal Krishna Goswami of the Radha Raman temple at Brindaban, a Gaudiya temple. A Mirtola website says that he and Madhava Ashish taught in an increasingly direct, less religious way as the years passed, and that disciples came from across the world.

The line is unusual in what it combines. Krishna Prem inherited the Theosophical circle at Lucknow, and Theosophical Society ideas stayed with him: a Theosophy magazine article, which calls itself a collection of anecdotes, mentions his first draft of a commentary on the Stanzas of Dzyan from Blavatsky's *Secret Doctrine*. He also inherited the Bhagavata devotion he received from Yashoda Ma and the Upanishadic and Gita study that fills his books. What was new is the joining of the two. Madhava Ashish's own commentaries on the Stanzas of Dzyan, including *Man, the Measure of All Things*, the book he wrote with Krishna Prem, carried the Theosophical text into a Vaishnava ashram. In this book he is the one figure who sat among Theosophists, took Vaishnava initiation and went on writing about both.

Documented edges on the tree run from Yashoda Ma to Krishna Prem by initiation, and from Krishna Prem to Madhava Ashish as mentor to successor. Ramana's role in Madhava Ashish's turn to India is documented through Wikipedia. The edge from Blavatsky is an influence through her book and rests on the commentary he wrote on it. The sources read here do not give the details of Krishna Prem's practice. Descriptions of his daily routine exist in memoirs by disciples, which were not consulted.
