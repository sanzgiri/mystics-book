## Life

Almost everything written about Ayya Vaikundar comes from his followers. They say he was born in 1809 in a poor Nadar family at Poovandanthoppu, a hamlet near Tamaraikkulam, about five miles from Kanyakumari, in the princely state of Travancore. The Nadars were palmyra climbers and toddy tappers, ranked low in the local caste order. A 2019 paper in the *Journal of Emerging Technologies and Innovative Research* (JETIR) gives his parents' names as Ponnu Nadar and Veyilal Ammaiyar. It says that the family first named him Mudisoodum Perumal, that upper-caste people and officials objected to the name, and that he became Muthukutty. English Wikipedia reports the same account and notes that its biographical details come mainly from texts compiled by followers.

The central event is dated to March 1833. In his followers' account, he fell ill with a skin disease, and his mother took him to the Masi festival at Tiruchendur. There he walked into the sea, vanished for about three days and came out as a new being. He took the name Vaikundar. Ayyavazhi tradition holds that Vishnu entered him in the sea. After that he is called *Ayya*, "father." Wikipedia's article says the movement dates the event to 1 March 1833, and its timeline gives 4 to 6 March. Nothing outside the devotional record confirms the sea episode.

He then walked south to Swamithoppu, where in January 1834 he began a penance that his followers describe as three stages of two years each. One Wikipedia timeline makes it about seven years. The English Wikipedia article on the historical Vaikundar, drawing on devotional texts, describes a first phase from 1834 spent standing in a pit with little speech and no food, a second from December 1835 spent seated at ground level, and a third from January 1838 on a raised pedestal. It says the second phase was directed at ending caste discrimination and the third at the uplift of women and of future generations. His followers began a collective penance called *Thuvayal Thavasu*, dated to 1840. Followers tell of five chief disciples, called Seedars after the Pandava brothers of the *Mahabharata*. The Wikipedia article on the historical Vaikundar names them Dharma, Bheeman, Arjunan, Nakulan and Sahadevan Seedar, while the article on Ayyavazhi applies the title to the scribe Hari Gopalan as well. He founded shrines and died in 1851. His followers do not use the word death. They say he returned to Vaikundam, Vishnu's abode, on 2 or 3 June.

## The teaching

The scripture of the movement is the *Akilathirattu Ammanai*, a long Tamil verse text. Followers say that it was dictated in 1841 to a disciple, Hari Gopalan Citar. Wikipedia names the same scribe elsewhere as Arigopalan Citar and gives its dictation date as 10 December 1841 in one place and 11 December 1840 in another. Early editions carry no publication data, so the print dates are uncertain. A second book, the *Arul Nool*, collects prayers, hymns and rules of worship.

In the Akilam's story, the world is moving from an age of Kali to an age of dharma. Kali is an evil force that has taken several forms over the ages, and Vaikundar came as an incarnation of Narayana to end it. In its cosmology a primordial evil, Kroni, divides across six ages into fragments, and the last of them, Kaliyan, embodies illusion in the present age. The movement teaches a supreme Oneness, *Ekam*, from which creation unfolded, and holds that human souls reflect it. Followers speak of Narayana as Father, Mahalakshmi as Mother and Vaikundar as Son, and address God as Ayya. Wikipedia adds that the eight ages are often read philosophically as eight centres of the body, ending in the lotus at the crown. English Wikipedia describes the thought as monistic in parts and closer to monotheism in its focus on Vaikundar. The Akilam rejects some Hindu ideas and holds that the Vedas were corrupted by Kali. It runs to more than fifteen thousand verses in seventeen sections. Wikipedia's article on Ayyavazhi dates its first printing to 1933 and that of the *Arul Nool* to 1927; a footnote there gives 1939 and 1918, and the article on Vaikundar gives 1939. Several versions circulate, named for the centres that preserve them.

The social teaching is the best attested part. Followers record that he told them to bathe at common wells and eat together regardless of caste, and Wikipedia gives the refrain "Uplift of the lowly is dharmam." Wikipedia says the movement condemns caste inequality more than caste as such. The early followers came largely from poor and marginalised groups, mainly Nadars, and the English article says many people from other castes also joined. The movement's Nizhal Thangals and Pathis (holy places) have no idols and no priestly class. Worship, called *panividai*, is performed at least once daily, and some Nizhal Thangals use a mirror where other temples use an image. The article counts more than seven thousand Nizhal Thangals in South India. They serve for worship, for study of the scripture, for charity and, in earlier times, for schooling.

Wikipedia's article on the teachings adds the ethical side. Lower castes, barred from temples, had worshipped at small mud or brick pyramids, and he offered a new form of worship in their place. He taught that devils and demons do not exist and that sorcery has no effect, and he rejected blood sacrifice. He told followers to fear no one except Vaikundar. A verse the article quotes in translation says, "If you live with dignity and self-respect, the Kali would destroy itself." Charity and almsgiving are counted as *dharmam*. Followers wear headgear during worship, a custom the article ties to human dignity, and mark the forehead with a flame-shaped sign called the *Thirunamam*.

## Lineage

Followers regard him as the first and foremost full incarnation of Narayana, so the movement names no human teacher. The Akilam holds that the Hindu scriptures were corrupted at the start of the present age, and so it stands apart from Shaivism and Vaishnavism, while Wikipedia notes that some of its ideas resemble Advaita and the Smarta tradition. Many followers regard Ayyavazhi as a Hindu sect, and the census counts it as Hindu. The Wikipedia article on Ayyavazhi lists seven chief shrines, five of which are counted as the most important, with Swamithoppu as the religious headquarters. Most are run by independent committees. Links with other reformers of the south are asserted in some popular accounts, but none could be verified from the sources consulted, and the tree therefore shows no edges.

## The practice

Worship in the movement is plain. The steps below summarise what Wikipedia's article on Ayyavazhi describes, and a visitor would learn the details at the shrine itself.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Go to a Nizhal Thangal or a Pathi.</strong> These are small shrines with no image and no hereditary priest. In many of them a mirror often stands where other temples keep an idol.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Cover the head.</strong> The headgear worn in worship marks equality and dignity, and the forehead carries the flame-shaped <i>Thirunamam</i>.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Perform the worship, panividai.</strong> It is offered at least once daily at a worship centre, and up to three times at some.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Give food.</strong> The meal shared without distinction of caste, called <i>Anna Dharmam</i>, is part of the practice.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Take up the penance.</strong> The collective penance, <i>Thuvayal Thavasu</i>, is described as a washing penance first held in 1840 and as training toward the coming age of dharma.</div></li>
</ol>
