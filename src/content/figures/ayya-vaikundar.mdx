## Life

Almost everything written about Ayya Vaikundar comes from his followers. They say he was born in 1809 in a poor Nadar family at Poovandanthoppu, a hamlet near Tamaraikkulam, about five miles from Kanyakumari, in the princely state of Travancore. The Nadars were palmyra climbers and toddy tappers, ranked low in the local caste order. A 2019 paper in the *Journal of Emerging Technologies and Innovative Research* (JETIR) gives his parents' names as Ponnu Nadar and Veyilal Ammaiyar. It says that the family first named him Mudisoodum Perumal, that upper-caste people and officials objected to the name, and that he became Muthukutty. English Wikipedia reports the same account and notes that its biographical details come mainly from texts compiled by followers.

The central event is dated to March 1833. In his followers' account, he fell ill with a skin disease, and his mother took him to the Masi festival at Tiruchendur. There he walked into the sea, vanished for about three days and came out as a new being. He took the name Vaikundar. Ayyavazhi tradition holds that Vishnu entered him in the sea. After that he is called *Ayya*, "father." Wikipedia's article says the movement dates the event to 1 March 1833, and its timeline gives 4 to 6 March. Nothing outside the devotional record confirms the sea episode.

He then walked south to Swamithoppu, where in January 1834 he began a penance that his followers describe as three stages of two years each. One Wikipedia timeline makes it about seven years. In his followers' account, the maharaja of Travancore, Swathi Thirunal, had him arrested in 1838 and held at Singarathoppu near Suchindram. The release date is given as 6 March or 26 March 1839, and followers say he was freed after about 110 days. Missionaries of the London Missionary Society wrote about him in their annual reports, often critically: Ebenezer Lewis in 1858 called him an "imposter." The historian G. Patrick, in *Religion and Subaltern Agency* (2003), quotes a report of 1838 saying that the government "interfered" and the excitement died away, which may refer to the arrest. After release, his followers began a collective penance called *Thuvayal Thavasu*, said to have started in March 1840. He founded shrines and died in 1851. His followers do not use the word death. They say he returned to Vaikundam, Vishnu's abode, on 2 or 3 June.

## The teaching

The scripture of the movement is the *Akilathirattu Ammanai*, a long Tamil verse text. Followers say that it was dictated in 1841 to a disciple, Hari Gopalan Citar. Wikipedia names the same scribe elsewhere as Arigopalan Citar and gives its dictation date as 10 December 1841 in one place and 11 December 1840 in another. One reference in the article gives the print dates of the text as uncertain. A second book, the *Arul Nool*, collects prayers, hymns and rules of worship.

In the Akilam's story, the world is moving from an age of Kali to an age of dharma. Kali is an evil force that has taken several forms over the ages, and Vaikundar came as an incarnation of Narayana to end it. The movement teaches a supreme Oneness, *Ekam*, from which creation unfolded, and holds that human souls reflect it. English Wikipedia describes the thought as monistic in parts and closer to monotheism in its focus on Vaikundar. The Akilam rejects some Hindu ideas and holds that the Vedas were corrupted by Kali.

The social teaching is the best attested part. Followers record that he told them to bathe at common wells and eat together regardless of caste, and Wikipedia gives the refrain "Uplift of the lowly is dharmam." Wikipedia says the movement condemns caste inequality more than caste as such. The early followers came largely from poor and marginalised groups, mainly Nadars, and the English article says many people from other castes also joined. The movement's Nizhal Thangals and Pathis (holy places) have no idols and no priestly class. Worship, called *panividai*, is performed at least once daily, and some Nizhal Thangals use a mirror where other temples use an image. The article counts more than seven thousand Nizhal Thangals in South India.

## Lineage

Followers regard him as the first and foremost full incarnation of Narayana, so the movement names no human teacher. Links with other reformers of the south are asserted in some popular accounts, but none could be verified from the sources consulted, and the tree therefore shows no edges.
