## Life

Henri Le Saux was born on 30 August 1910 at Saint-Briac in Brittany. He entered the minor seminary at Châteaugiron in 1921 and the major seminary at Rennes in 1925. On 15 October 1929 he entered the Benedictine abbey of Kergonan, made his solemn profession on 30 May 1935 and was ordained priest on 21 December 1935. Wikipedia reports that he first felt a call to India in 1934 and that his years at the abbey were interrupted by army service in 1939 to 1941.

In 1947 he wrote to the bishop of Tiruchirappalli asking for help in living a contemplative life in the manner of an Indian sannyasi. The French priest Jules Monchanin, already in Tamil Nadu, answered with practical advice. Le Saux left France on 26 July 1948, landed at Colombo on 15 August and joined Monchanin at Kulittalai a few days later. On 21 March 1950 the two inaugurated Shantivanam, a Christian ashram on the Kaveri, and Le Saux took the name Abhishiktananda.

On 24 January 1949 he had gone to Tiruvannamalai and received darshan of Ramana Maharshi, returning in August. The encounter is the one he named as formative, and from 1952 he spent long periods in the caves of Arunachala, settling at the Vanatti cave on 29 March 1952 and staying into the summer in silence. In 1953 he met H. W. L. Poonja (Papaji), according to Wikipedia. In December 1955 he met Sri Gnanananda Giri, the ascetic of Tirukoilur, whom he took as his guru, and he returned for retreats in 1956. Monchanin died on 10 October 1957.

In August 1968 he handed Shantivanam to Bede Griffiths and moved to the Himalayas. He had bought land in 1961 at Gyansu near Uttarkashi and built a small hermitage there, and he made a first pilgrimage to Gangotri in 1963. He became an Indian citizen on 18 June 1960. In October 1971 he met in Delhi a young Frenchman, Marc Chaduc, who became his disciple and, at a sannyasa initiation on 27 to 30 June 1973, Swami Ajatananda Saraswati. On 14 July 1973 Abhishiktananda suffered a heart attack in Rishikesh, which he described as a final spiritual awakening. He died in Indore on 7 December 1973; his last words are reported as <q>God's will be done</q>.

## Why this figure appears here

This part asks whether the Self can be known without method. Abhishiktananda puts the question to the tradition he came from. The record shows a sequence: Ramana in 1949, the caves of Arunachala from 1952, then a living guru in 1955.

What he inherited was Benedictine monastic life and the plan, drawn up with Monchanin and issued in 1951 as *An Indian Benedictine Ashram*, for a Christian community in Indian monastic dress. What he took from India was a claim about experience. Wikipedia summarises him as holding that the truth of the Upanishads must be realised directly and not only studied, and that realising "I am" as ultimate reality tests and dissolves fixed theological concepts. From Gnanananda he took a practice of inward withdrawal, the stilling of thought and recognition of the Self, along with the teaching that darshan of the guru is a direct realisation of the Atman. His motto, reported by Wikipedia, was <q>Beyond, always beyond</q>.

Two of his books carry the Christian side of this exchange. *Saccidananda* (1974) is subtitled "A Christian Approach to Advaitic Experience", and *Ascent to the Depth of the Heart* (1998) publishes the spiritual diary he kept from 1948 to 1973. The sources used here do not describe their arguments in detail, so this sidebar does not summarise them.

On the tree his lineage is partly Indian and partly Catholic. The Ramana edge is documented by the dated visits of 1949. The tie to Papaji is dated 1953, the year of their meeting. Gnanananda is the guru he names, a relationship recorded by the Gnanananda Peetam and in *Guru and Disciple*. Monchanin was his partner and co-founder at Shantivanam, and Bede Griffiths received the ashram from him in 1968 and continued its development. Raimon Panikkar joined others in founding the Abhishiktananda Society in 1978, which promoted his writings until it dissolved in 2008. His manuscripts are now in the Abhishiktananda Archives, and his English publishing rights lie with the Delhi Brotherhood Society.
