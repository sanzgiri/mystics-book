<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> He was born Jagadish Vasudev and called Jaggi at home. Followers and his foundation call him Sadhguru, from the Sanskrit for a true teacher. Indian courts and much of the press still use Jaggi Vasudev, and this chapter uses both.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>One afternoon in September 1982, by his own account, a twenty-five-year-old Mysore businessman rode his motorcycle up Chamundi Hill, the temple hill on the edge of his city, and went to a large rock where he often sat. He sat with his eyes open. He has told what followed many times, and Isha's published version keeps close to the same words.</p>
<p>Until that moment, he says, he had always taken it for granted that this was him and that was someone else. Then he no longer knew which was which. <q>What was me was spread all over the place,</q> he says. He thought the state lasted five or ten minutes. When it passed, it was about half past seven in the evening and four and a half hours had gone. He was weeping, he says, so much that his shirt was wet.</p>
<p>He says the experience came back over the following weeks, once for thirteen days, and that within about six weeks his voice and the shape of his eyes had changed. He closed his businesses. A year later he taught his first yoga class in Mysore, to seven people.</p>
</section>

## Life

Jagadish Vasudev was born in Mysore on 3 September 1957, the youngest of five children of B.V. Vasudev, an ophthalmologist with the railway hospital in the city, and Susheela Vasudev. He was a restless boy who liked the forests and hills around Mysore more than the classroom. At about twelve or thirteen he met Malladihalli Raghavendra Swami (1890–1996), a yoga teacher from Chitradurga district who toured Karnataka teaching postures and the sun salutation and ran a charitable trust at Malladihalli. The Swami taught him a short set of hatha yoga postures, and he says he kept up the practice every day from then on, as a physical habit, without any religious interest.

He studied English literature at the University of Mysore. He did not go on to a postgraduate degree, which his parents had wanted. He set up a poultry farm in Mysore, which made money, and then a construction business with a friend who was a civil engineer, though he had no engineering training himself. By his early twenties he was a young man with a motorcycle, a business and a circle of friends, and he has always described himself in those years as having had no spiritual interests at all.

The experience on Chamundi Hill, dated 23 September 1982 in the accounts his foundation gives, changed that. He wound up his businesses and travelled for some months. In 1983 he taught his first yoga class in Mysore, with seven participants, and over the next years he taught short courses across Karnataka and in Hyderabad. He called what he taught Sahaja Sthiti Yoga. The classes were free, and he lived, by his account, on the income from his poultry farm.

In 1984 he married Vijayakumari, known as Vijji, a bank employee he had met at one of his classes. Their only child, a daughter, Radhe, was born in 1990.

In 1992 he set up the Isha Foundation as the vehicle for his teaching. The date is given as 1993 in some press accounts. *Isha*, he says, means the formless divine. In 1994 the foundation bought land in the foothills of the Velliangiri hills, about forty kilometres west of Coimbatore in Tamil Nadu, and began building the Isha Yoga Center. Velliangiri is an old Shaiva pilgrimage range, and Jaggi Vasudev said he had known the place before, in a previous life. His foundation's account describes him seeing the seventh hill from a bend in the road one evening and telling his companions that this was the place.

The centre's first great project was the Dhyanalinga, a large stone linga consecrated as a space for meditation, open to people of any religion. The consecration, which he describes as a process of *prana pratishtha*, took three years and was completed on 24 June 1999. Isha says no rituals or prayers are performed inside it and that it is kept in silence.

Vijji died in January 1997, on 23 January in most accounts. Jaggi Vasudev and his foundation say she left her body consciously in *mahasamadhi*, a yogic death, and Isha commemorates the day. Her father filed a complaint with the police about her death; that matter is described below under "Critiques and questions."

Through the 2000s the Inner Engineering course spread across India and abroad, much of it taught by trained volunteers. The foundation also took on rural health camps, the Isha Vidhya schools for rural children, and Project GreenHands, a tree-planting programme founded in 2004 that received the Indian government's Indira Gandhi Paryavaran Puraskar in 2010. In 2016 Harmony, an imprint of Penguin Random House, published *Inner Engineering: A Yogi's Guide to Joy*, which reached the *New York Times* bestseller list. *Death: An Inside Story* followed in 2020 and *Karma: A Yogi's Guide to Crafting Your Destiny* in 2021.

On 24 February 2017, the night of Mahashivratri, Prime Minister Narendra Modi unveiled a 112-foot (34-metre) steel bust of Shiva as Adiyogi, the first yogi, at the Isha Yoga Center. Guinness World Records lists it as the largest bust sculpture. In the same year Jaggi Vasudev received the Padma Vibhushan, India's second-highest civilian award.

From 2017 his public work turned increasingly to ecology. Rally for Rivers, launched from the Isha Yoga Center on 3 September 2017, was a month-long road campaign for river protection. Cauvery Calling, launched in July 2019, set out to help farmers plant 242 crore (2.42 billion) trees in the Cauvery river basin. In 2022 he rode a motorcycle for about a hundred days from London to India for a campaign called Save Soil, and addressed the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification in May of that year.

In March 2024 he had emergency surgery at Indraprastha Apollo Hospital in New Delhi for bleeding in the skull, a subdural haematoma that the hospital said had developed over three or four weeks. He recovered and returned to teaching. In 2024 and 2025 he and his foundation were also parties to several court cases, described below.

<aside class="note"><strong>Prana pratishtha.</strong> Literally the installing of life breath. In Hindu temple practice it is the rite that makes an image into a living presence. Jaggi Vasudev uses the term for his consecrations, which he describes as an energy process carried out through his own body.</aside>

## The teaching

The centre of Jaggi Vasudev's teaching is a claim about where experience comes from. Pleasure, misery, peace and anxiety, he says, are produced inside a person, whatever their outer occasion. <q>The nature and basis of your experience is within you,</q> he says in one talk published by Isha. If that is so, then a person who learns how the inner machinery works can set it to produce well-being instead of suffering. He calls that knowledge Inner Engineering, and he uses the word technology without embarrassment. <q>Technology means doing something the way it works,</q> he says. Stress, in this account, is something people make for themselves: <q>stress is your creation.</q>

Yoga is the name he gives to this inner technology. He presents it as a science of the body, breath, mind and energy that is older than any religion and needs no belief. His most quoted line at the Adiyogi unveiling was that the next generations <q>are seekers, not believers.</q> He invites listeners to test what he says on themselves, and he is impatient with piety, with scripture taken on authority and with what he calls belief systems. At the same time he speaks freely and with complete assurance about things no listener can test: the energy body and its seven centres, consecrated objects, the dead, past lives, and the effect of the moon on food.

**Shiva as the first yogi.** He tells the story of yoga through Shiva, whom he calls Adiyogi, the first yogi, and treats less as a god to be worshipped than as the source of a body of knowledge. In his telling Adiyogi sat on the shores of Kanti Sarovar in the Himalayas and taught seven disciples, the Saptarishis, who carried yoga across the world. Isha says the 112-foot face <q>represents the 112 ways</q> in which a human being can reach the ultimate. The number echoes the 112 meditation methods of the Kashmiri tantric text *Vijnana Bhairava*, though Isha's materials do not always name it. Through this figure he gives a cosmopolitan audience a Shaiva yoga in which Shiva is the founder of a method more than the object of devotion.

**Consecration.** A second strand of his teaching, less visible to his online audience, concerns sacred space. He says that a space or an object can be charged with energy, and that sitting in such a space supports meditation. The Dhyanalinga, the Linga Bhairavi shrine and the Adiyogi figure are, in his account, consecrated forms of this kind. He describes the Dhyanalinga as the completion of a task that had failed in two earlier lives.

**Past lives.** That account of earlier lives is his own claim, given in Isha's materials and in Arundhathi Subramaniam's authorised biography, *Sadhguru: More Than a Life* (2010). About four hundred years ago, he says, he was Bilva, a snake charmer in a Shiva-worshipping tribe in what is now Madhya Pradesh, who was tied to a tree and killed by a snakebite and in his last minutes began to watch his breath. In the next life he was a yogi called Shivayogi, whose guru <q>touched me with his stick</q> and passed on, without words, the knowledge needed to consecrate the Dhyanalinga. Subramaniam's biography names this guru as Palani Swami. Shivayogi failed to build it. In a third life he was Sadhguru Sri Brahma, a fierce teacher in Tamil Nadu who, Isha says, set up many institutions, met hostility, and left his body on the seventh hill of Velliangiri, saying he would come back. These accounts are the source of his claim to a lineage, and the only evidence for them is his own testimony.

**Logic and its limits.** In talks he argues mostly by analogy and anecdote, with a comedian's timing and a businessman's directness. He rarely quotes scripture. He describes his teaching as experiential, and when asked about knowledge he likes to say that he does not know, which in his use means a state of attention more than ignorance. He has said in many talks that he has not read the *Bhagavad Gita*, the Upanishads or the *Yoga Sutras* in the usual way and teaches from what he calls inner perception; this is part of his public persona, and readers should weigh it accordingly.

I find him hard to place because he says so little that is new in doctrine and does so much that is new in form. The claim that suffering is self-made and that yoga is the method for unmaking it is as old as Patanjali. What he added was the vocabulary of engineering, the confidence of a man who had run businesses, and a figure, Adiyogi, around which a scattered set of practices could be gathered. His past-life account does the work that a guru line does for the other teachers in this part, and it is the one element of his authority that no one else can check.

## Lineage

Jaggi Vasudev stands outside the formal guru lines of this book. He had one documented yoga teacher in childhood, and he grounds his authority in his own experience of 1982 and in a past-life lineage that he alone reports.

<MiniTree id="sadhguru" />

The edge from **Malladihalli Raghavendra Swami** rests on his own account, repeated in his authorised biography, and is consistent with the Swami's well-documented career as a travelling hatha yoga teacher in Karnataka; it is graded as testimony. The edge from **Palani Swami**, the guru of his previous life, is graded as a hagiographic claim. The broad edge from the hatha yoga tradition is documented by the content of what Isha teaches. He did not take sannyasa in any order and does not claim initiation from a living teacher.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Preparation.</strong> Inner Engineering begins with a set of sessions, given in person and, since the pandemic, also online, on his ideas about well-being and responsibility. He says they bring the student <q>to the right level of receptivity.</q></div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Initiation.</strong> The student is then taught the <i>Shambhavi Mahamudra Kriya</i> in a program led by Isha teachers. The technique is not published. Students are asked not to teach it to others.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Daily practice.</strong> The kriya is a twenty-one-minute sequence of breathing and meditation, done once or twice a day. He asks for at least ninety days of unbroken practice.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hatha yoga.</strong> Isha also teaches classical hatha yoga postures and sequences, including a twenty-one-week teacher training at the Isha Yoga Center.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Sacred spaces.</strong> Practitioners are encouraged to sit in the Dhyanalinga and the other consecrated spaces at the centre, and to attend the all-night Mahashivratri festival there each year.</div></li>
</ol>

He explains the word *mudra* as a seal: <q>you lock it,</q> he says, so that energy turns in a direction it normally would not. He calls the practice a kriya, an inner action, and says it does not fit the usual categories of meditation.

## Critiques and questions

**Vijji's death, 1997.** After Vijayakumari's death in January 1997, her father filed a police complaint against Jaggi Vasudev about the death. The *New Indian Express* reported the case on 10 October 1997. According to *The Week*, the case was later dismissed by the police in Coimbatore. No charge against him has been reported. He has said he is innocent and that his wife left her body in *mahasamadhi*. When the 1997 report was recirculated on social media in March 2019 by the head of the Congress party's digital team, an Isha spokesperson called it <q>a baseless charge that was brought up by a few motivated groups</q> and said it had been dismissed long ago.

**The habeas corpus petitions, 2016 and 2024.** S. Kamaraj, a retired professor in Coimbatore, has twice asked the Madras High Court to produce his two daughters, Geetha and Latha, who live at the Isha Yoga Center as renunciates, on the ground that they were being held there against their will. A habeas corpus petition is a request to a court to have a person who is said to be unlawfully detained brought before it. In 2016 the High Court had a district judge visit the centre and record the women's statements; the petition did not lead to any finding of illegal detention. Accounts of the district judge's report differ. In 2024 Kamaraj filed again. The women, then aged 42 and 39, told the court they were living at the centre of their own free will. On 30 September 2024 a bench of Justices S.M. Subramaniam and V. Sivagnanam said more inquiry was needed, directed the police to gather details of criminal cases relating to the foundation, and, according to *LiveLaw*, asked orally why Jaggi Vasudev, whose own daughter had married, encouraged young women to renounce the world. On 1 October about 150 police officers went to the centre and stayed into the next day. Isha said in a statement that it does not ask anyone to marry or to become a monk, and that <q>adult individual human beings have the freedom and the wisdom to choose their path.</q>

The foundation appealed. On 3 October 2024 a Supreme Court bench of Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud and Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra stayed the High Court's order, told the police to take no further action under it, and moved the case to itself. The judges spoke to the two women. On 18 October the Court closed the petition, recording that both women were adults living at the ashram voluntarily and without coercion, and said the High Court's order had gone beyond what a habeas corpus case required. The Chief Justice said the purpose was not to <q>malign an organisation</q>, but that where women and children live, the foundation needed an internal complaints committee, and the Court said its order <q>will not come in the way of</q> any police investigation.

**Other matters in Coimbatore, 2023 to 2025.** The police status report placed before the Supreme Court in October 2024 referred to missing-person cases and other complaints. According to *BOOM*, police told the Madras High Court in 2024 that six people had been reported missing from the centre since 2016 and that five had been traced; one man, missing since March 2023, had not been found, and the court left the matter to the police investigation. In September 2024 a doctor working for one of Isha's rural mobile medical units was arrested under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, accused of assaulting girl students at a medical camp in a government school; Isha said it had zero tolerance for such crimes and was cooperating with the police. No verdict in that case had been reported. In 2024 and 2025 the foundation sued several critics for defamation, including the Tamil magazine *Nakkheeran* and the YouTuber Shyam Meera Singh. In March 2025 Justice Subramonium Prasad of the Delhi High Court ordered, as an interim measure, that Singh's video alleging the exploitation of minors at the ashram be taken down pending trial. None of these matters has produced a finding against Jaggi Vasudev himself.

**Construction and the forest.** The Isha Yoga Center lies in a hill area of the Western Ghats where building needs the approval of the Hill Area Conservation Authority. A November 2012 inspection by the Coimbatore town planning department found sixty foundation buildings put up without prior approval and thirty-four more under construction. In 2017 the Comptroller and Auditor General of India reported that Isha buildings stood in the protected area of the Booluvampatti reserve forest range and that approval was still pending. Environmental groups, the CPI(M), Dalit organisations and tribal residents protested, and an application before the southern bench of the National Green Tribunal alleged harm to an elephant corridor. Isha replied that the centre stands on private land bought from individuals, that the Tamil Nadu forest department had told the courts there was no encroachment, that its campus is nowhere near a notified elephant corridor, and that the Hill Area Conservation Authority approved all its buildings in March 2017.

Separately, on 19 November 2021 the Tamil Nadu Pollution Control Board issued a show-cause notice over buildings put up between 2006 and 2014 without prior environmental clearance. In December 2022 the Madras High Court quashed it, holding that the foundation counted as an educational institution and was covered by a 2014 exemption. The board appealed two years later. On 28 February 2025 a Supreme Court bench of Justices Surya Kant and N. Kotiswar Singh dismissed the appeal, said that when the state comes to court so late <q>we become suspicious</q>, ordered that no coercive steps be taken against the centre, and said its order was not a precedent for regularising illegal buildings. It added that any future expansion must first be approved by the competent authority. Isha told the Court it would comply with the law.

**The river campaigns.** Critics argued that Rally for Rivers and Cauvery Calling offered a simple remedy, tree planting, for complex problems of river flow, and asked how the money was raised. In September 2019 the Coalition for Environmental Justice in India said trees should be planted only where local ecology and need called for them. A public interest petition in the Karnataka High Court, filed in 2019, challenged the collection of public money for Cauvery Calling; the court later turned it into a case on its own motion, and in January 2020 a bench headed by Chief Justice Abhay Oka asked what authority the foundation had to collect money. On 7 September 2021 a bench of Acting Chief Justice Satish Chandra Sharma and Justice Sachin Shankar Magadum dismissed the case, noting that the state said Cauvery Calling was not its project and that no government land was being used. <q>Planting trees on a barren land is not a crime,</q> the court said. Isha's counsel said the foundation is a public charitable trust that publishes its accounts every year.

**Scientific claims.** He often presents yogic lore in the language of science, and scientists and rationalists have challenged some of these statements. In a blog post he said that food cooked before a lunar eclipse deteriorates and that <q>What was nourishing food turns into poison.</q> In February 2018 the journalist Prayas Sutar argued in *Newslaundry* that this had no scientific basis; Isha later published his reply under the title "Eclipse and food: life sense, not internet science." In February 2018, after India moved to ratify the Minamata Convention on mercury, he tweeted that <q>Responsible usage of mercury for medicinal and yogic purposes must be allowed</q>, and he has said he can solidify mercury in his hand at room temperature. The neuroscientist Sumaiya Shaikh, writing for *Alt News*, set out the evidence on mercury's toxicity and pointed out that pure mercury is liquid at room temperature. For Shambhavi he cites measurable benefits, including a claim that after ninety days of practice <q>you are 6.4 years younger</q> at the level of DNA. Published studies of the practice exist, but many are small and some have no control group; a 2022 study of the online course in *Frontiers in Psychology*, for example, followed self-selected participants without a comparison group.

## Descendants

Jaggi Vasudev has no named successor in the public record. His teaching continues through the Isha Foundation's resident monks and volunteers, the teachers it trains for Inner Engineering and hatha yoga, and its centres in India and abroad, including the Isha Institute of Inner-sciences in Tennessee. The foundation says its programmes reach millions of people. His influence also runs well beyond Isha. His short video talks, his Adiyogi figure and his idea of yoga as an inner technology have given a large urban and diaspora audience a way to speak about spiritual life in the vocabulary of science, self-management and national heritage.
