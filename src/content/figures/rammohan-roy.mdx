<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> He wrote his own name in English as Rammohun Roy, and it also appears as Ram Mohan Roy and Rammohan Ray. "Raja" was a title given to him by the Mughal emperor Akbar II, who sent him to England as his envoy.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>North Calcutta, the evening of 20 August 1828. A group of friends and relatives gathers in a rented house belonging to a man known as Feringhi Kamal Bose. There is no image in the room, no lamp waved before a deity and no offering of food. Accounts of the early meetings describe the order of the evening. Two Telugu Brahmins chant passages from the Vedas in Sanskrit. A pandit, Utsavananda Vidyabagish, explains passages from the Upanishads in Bengali so that everyone present can follow. Then the company sings hymns, several written by their host.</p>
<p>The host is Rammohan Roy, in his mid-fifties, a retired revenue official of the East India Company and the most argued-about man in the city. For thirteen years he has been publishing Sanskrit texts with Bengali and English translations, telling orthodox pandits that their own scriptures forbid the image worship they perform, and telling Baptist missionaries that the Trinity has no basis in the Gospels. This evening's gathering, which he calls the Brahmo Sabha, the assembly of those who worship Brahman, is his answer to a question he has been asking since his twenties. What would worship look like if it followed only what reason and the oldest scriptures agree on?</p>
</section>

## Life

Rammohan Roy was born in Radhanagar, a village in the Hooghly district of western Bengal, into a prosperous Brahmin family that had served the Mughal administration. Most sources give 22 May 1772; some give 1774, and historians treat the year as uncertain. His father, Ramkanta Roy, was a Vaishnava, a worshipper of Vishnu. His mother, Tarini Devi, came from a Shakta family devoted to the goddess. Like many Kulin Brahmin boys of his time he was married as a child, more than once. His second wife was the mother of his two sons, Radhaprasad (born 1800) and Ramaprasad (born 1812), and she died in 1824.

Little is firmly known about his schooling. The usual account sends him at about nine to Patna, then a centre of Persian and Islamic learning, to study Persian and Arabic, and then to Benares for Sanskrit. Through Arabic he read Islamic theology and parts of Aristotle's logic and Euclid. A Sanskrit scholar named Nandakumar Vidyalankar taught him in his youth. This man later became a Tantric teacher under the name Hariharananda Tirthaswami and, according to Bengali biographers, initiated Rammohan into Tantric practice.

A short letter published in London in 1833 under his name describes a break with his family at sixteen. "When about the age of sixteen, I composed a manuscript calling in question the validity of the idolatrous system of the Hindoos," it says. The letter goes on to describe years of travel and a return at twenty when his father "recalled me, and restored me to his favour." Later biographers built on it, adding a journey to Tibet to study Buddhism. His first serious biographer, Sophia Dobson Collet, called the letter spurious, and the Tibetan journey rests on hearsay. What can be said is that by his thirties he had read widely in three scriptural languages and had already concluded that worship of images was a human invention.

His first book shows this. The *Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin*, "A Gift to Monotheists," was written in Persian with an Arabic preface and published at Murshidabad around 1804, though the date is uncertain. It argues that belief in one Source of Creation is natural to every human being, and that the particular doctrines of each religion, its miracles, its rules of purity and its priests, grow from custom. The Persian scholars of Calcutta were impressed enough to call him a *zabardast maulavi*, a formidable cleric.

From 1803 to about 1814 he worked for the East India Company in the revenue service, first as a private clerk at Murshidabad and then for a Company officer named John Digby, whom he served for about nine years from 1805, latterly as *dewan*, chief Indian officer of the revenue office, at Rangpur in northern Bengal. At Rangpur he held evening discussions on religion with friends of different faiths, quoting the Qur'an, the Bible and the Vedanta in support of the unity of God, and Hariharananda stayed with him there for a time. Local opposition is said to have made his position uncomfortable.

In 1814 he retired and settled in Calcutta with an income from his landed estates. In 1815 (some sources say 1814) he founded the Atmiya Sabha, the "Friendly Society," a discussion circle of educated Bengalis that met to read Sanskrit texts and sing hymns. In 1815 he published the *Vedanta Grantha*, a Bengali version of the *Brahma Sutras*, the classical digest of Upanishadic teaching, followed by a shorter abridgement, the *Vedantasara*, which he also put into English. Between 1816 and 1819 he published Bengali and English translations of five Upanishads: the Isha, Kena, Katha, Mundaka and Mandukya. These books were among the earliest works of printed Bengali prose, and they were written to persuade. Each preface argued that the texts taught one God beyond all form, and that the image worship practised around him had no warrant in them.

The orthodox replied. A pandit writing as Sankara Sastri attacked his translations in the *Madras Courier*, and Rammohan answered with *A Defence of Hindoo Theism* (1817). Calcutta pandits wrote Bengali rebuttals and he answered them too, in a series of tracts that ran for years. The same scriptural argument carried him into the campaign against *sati*, the burning of widows on their husbands' pyres. Bengali biographers say that in 1811 (some accounts say 1812) the widow of one of his elder brothers died this way, and that he vowed to end the practice; the story is widely repeated and hard to document. His two tracts in the form of a dialogue between an advocate and an opponent of widow-burning appeared in 1818 and 1820. Their method was the method of his religious books. He set out what the lawgivers and the Upanishads actually said and argued that the self-immolation of widows was nowhere required by the texts the orthodox claimed to follow.

Around 1816 the Baptist missionaries at Serampore began to hope he would convert. He studied the Gospels closely, and in 1820 he published *The Precepts of Jesus: The Guide to Peace and Happiness*, an anthology of Jesus's moral teaching that left out the miracles and the passages on his divinity. Joshua Marshman, editor of the Serampore *Friend of India*, attacked it at once, and Rammohan replied with three *Appeals to the Christian Public* (1820, 1821 and 1823), long works of biblical criticism arguing that the Gospels did not teach the Trinity or the atonement. One of the missionaries, William Adam, was won over to his side and became a Unitarian. In 1821 the two founded the Calcutta Unitarian Committee, which Rammohan helped fund. He attended Unitarian services for some years and is reported to have called himself a Hindu Unitarian, though he never joined a church.

The 1820s were his busiest decade. He started a Bengali weekly, *Sambad Kaumudi* (1821), and a Persian one, *Mirat-ul-Akhbar* (1822), and protested the press restrictions of 1823. In December 1823 he wrote to the Governor-General, Lord Amherst, asking that public money go to English and scientific education instead of a new Sanskrit college. In 1826 he set up a Vedanta College of his own. When the Unitarian association became more of a Christian mission, he and his Bengali supporters withdrew their money. In August 1828 he opened the Brahmo Sabha. A trust deed, signed on 8 January 1830, gave it a building on Chitpore Road and set out its purpose in a single long sentence that still governs the Adi Brahmo Samaj.

On 4 December 1829 Lord William Bentinck's government prohibited sati in Bengal. Orthodox Calcutta organised a Dharma Sabha, led by Radhakanta Deb, to petition against the law and appeal to the Privy Council in London. The Mughal emperor Akbar II, by then a pensioner of the Company in Delhi, gave Rammohan the title of Raja and asked him to present his grievances in London. He sailed late in 1830 and reached Liverpool in April 1831. In England he met Jeremy Bentham, William Godwin and Robert Owen, was in the country when the Privy Council upheld the sati ban, and visited France in 1832.

In September 1833 he was in Bristol, where the Unitarian minister Lant Carpenter was among his friends, staying at Stapleton Grove, a house outside the city. He fell ill with fever and died there on 27 September 1833. Most sources give meningitis as the cause. He was buried in the garden of the house in October. In 1843 his body was moved to Arnos Vale Cemetery in Bristol, where a *chhatri*, a domed Indian-style canopy, was raised over the grave with money from his friend Dwarkanath Tagore.

<aside class="note"><strong>Sati.</strong> A widow who burns herself on her husband's funeral pyre, and by extension the practice. The word means "a virtuous woman." The campaign against it in the 1810s and 1820s centred on Bengal.</aside>

## The teaching

Rammohan's teaching can be stated in a sentence from the trust deed of 1830. The building was to be used for "the worship and adoration of the Eternal Unsearchable and Immutable Being who is the Author and Preserver of the Universe." Everything else in his religious writing defends that sentence from two directions: against Hindus who worshipped images and against Christians who worshipped a Trinity.

**One God, known by reason.** In the *Tuhfat* the starting point is reason and human nature. Every people believes in a Source of Creation, and this belief is "an indispensable characteristic of man." What divides them is custom: rules of eating and purity, stories of miracles and the authority of priests. "Falsehood is common to all religions without distinction," he wrote, and custom and training "make the individuals of mankind blind and deaf notwithstanding their having eyes and ears." The book closes with a line of the Persian poet Hafiz urging his readers to injure no one. The tone owes a good deal to the rational theology he read in Arabic and Persian. Some historians, following the philosopher Brajendranath Seal, have connected it to the Mu'tazila, the early Islamic school that made reason a judge of doctrine; others point to Indo-Persian writers closer to his own time. The link is a matter of interpretation.

**One God, taught by the Vedas.** When he turned to Sanskrit, he did something the *Tuhfat* had not. He argued that the oldest Hindu scriptures, read correctly, teach exactly what reason teaches. The Upanishads, he said, describe Brahman as one, formless, beyond the reach of the senses and the source of the world, and the *Brahma Sutras* summarise that teaching. He followed the commentaries of Shankara, the eighth-century teacher of Advaita Vedanta, and relied on them for his readings. "The doctrines of the unity of God are real Hindooism," he wrote in 1817, and the worship of images was a later growth. He kept scripture as an authority. He treated the Upanishads as the core of the Vedas and claimed them for monotheism. Translating them into Bengali let readers who knew no Sanskrit check what was being done in the texts' name, so the translation was itself part of the argument.

**Images as a concession.** He accepted a point found in the texts themselves, that worship of forms is permitted for those who cannot yet grasp the formless. In the *Defence of Hindoo Theism* he wrote that "numerous figures have been invented for the benefit of those who are not possessed of sufficient understanding." He took this to mean that image worship was a lower stage that educated people should leave behind, and he claimed that "many learned Brahmans are perfectly aware, of the absurdity of idol-worship." He also argued that it did moral harm, since the stories told of the gods, and the ritual purity rules attached to them, distracted worshippers from ethical conduct.

**The householder's religion.** He was a married man of property who never took vows of renunciation, and the religion he described suited people like him: worship in the mind, ethical living and kindness to others, open to anyone who could read a translation. The Brahmo Sabha was built for such people. Its trust deed made it a meeting place for "all sorts and descriptions of people without distinction" who behaved in an orderly and devout manner, forbade any "graven image statue or sculpture, carving, painting, picture, portrait or the likeness of anything," and forbade any sacrifice, offering or feasting, and required that nothing worshipped by any other group be "reviled or slightingly or contemptuously spoken of."

**Jesus as a teacher.** His reading of the Gospels applied the same rule. In *The Precepts of Jesus* he kept Jesus's moral teaching and set the miracle stories and doctrinal passages aside, because those were the parts that divided Christians and that no reasoning person could verify. In the *Appeals* he argued at length, using Greek and Hebrew, that Jesus never claimed to be God, that salvation came through following his teaching, and that the Holy Spirit was not a separate person. He did not call Jesus a mere man. He described him as a being with a special mission and a rank "superior even to the angels in heaven." This brought him close to the English and American Unitarians, who read the Bible the same way, and it angered the Baptists, who had expected a convert.

**Where reason and revelation meet.** Put together, these positions give his answer to the question of this part. Reason, he held, shows that there is one Creator, to be known "in his works of nature." Revelation, rightly read, confirms it. Where a scripture appears to teach something reason cannot accept, such as a God with a body, three persons in one God, or a widow's duty to burn, the fault lies with the interpreters and the later accretions. He applied this test to Hinduism, Islam and Christianity, and in each case he found what he was looking for: a pure monotheism at the source, covered over by custom.

I find him most original as a reader. Shankara had already said that Brahman is without form, and Muslim theologians had already made the unity of God the measure of every belief. What Rammohan added was a procedure that anyone could follow: take the oldest text, translate it, print it, and ask whether the practice in front of you is actually in it. Dayananda Saraswati would use the same procedure on the Vedic hymns, Debendranath Tagore would turn it against the Vedas themselves, and Vivekananda would build on the Vedanta that Rammohan had first made public.

## Lineage

Rammohan sits at the root of the reform branch of the tree. He had teachers, but no single guru who defines him, and he left an institution in place of a line of disciples.

<MiniTree id="rammohan-roy" />

The documented edges are textual. He drew directly on the Upanishads and on Shankara's Advaita Vedanta, which he translated and cited; on the Gospels and Unitarian criticism, through his work with William Adam from 1821; and on Islamic and Persian learning, which shaped the *Tuhfat*. The link to Hariharananda Tirthaswami rests on biographers' accounts that Hariharananda taught him Sanskrit and initiated him into Tantric practice, and it is shown as testimony. Forward from him the line is institutional. Ramchandra Vidyabagish, the Sabha's first secretary, kept the weekly services going after 1833 and in 1843 formally initiated Debendranath Tagore and about twenty young men into the Sabha. Debendranath reorganised it as the Brahmo Samaj, and through him and Keshab Chandra Sen it passed to the rest of Part 1.

## The practice

Rammohan taught no meditation technique and gave no initiations. The practice he established was congregational worship, as it ran at the Brahmo Sabha in its early years.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Recitation.</strong> Passages of the Vedas chanted in Sanskrit by Brahmin reciters.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Exposition.</strong> A pandit reads and explains Upanishad passages in Bengali, so that the meaning reaches people who know no Sanskrit.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hymns.</strong> The congregation sings <i>brahmasangit</i>, devotional songs addressed to the formless God, many composed by Rammohan and his friends.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Contemplation in daily life.</strong> Outside the assembly, worship consists of reflecting on the one God and living ethically as a householder, with no images, offerings or priestly rites.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

He died in 1833, before photography and sound recording, so no image from life other than painted and drawn portraits survives, and there are no recordings. His voice survives in print: the Persian *Tuhfat*, the Bengali and English translations and tracts, and the English polemics, gathered in several collected editions. An oil portrait of him by Henry Perronet Briggs, painted in England around 1832, is in the Bristol Museum and Art Gallery.

## Critiques and questions

**The orthodox reply.** Calcutta pandits answered his translations from 1817 onward, arguing that he had misread the Vedanta, that Shankara himself accepted image worship and ritual for those still in the world, and that a householder who ignored caste rules had no standing to interpret the texts. The Dharma Sabha of 1830 was founded largely in opposition to his campaign against sati. Many Brahmin leaders of his day regarded his attacks on sects and image worship as a rejection of Hinduism itself.

**The missionaries' reply.** Joshua Marshman and the Serampore Baptists argued in 1820 and after that the *Precepts* cut the heart out of the Gospels by removing the miracles and the doctrine of atonement. To them his Unitarian reading was a heresy, and his claim to find pure monotheism in the Upanishads was special pleading.

**Was he too Western?** In April 1921 Gandhi told an audience in Orissa that Rammohan and Tilak would have been "far greater men had they not the contagion of English learning," and called them "pygmies" beside Chaitanya, Shankara, Kabir and Nanak. Rabindranath Tagore wrote to C.F. Andrews on 10 May 1921, "I strongly protest against Mahatma Gandhi's depreciation of such great personalities of Modern India as Rammohan Roy," arguing that Rammohan continued the work of Nanak and Kabir and that his openness to the West grew from a thoroughly Eastern education. Later historians have argued the same question in other terms. Some, such as Sumit Sarkar in the 1970s, questioned how far he broke with the colonial and landed order he belonged to; others, such as Brian Hatcher, have asked whether calling him a reformer obscures how much the Brahmo Sabha resembled older Hindu religious communities.

**What is documented.** Several famous parts of his story rest on weak evidence. The autobiographical letter of 1833 was called spurious by his biographer Collet. The Tibet journey is hearsay. The account of his sister-in-law's death on a pyre in 1811 or 1812 is repeated by Bengali biographers but hard to document. A claim that he helped compose the *Mahanirvana Tantra*, a Tantric text he admired, is considered highly improbable by the legal historian J. Duncan M. Derrett.

## Descendants

The Brahmo Sabha nearly faded after his death and was revived by Debendranath Tagore, whose Tattvabodhini Sabha (1839) and covenant of 1843 turned it into the Brahmo Samaj. Debendranath later gave up the claim that the Vedas were infallible, a step Rammohan had not taken, and Keshab Chandra Sen carried the Samaj toward a universal church drawing on all religions. Through the Tagore family, the movement shaped Rabindranath's religious thought. Dayananda Saraswati, who met Keshab Chandra Sen and other Brahmo leaders in Calcutta between December 1872 and April 1873, took the opposite path from the same starting point and made the Vedic hymns themselves the infallible source. The Neo-Vedanta of Vivekananda, who attended the Brahmo Samaj as a young man, presented the Upanishads to the world as Rammohan had first presented them in English.
