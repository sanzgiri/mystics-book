<aside class="note"><strong>Two Krishnamurtis.</strong> This chapter is about Jiddu Krishnamurti, usually called J. Krishnamurti or simply K. U.G. Krishnamurti, the next profile, was no relation, though their lives crossed.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>On 3 August 1929, the opening day of the annual Star Camp at Ommen in the Netherlands, a thirty-four-year-old Indian man stood up to address about three thousand members of the Order of the Star, the organisation that had been founded eighteen years earlier to prepare the world for his arrival as its spiritual teacher. Its members had come from many countries. The estate around them, Castle Eerde, had been given for the work by a Dutch baron, Philip van Pallandt.</p>
<p>He told them he was dissolving it. The published text of the talk records him saying, <q>I maintain that Truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect.</q> A truth that is limitless, he went on, cannot be organised, and no organisation can lead anyone to it. He added, <q>The moment you follow someone you cease to follow Truth.</q></p>
<p>Over the following years the money and property given to the Order were returned, and in 1931 Castle Eerde went back to van Pallandt. Krishnamurti went on speaking in public until a few weeks before his death in 1986, and from that morning on he refused to be anyone's guru.</p>
</section>

## Life

Jiddu Krishnamurti was born in Madanapalle, a market town in what is now Andhra Pradesh, into a Telugu Brahmin family. The date usually given, 11 May 1895, comes from his biographer Mary Lutyens. The researcher Christine Williams found that the registrations are unreliable and that claimed dates run from 4 May 1895 to 25 May 1896. His father, Jiddu Narianiah (also spelt Narayaniah), worked in the revenue administration of the British government. His mother, Sanjeevamma, bore eleven children, of whom six survived childhood. She died when Krishnamurti was about ten. He was a dreamy, sickly boy who did badly at school and was often beaten for it.

His father was a Theosophist. The Theosophical Society had been founded in New York in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky and Henry Steel Olcott and had moved its headquarters to Adyar, on the edge of Madras, in 1882. It taught that all religions share a hidden core of wisdom, preserved by a brotherhood of perfected beings called the Masters, and by the early 1900s its leaders expected a World Teacher to appear soon, as the Christ and the Buddha had appeared before. After retiring, Narianiah took a clerk's job at Adyar and moved his sons there in January 1909.

<aside class="note"><strong>Theosophy</strong> ("divine wisdom") was a nineteenth-century movement that blended Western esotericism with Hindu and Buddhist ideas. It did a good deal to make Indian religion respectable among educated Westerners and Indians alike. Its doctrine of a coming World Teacher was the frame for Krishnamurti's whole early life.</aside>

In 1909, Charles Webster Leadbeater, a former Anglican curate who claimed clairvoyant powers and was one of the Society's leading figures, noticed the boy among a group of children on the Society's beach by the Adyar river. Most accounts place this in April or May 1909. By Leadbeater's account, the boy had an unusually selfless aura. Leadbeater began investigating what he took to be the boy's past lives, and in letters of late 1909 he refers to him by the name Alcyone. He and the Society's president, Annie Besant, came to believe that this unwashed, thin, apparently slow child was to be the vehicle of the World Teacher.

The Society took over Krishnamurti and his younger brother Nityananda, known as Nitya, who was devoted to him. The boys were bathed, dressed, tutored in English and trained in Theosophical discipline. A short book of spiritual advice, *At the Feet of the Master*, appeared in 1910 under the name Alcyone. In 1911 the Society founded the Order of the Star in the East, with Krishnamurti as its head, to prepare the world for the Teacher's coming. Besant took the brothers to England that year.

Their father had signed the boys' guardianship over to Besant in March 1910, and he soon regretted it. In July 1912 he revoked the arrangement and sued to recover custody. In April 1913 Justice Bakewell of the Madras High Court ruled for him and ordered the boys returned, and an appeal bench of the same court upheld the order on 29 October 1913. Besant appealed to the Privy Council in London, which gave judgment on 25 May 1914. It agreed that a father is the natural guardian of his children and that his consent to someone else's guardianship can be revoked, but it set aside the Indian order and dismissed the suit, because the boys were living in England, did not wish to return to India and could not lawfully be sent back without their consent. Their father was left free to apply to the English courts, and the brothers stayed with Besant. Krishnamurti grew up in England, failed the university entrance examinations several times, and became a well-dressed young man with a taste for good cars and good tailoring.

In 1922 the brothers went to Ojai, a valley in California about eighty miles from Los Angeles, because its dry air was thought good for Nitya, who had tuberculosis. On 17 August Krishnamurti began to suffer severe pain at the nape of his neck. On 19 August, by the accounts of those present (Nitya, a young American woman named Rosalind Williams who was helping look after them, and an older Theosophist, A.P. Warrington), he passed through what he called <q>the first most extraordinary experience</q>, in which he felt at one with everything around him. Part of it took place under a pepper tree near the cottage. In his own written account he said he had <q>touched compassion which heals all sorrow and suffering; it is not for myself, but for the world.</q> The pain, fainting and episodes of altered consciousness returned at intervals for years. His circle called them simply "the process."

Krishnamurti had come to believe that Nitya would live, because the Masters needed him for the mission. Nitya died in Ojai on 13 November 1925, aged twenty-seven, while Krishnamurti was at sea on his way to India; he heard the news by telegram at Port Said. By Lutyens's account, the death shook his trust in the Masters, and those near him noticed a change after it. Over the next four years he spoke less of the Masters and more of a truth each person must find alone. In Paris in 1927, according to the Krishnamurti Foundation of America's chronology, he said the Masters were only incidents. In August 1929 at Ommen he dissolved the Order. The same chronology records Leadbeater saying at Adyar that year that the Coming had gone wrong.

He resigned from the Theosophical Society in 1930. Besant, by all accounts, went on loving him until her death in 1933. He made his home in Ojai with Rosalind Williams, who had married his friend and business manager Desikacharya Rajagopal in 1927, and their daughter Radha. From the early 1930s Rajagopal ran the publishing side of his work, first through the Star Publishing Trust and later through Krishnamurti Writings Inc., known as KWINC. During the Second World War Krishnamurti gave no public talks between 1940 and 1944. He lived quietly on the small farm at Arya Vihara, whose surplus went to relief in Europe, and he later described it as a period with no challenge and no demand. He met the novelist Aldous Huxley in 1938, and they became close friends.

From the late 1940s he settled into the pattern of the rest of his life. He travelled each year between India, Europe and California, speaking to large audiences, holding smaller discussions and meeting people privately. *The First and Last Freedom*, edited from his talks, was published by Harper in 1954 with Huxley's encouragement and reached a wide public. *Commentaries on Living* followed in three volumes. From June 1961 to early 1962 he kept a private journal, later published as *Krishnamurti's Notebook*, which describes, day after day, a state he called the benediction, the immensity or the otherness. It also records the return of the painful "process," which Lutyens, in her foreword, says should not be confused with that state. Around 1959 the physicist David Bohm and his wife Saral found *The First and Last Freedom* in a public library in Bristol, and Bohm was struck by its account of the observer and the observed. He wrote to Krishnamurti and met him in London about 1960 or 1961. He then attended most of his talks in Europe and California, and the two men held recorded conversations from 1965 into the 1980s.

He cared most about schools. Rishi Valley School, near his birthplace, dates from 1926, when land for it began to be acquired, and other schools followed at Rajghat in Varanasi, at Brockwood Park in Hampshire (1969) and at Oak Grove in Ojai. In the late 1960s his work was reorganised into new foundations: the Krishnamurti Foundation Trust in England in 1968, the Krishnamurti Foundation of America in 1969 and the Krishnamurti Foundation India, based in Madras. The move followed a falling-out with Rajagopal, who controlled KWINC, its copyrights, its funds and its property. In 1971 Krishnamurti's side sued Rajagopal and KWINC to recover them, and the California Attorney General's office, which oversees charities in the state, took part in the case. A settlement approved by the court in December 1974 returned the copyrights and some of the property, including Pine Cottage, to Krishnamurti's side, and the American foundation built its Ojai offices and archives on that land. Disputes over how the settlement was carried out, and over the archives, continued. In May 1983 Rajagopal and his associates countersued Krishnamurti and the foundation's trustees for breach of the settlement and slander. The remaining claims were settled in 1986, shortly after Krishnamurti's death.

In 1984 and 1985 he spoke to invited audiences at the United Nations in New York. He gave his last public talk in Madras on 4 January 1986, already ill, and returned to Ojai, where pancreatic cancer was diagnosed. In a statement tape-recorded on 7 February 1986 he said that an immense energy and intelligence had gone through his body, and that others might come into touch with it if they lived the teachings. <q>But nobody has done it. Nobody. And so that's that.</q> He died at Pine Cottage in Ojai ten minutes after midnight on 17 February 1986. His ashes were divided between Ojai, England and India.

## The teaching

Krishnamurti taught that every human being is conditioned by the past, by culture, religion, nation, family and personal memory, and that this conditioning is the root of conflict both inside a person and between people. He held that a free mind cannot be reached gradually by following a method, a teacher or a tradition, because each of these is itself more conditioning. What can happen is a direct seeing of how one's own mind works, and he said that this seeing brings its own change.

**Against authority.** The 1929 speech states the theme he never changed. In spiritual matters he rejected every authority, including scripture, the guru, the priest, the organisation and the expert, and his own authority with them. He described his role as a mirror in which listeners could look at themselves, and he asked audiences not to treat what he said as something to believe. He opened many talks by saying that speaker and listener were going to inquire together.

**Thought and time.** He described thought as the response of memory, and so as always old. The self, the "me," was in his view a bundle of memories, images and reactions held together by thought. Psychological time, the habit of saying "I will become better, calmer, freer later," was for him one of thought's tricks. It postpones a change that could only take place now, if it takes place at all. He accepted thought as necessary for practical life, for building a bridge or catching a train, and said trouble begins when it is used to build up the self.

**The observer and the observed.** This is the idea that drew David Bohm. When a person is angry and tries to control the anger, there seem to be two things, the anger and an observer who judges it. Krishnamurti argued that the observer is made of the same past as the anger. The controller and the controlled are one movement of thought, and the conflict between them keeps the movement going. When this is seen clearly, he said, the division ends, and with it the struggle that keeps the anger alive.

**Choiceless awareness.** He called his alternative choiceless awareness, which means watching thoughts, feelings and reactions as they arise, without condemning, approving or trying to change them. *The First and Last Freedom* describes it as observation without condemnation or justification. He said this attention could go on in relationship, at work and in conversation, and that relationship is where the self shows itself most clearly. He called it meditation, and he distrusted every technique that went under that name, mantras and breathing exercises included, because in his view they quieted the mind by conditioning it.

**Something beyond thought.** Although he spoke mostly as a psychologist of everyday life, his talks and the *Notebook* return again and again to what he called the sacred, the immeasurable or the otherness. He said it could not be sought, because seeking is thought's activity, and it came when the mind was completely silent. Readers who know him only as a rationalist are often surprised by these passages.

<aside class="note"><strong>K's vocabulary.</strong> He used ordinary English words, and he used them with special weight: <i>conditioning</i> (everything the past has put into the mind), <i>the me</i> (the self as a construct of thought), <i>psychological time</i> (becoming), <i>choiceless awareness</i>, <i>the observer is the observed</i>. He avoided Sanskrit almost entirely.</aside>

### The dialogues with Bohm

The conversations with Bohm, held from the 1960s to the 1980s and partly published as *Truth and Actuality* (1977), *The Ending of Time* (1985) and *The Future of Humanity* (1986), are the most sustained attempt to test his ideas against another trained mind. *The Ending of Time* records thirteen conversations from 1980, eight at Ojai in April and five at Brockwood Park in June. Bohm, a quantum physicist who had discussed the foundations of quantum theory with Einstein at Princeton, found in Krishnamurti's account of fragmented thought a parallel to his own ideas about wholeness in physics. According to Bohm's biographer F. David Peat, the friendship was strained in Krishnamurti's last years, after Bohm began to question the guru-like image that grew up around him and Krishnamurti challenged Bohm's dependence on others. They went on meeting, but Peat says their conversations never regained their earlier depth. Bohm's later work on group "dialogue" carried something of their conversations into management training and education.

I find Krishnamurti hard to place on a lineage tree, which is the point of putting him in this part. He was raised by an institution to be a messiah and spent his life saying that no one could be one. What he said about the observer and the observed overlaps with Advaita, Buddhism and Western phenomenology, but he arrived at it, or presented it, without borrowing their vocabulary, and he cut away the practices and the guru that went with them. Whether that is possible, or whether he simply became a guru of a new kind, is a question that runs through the rest of this chapter.

## Lineage

Krishnamurti's tree has a strong line above him and a deliberately broken one below. Above him is the Theosophical Society, which picked him out, educated him and built an organisation around him, along with the two people who did most of it, Leadbeater and Besant. He reacted against all three, but he cannot be understood without them. Below him there is no succession. He warned associates against presenting themselves as his spokesmen or successors, and he left foundations charged with preserving the recordings and texts and running the schools.

The lines that run out from him are lines of influence: to David Bohm; to Aldous Huxley; to Maurice Frydman, who knew him well before he recorded Nisargadatta's talks; and to **U.G. Krishnamurti**, who listened to him for years, broke with him and spent the rest of his life arguing against him.

## The practice

He refused to give a method and warned that any method would become another habit. What he asked of listeners, in talk after talk, can still be set out as a sequence of things to look at.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Watch without judging.</strong> Notice a thought, feeling or reaction as it arises, without condemning it, justifying it or trying to change it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Notice the watcher.</strong> See whether the one who judges the anger or the fear is itself made of the same memory and conditioning as what it judges.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Look in relationship.</strong> Pay this attention at work, at home and in conversation, where he said the self shows itself most clearly.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Drop the goal.</strong> Leave aside the wish to become calmer or freer later, which he regarded as psychological time.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Leave room for silence.</strong> Let the mind be quiet without forcing it, since he held that a silence produced by technique is itself conditioned.</div></li>
</ol>

The Krishnamurti Foundation Trust says it has digitised about 600 full-length videos and aims to publish a selection of around 2,500 audio recordings, all free and without advertising, with volunteer subtitles in about forty languages. ## Critiques and questions

**A guru against gurus.** The most common objection is that he became what he condemned. He spoke from a raised platform to thousands of people a year for half a century, and many listeners treated him with the reverence they would give a guru. The German writer Peter Michel argued that real dialogue was conspicuously lacking in the talks, and that the invitations to look into things together were rhetorical introductions to a monologue. Lutyens wrote that what kept many people coming was the prospect of an almost instantaneous psychological transformation. Krishnamurti himself said in his last recorded statement that nobody had lived the teaching.

**The Theosophical inheritance.** Critics and sympathisers alike have asked how much of the "process" and of the *Notebook*'s otherness came out of the Theosophical world of Masters and initiations he was raised in. He spoke of the process as something outside his control, believed it necessary to his development, and did not treat it as an illness. Commentators have suggested kundalini awakening, epilepsy or grief for his mother as explanations.

**Radha Rajagopal Sloss's book.** In May 1991 Bloomsbury in London published *Lives in the Shadow with J. Krishnamurti* by Radha Rajagopal Sloss, born in 1931, the daughter of Rosalind and D. Rajagopal, who grew up close to Krishnamurti in Ojai. An American edition followed from Addison-Wesley in 1993. Her central claim is that Krishnamurti and her mother had a secret sexual relationship that began in 1932 and lasted about twenty-five years, while Rosalind was married to Rajagopal. She also tells the story of the quarrel and the lawsuits from her family's side and gives a critical portrait of Krishnamurti's character. In an interview with Helen Tworkov in *Tricycle* (Winter 1991) she said the deception troubled her more than the relationship itself: <q>it wasn't the affair that was so upsetting; it was all the lying.</q> The book was reviewed favourably in *The Times*, the *London Review of Books* and the *Times Literary Supplement*, and it distressed many of Krishnamurti's admirers. The Krishnamurti Foundation of America replied in 1995 with a published statement on the book. According to that statement, the trustees of the foundations already knew of the relationship, because it had come up in the legal disputes with the Rajagopals in the 1970s and early 1980s. In 1996 the Foundation published *Krishnamurti and the Rajagopals* by Mary Lutyens, his authorised biographer and a friend of many years. Lutyens acknowledged the relationship, but she said the book contained many misstatements of fact and false inferences, and she disputed Sloss's portrait of Krishnamurti and her account of the Rajagopals' conduct. Krishnamurti himself never confirmed the relationship and never discussed it in public. For many readers the book raised the question of how his teaching on honesty in relationship squared with a long concealed one.

**Can anyone use it?** The teaching offers no method, which its admirers count as its strength. Critics within the traditions he rejected, Advaitins and Buddhists among them, argue that without practice, teacher and community, most people simply cannot do what he described, and that his own long Theosophical training was a preparation he denied to others.

## Descendants

He named no successor, and none has emerged. His work continues through the foundations in England, America, India and Latin America and through the schools: Rishi Valley, Rajghat, Brockwood Park, Oak Grove and others in India. His influence runs wider than any organisation. Huxley, Bohm, the writer Henry Miller and many teachers of mindfulness and inquiry drew on him. In India his talks reached a large educated audience who found in them a modern, secular-sounding spirituality. Among the people who learned from him and turned against him, the most striking is **U.G. Krishnamurti**, whose profile follows. Maurice Frydman's friendship with him also links this part to Nisargadatta and Part 3.
