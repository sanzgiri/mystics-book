<aside class="note"><strong>Two Krishnamurtis.</strong> This chapter is about Jiddu Krishnamurti, usually called J. Krishnamurti or simply K. U.G. Krishnamurti, the next profile, was no relation, though their lives crossed.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>On the morning of 3 August 1929, at the annual Star Camp at Ommen in the Netherlands, a thirty-four-year-old Indian man stood up to address the Order of the Star, the organisation that had been founded eighteen years earlier to prepare the world for his arrival as its spiritual teacher. Its members had come from many countries. The estate around them, Castle Eerde, had been given to the Order by a Dutch baron.</p>
<p>He told them he was dissolving it. The published text of the talk records him saying, <q>I maintain that Truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect.</q> A truth that is limitless, he went on, cannot be organised, and no organisation can lead anyone to it. He added, <q>The moment you follow someone you cease to follow Truth.</q></p>
<p>The money, the land and the membership went back to their donors over the following years. Krishnamurti went on speaking in public until a few weeks before his death in 1986, and from that morning on he refused to be anyone's guru.</p>
</section>

## Life

Jiddu Krishnamurti was born in Madanapalle, a market town in what is now Andhra Pradesh, into a Telugu Brahmin family. The date usually given, 11 May 1895, comes from his biographer Mary Lutyens. The researcher Christine Williams found that the registrations are unreliable and that claimed dates run from 4 May 1895 to 25 May 1896. His father, Jiddu Narianiah (also spelt Narayaniah), worked in the revenue administration of the British government. His mother, Sanjeevamma, bore eleven children, of whom six survived childhood. She died when Krishnamurti was about ten. He was a dreamy, sickly boy who did badly at school and was often beaten for it.

His father was a Theosophist. The Theosophical Society had been founded in New York in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky and Henry Steel Olcott and had moved its headquarters to Adyar, on the edge of Madras, in 1882. It taught that all religions share a hidden core of wisdom, preserved by a brotherhood of perfected beings called the Masters, and by the early 1900s its leaders expected a World Teacher to appear soon, as the Christ and the Buddha had appeared before. After retiring, Narianiah took a clerk's job at Adyar and moved his sons there in January 1909.

<aside class="note"><strong>Theosophy</strong> ("divine wisdom") was a nineteenth-century movement that blended Western esotericism with Hindu and Buddhist ideas. It did a good deal to make Indian religion respectable among educated Westerners and Indians alike. Its doctrine of a coming World Teacher was the frame for Krishnamurti's whole early life.</aside>

In 1909, Charles Webster Leadbeater, a former Anglican curate who claimed clairvoyant powers and was one of the Society's leading figures, noticed the boy among a group of children on the Society's beach by the Adyar river. Most accounts place this in April or May; one account of the later lawsuit places it in July. By Leadbeater's account, the boy had an unusually selfless aura. Leadbeater began investigating what he took to be the boy's past lives, and in letters of late 1909 he refers to him by the name Alcyone. He and the Society's president, Annie Besant, came to believe that this unwashed, thin, apparently slow child was to be the vehicle of the World Teacher.

The Society took over Krishnamurti and his younger brother Nityananda, known as Nitya, who was devoted to him. The boys were bathed, dressed, tutored in English and trained in Theosophical discipline. A short book of spiritual advice, *At the Feet of the Master*, appeared in 1910 under the name Alcyone. In 1911 the Society founded the Order of the Star in the East, with Krishnamurti as its head, to prepare the world for the Teacher's coming. Besant took the brothers to England that year.

Their father soon regretted the arrangement. In 1912 he sued Besant to recover custody of his sons. The Madras High Court ruled for him in April 1913. Besant appealed to the Privy Council in London, and after a retrial and appeal the boys stayed with her. The Privy Council held that a father's rights could not be signed away by agreement, but the brothers were by then old enough to choose, and they chose Besant. The sources summarise the sequence of hearings differently, though they agree on the outcome. Krishnamurti grew up in England, failed the university entrance examinations several times, and became a well-dressed young man with a taste for good cars and good tailoring.

In 1922 the brothers went to Ojai, a valley in California about eighty miles from Los Angeles, because its dry air was thought good for Nitya, who had tuberculosis. On 17 August Krishnamurti began to suffer severe pain at the nape of his neck. On 19 August, by the accounts of those present (Nitya, a young American woman named Rosalind Williams who was helping look after them, and an older Theosophist, A.P. Warrington), he passed through what he called <q>the first most extraordinary experience</q>, in which he felt at one with everything around him. Part of it took place under a pepper tree near the cottage. In his own written account he said he had <q>touched compassion which heals all sorrow and suffering; it is not for myself, but for the world.</q> The pain, fainting and episodes of altered consciousness returned at intervals for years. His circle called them simply "the process."

Leadbeater and Besant assured him that Nitya would live, because he was needed for the mission. Nitya died in Ojai on 13 November 1925, aged twenty-seven. By Lutyens's account, the death shook Krishnamurti's trust in the Masters who had supposedly promised it, and those near him noticed a change after it. Over the next four years he spoke less of the Masters and more of a truth each person must find alone. In Paris in 1927, according to the Krishnamurti Foundation of America's chronology, he said the Masters were only incidents. At the Society's convention at Adyar in 1929 Leadbeater was heard to say that the Coming had gone wrong. In August that year at Ommen he dissolved the Order.

He resigned from the Theosophical Society, in 1930 by most accounts; Lutyens treated the last tie as cut only when Besant died in 1933. Besant, by all accounts, went on loving him. He made his home in Ojai with Rosalind Williams, who had married his friend and business manager Desikacharya Rajagopal in 1927, and their daughter Radha. From 1930 Rajagopal ran the publishing side of his work, first through the Star Publishing Trust and from 1944 through Krishnamurti Writings Inc. During the Second World War Krishnamurti gave no public talks between 1940 and 1944. He lived quietly on the small farm at Arya Vihara, whose surplus went to relief in Europe, and he later described it as a period with no challenge and no demand. He met the novelist Aldous Huxley in 1938, and they became close friends.

From the late 1940s he settled into the pattern of the rest of his life. He travelled each year between India, Europe and California, speaking to large audiences, holding smaller discussions and meeting people privately. *The First and Last Freedom*, edited from his talks, was published by Harper in 1954 with Huxley's encouragement and reached a wide public. *Commentaries on Living* followed in three volumes. From 1961 to 1962 he kept a private journal, later published as *Krishnamurti's Notebook*, which describes, day after day, what he called <q>that strong feeling of otherness</q>, a presence he linked to the old "process." In 1961 the physicist David Bohm read *The First and Last Freedom* and was struck by its account of the observer and the observed. Soon after, he began attending Krishnamurti's talks, and the two men held recorded conversations for about two decades.

He cared most about schools. Rishi Valley School, near his birthplace, was founded in 1928, and other schools followed at Rajghat in Varanasi, at Brockwood Park in Hampshire (1969) and at Oak Grove in Ojai. In the late 1960s his work was reorganised into new foundations: the Krishnamurti Foundation Trust in England in 1968, the Krishnamurti Foundation of America in 1969 and the Krishnamurti Foundation India, based in Madras. The move followed a falling-out with Rajagopal, who controlled the older organisation, its copyrights and its property. Krishnamurti and his supporters sued in 1971 to recover them. The litigation ran for fifteen years and was settled in 1986, shortly after his death.

In the 1980s he spoke twice at the United Nations in New York, in 1984 and 1985. Late in 1985 he became ill in India and returned to Ojai, where pancreatic cancer was diagnosed. In a recorded statement a few days before his death he said that an immense energy and intelligence had gone through his body, that no one had understood it and lived it, and, of his followers, <q>But nobody has done it. Nobody. And so that's that.</q> He died at Pine Cottage in Ojai shortly after midnight on 17 February 1986. His ashes were divided between Ojai, England and India, where they were placed in the Ganga at Varanasi and Gangotri and in the sea off Adyar.

## The teaching

Krishnamurti taught that every human being is conditioned by the past, by culture, religion, nation, family and personal memory, and that this conditioning is the root of conflict both inside a person and between people. He held that a free mind cannot be reached gradually by following a method, a teacher or a tradition, because each of these is itself more conditioning. What can happen is a direct seeing of how one's own mind works, and he said that this seeing brings its own change.

**Against authority.** The 1929 speech states the theme he never changed. In spiritual matters he rejected every authority, including scripture, the guru, the priest, the organisation and the expert, and his own authority with them. He described his role as a mirror in which listeners could look at themselves, and he asked audiences not to treat what he said as something to believe. He opened many talks by saying that speaker and listener were going to inquire together.

**Thought and time.** He described thought as the response of memory, and so as always old. The self, the "me," was in his view a bundle of memories, images and reactions held together by thought. Psychological time, the habit of saying "I will become better, calmer, freer later," was for him one of thought's tricks. It postpones a change that could only take place now, if it takes place at all. He accepted thought as necessary for practical life, for building a bridge or catching a train, and said trouble begins when it is used to build up the self.

**The observer and the observed.** This is the idea that drew David Bohm. When a person is angry and tries to control the anger, there seem to be two things, the anger and an observer who judges it. Krishnamurti argued that the observer is made of the same past as the anger. The controller and the controlled are one movement of thought, and the conflict between them keeps the movement going. When this is seen clearly, he said, the division ends, and with it the struggle that keeps the anger alive.

**Choiceless awareness.** He called his alternative choiceless awareness, which means watching thoughts, feelings and reactions as they arise, without condemning, approving or trying to change them. *The First and Last Freedom* puts it in plain words: <q>Observe the self in operation, learn about it, watch it, be aware of it.</q> He said this attention could go on in relationship, at work and in conversation, and that relationship is where the self shows itself most clearly. He called it meditation, and he distrusted every technique that went under that name, mantras and breathing exercises included, because in his view they quieted the mind by conditioning it.

**Something beyond thought.** Although he spoke mostly as a psychologist of everyday life, his talks and the *Notebook* return again and again to what he called the sacred, the immeasurable or the otherness. He said it could not be sought, because seeking is thought's activity, and it came when the mind was completely silent. Readers who know him only as a rationalist are often surprised by these passages.

<aside class="note"><strong>K's vocabulary.</strong> He used ordinary English words, and he used them with special weight: <i>conditioning</i> (everything the past has put into the mind), <i>the me</i> (the self as a construct of thought), <i>psychological time</i> (becoming), <i>choiceless awareness</i>, <i>the observer is the observed</i>. He avoided Sanskrit almost entirely.</aside>

### The dialogues with Bohm

The conversations with Bohm, held from the 1960s to the 1980s and partly published as *Truth and Actuality* (1977), *The Ending of Time* (1985) and *The Future of Humanity* (1986), are the most sustained attempt to test his ideas against another trained mind. *The Ending of Time* records thirteen conversations from 1980, eight at Ojai in April and five at Brockwood Park in June. Bohm, a quantum physicist who had worked with Einstein, found in Krishnamurti's account of fragmented thought a parallel to his own ideas about wholeness in physics. The two men grew apart in Krishnamurti's last years, though they remained friends. Bohm's later work on group "dialogue" carried something of their conversations into management training and education.

I find Krishnamurti hard to place on a lineage tree, which is the point of putting him in this part. He was raised by an institution to be a messiah and spent his life saying that no one could be one. What he said about the observer and the observed overlaps with Advaita, Buddhism and Western phenomenology, but he arrived at it, or presented it, without borrowing their vocabulary, and he cut away the practices and the guru that went with them. Whether that is possible, or whether he simply became a guru of a new kind, is a question that runs through the rest of this chapter.

## Lineage

Krishnamurti's tree has a strong line above him and a deliberately broken one below. Above him is the Theosophical Society, which picked him out, educated him and built an organisation around him, along with the two people who did most of it, Leadbeater and Besant. He reacted against all three, but he cannot be understood without them. Below him there is no succession. He warned associates against presenting themselves as his spokesmen or successors, and he left foundations charged with preserving the recordings and texts and running the schools.

The lines that run out from him are lines of influence: to David Bohm; to Aldous Huxley; to Maurice Frydman, who knew him well before he recorded Nisargadatta's talks; and to **U.G. Krishnamurti**, who listened to him for years, broke with him and spent the rest of his life arguing against him.

## In his own voice

Krishnamurti is one of the best-recorded figures in this book. Audio recordings of his talks survive from 1949 and video from 1966. The Krishnamurti Foundation Trust says it has digitised about 600 full-length videos and aims to publish a selection of around 2,500 audio recordings, all free and without advertising, with volunteer subtitles in about forty languages. The Krishnamurti Foundation of America holds a large archive in Ojai and publishes an illustrated chronology of his life.

## Critiques and questions

**A guru against gurus.** The most common objection is that he became what he condemned. He spoke from a raised platform to thousands of people a year for half a century, and many listeners treated him with the reverence they would give a guru. The German writer Peter Michel argued that his invitations to inquire together were often rhetorical introductions to a monologue. Lutyens noted that many people came hoping for an instant psychological transformation, a different motive from his own. Krishnamurti himself acknowledged near the end that he knew of no one who had been transformed by the teaching.

**The Theosophical inheritance.** Critics and sympathisers alike have asked how much of the "process" and of the *Notebook*'s otherness came out of the Theosophical world of Masters and initiations he was raised in. He spoke of the process as something outside his control and refused to explain it. Radha Sloss suggested that some of the episodes may have been shaped, perhaps unconsciously, by what those around him expected.

**Radha Rajagopal Sloss's book.** In 1991 Bloomsbury published *Lives in the Shadow with J. Krishnamurti* by Radha Rajagopal Sloss, the daughter of Rosalind and D. Rajagopal, who had grown up in Krishnamurti's household in Ojai. Her central claim is that Krishnamurti and her mother had a secret sexual relationship that began in 1932 and lasted about twenty-five years, while Rosalind was married to Rajagopal. In a 1993 interview with *Tricycle* she said the deception troubled her more than the relationship itself: <q>it wasn't the affair that was so upsetting; it was all the lying.</q> The book also describes the falling-out and lawsuits from her family's side. Several reviewers in Britain received it favourably, and it upset many of Krishnamurti's admirers. The Krishnamurti Foundation of America replied in a pamphlet in 1995. In 1996 it published *Krishnamurti and the Rajagopals* by Mary Lutyens, his authorised biographer, who disputed much of Sloss's account, particularly her portrait of Krishnamurti's character and of the Rajagopals' role. Lutyens accepted that there had been a relationship and noted that Krishnamurti never confirmed it himself. The affair had already come up during the litigation of the 1970s and early 1980s, and the foundations' trustees knew of it. Sloss's book raised a question that the foundations' response did not settle, whether a man who taught complete honesty in relationship had lived a long double life. Krishnamurti never claimed to be celibate, and he never discussed the matter in public.

**Can anyone use it?** The teaching offers no method, which its admirers count as its strength. Critics within the traditions he rejected, Advaitins and Buddhists among them, argue that without practice, teacher and community, most people simply cannot do what he described, and that his own long Theosophical training was a preparation he denied to others.

## Descendants

He named no successor, and none has emerged. His work continues through the foundations in England, America, India and Latin America and through the schools: Rishi Valley, Rajghat, Brockwood Park, Oak Grove and others in India. His influence runs wider than any organisation. Huxley, Bohm, the writer Henry Miller and many teachers of mindfulness and inquiry drew on him. In India his talks reached a large educated audience who found in them a modern, secular-sounding spirituality. Among the people who learned from him and turned against him, the most striking is **U.G. Krishnamurti**, whose profile follows. Maurice Frydman's friendship with him also links this part to Nisargadatta and Part 3.
