<aside class="note"><strong>The names.</strong> His followers call him Babasaheb, a title of respect. "Ambedkar" comes from Ambadawe, the family's village in Ratnagiri district; the usual account says a schoolteacher entered the name in the school register. This chapter follows his religious thought and keeps his political and legal career in the background.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Nagpur, 14 October 1956, the morning of Dussehra. In Buddhist memory the day is also Ashoka Vijaya Dashami, kept as the day the emperor Ashoka is said to have turned to the Buddha's teaching. On an open ground on the edge of the city a crowd has been arriving since the night before, most of them dressed in white, most of them Mahars from the villages of Maharashtra and the Central Provinces.</p>
<p>Accounts of the morning agree on the outline. Bhimrao Ambedkar, sixty-five and unwell, climbs onto a platform with his wife Savita. An elderly monk, U Chandramani, Burmese by birth and long settled at Kushinagar, where the Buddha died, gives them the three refuges and the five precepts in Pali, with six other monks beside him. Ambedkar bows before an image of the Buddha. Then he turns to the crowd and asks those who wish to follow him to stand. He gives them the refuges and the precepts himself, and then a list of twenty-two vows he has written.</p>
<p>Estimates of how many people took the vows that day run from about 400,000 to half a million. Seven weeks later he was dead.</p>
</section>

## Life

Bhimrao was born on 14 April 1891 in the military cantonment of Mhow, where his father, Ramji Maloji Sakpal, was a subedar, a senior Indian non-commissioned officer, in the army. He was the youngest of fourteen children. The family were Mahars, the largest of the castes treated as untouchable in the Marathi-speaking country. Mahars served villages as watchmen, messengers and carriers of dead cattle, and they were barred from wells, temples and schoolrooms used by caste Hindus.

The household was religious. In Ambedkar's own account Ramji was a Kabirpanthi, a follower of the Kabir Panth, the sect formed around the songs of Kabir, the fifteenth-century weaver-poet of Banaras who mocked caste distinctions and the worship of idols, though Ramji's own father had been a Ramanandi. He read the books of his sect, and he made his sons read a portion of the Mahabharata and the Ramayana every night before bed.

<aside class="note"><strong>Untouchability.</strong> In Hindu law books the lowest groups were placed outside the four classes (<i>varna</i>) altogether, and contact with them was held to pollute. In practice this meant separate water, separate seats and exclusion from temples. The Mahars of Maharashtra were one such group; Ambedkar's own childhood included being refused water and being made to sit on a sack he carried from home to school.</aside>

His father retired in 1894 and the family moved to Satara and then to Bombay. His mother, Bhimabai, died when he was about five. In 1907 he passed the matriculation examination at Elphinstone High School, a rare achievement for an untouchable boy, and his community held a meeting to honour him. The meeting was chaired by a family friend and writer, Krishnaji Arjun Keluskar, known as Dada, who gave him a copy of his own Marathi life of the Buddha. Ambedkar wrote later that he "read the book with great interest, and was greatly impressed and moved by it." The story comes from his own retelling, many years afterwards, and it is the earliest point his followers give for his Buddhism.

He was married to Ramabai in 1906, as a boy. With scholarships from the Maharaja of Baroda he took a BA in Bombay in 1912 and then went to Columbia University in New York, where between 1913 and 1916 he studied economics, sociology and philosophy. One of his teachers was John Dewey, whose pragmatism, the view that ideas are tested by what they do in human life, shows in everything Ambedkar later wrote about religion. He studied next at the London School of Economics and Gray's Inn, and qualified as a barrister. London gave him a doctorate in 1923 and Columbia in 1927.

Back in Bombay he became the leader of a movement among the untouchables. Two campaigns of the late 1920s turned it into a religious question. On 20 March 1927 at Mahad, south of Bombay, he led several thousand people to drink from the Chavdar tank, a public reservoir that caste Hindus kept closed to them. That December, at a second conference at Mahad, his followers publicly burned a copy of the *Manusmriti*, the ancient law book that fixed the duties and disabilities of each caste. From 1930 to 1935 his followers tried to enter the Kalaram temple at Nashik, and were turned away again and again.

In May 1935 Ramabai died. Her wish, according to his biographers, had been to visit the Vithoba temple at Pandharpur, which as an untouchable she could not enter. Five months later, on 13 October 1935, he spoke to a conference of the Depressed Classes at Yeola, near Nashik. He told them to stop asking for entry to Hindu temples and to look for another religion, and he made a personal vow. "I solemnly assure you that I will not die as a Hindu," he said, in the words recorded by his biographer Dhananjay Keer.

The declaration started a long courtship. Leaders of Islam, Christianity and Sikhism approached him, and in April 1936 he addressed a Sikh Mission Conference at Amritsar and repeated his intention. In May 1936 he published *Annihilation of Caste*, the speech he had prepared for a Hindu reform society in Lahore, which withdrew its invitation when it read the text. The speech argued that caste had its authority in the Hindu scriptures, so reformers who left the scriptures untouched could not end it. Gandhi replied in his paper *Harijan*, and the exchange was printed with the second edition in 1937. At the end of May 1936, at a Mahar conference in Bombay, he spoke in Marathi on the question "What path to salvation?" and argued that conversion was a practical necessity for his people.

For the next decade and more he did not choose. In these years he wrote long studies of Hinduism and its history, including *Who Were the Shudras?* (1946), dedicated to the memory of Jotirao Phule, and *The Untouchables* (1948). He drafted, but did not publish, essays later collected as *Philosophy of Hinduism* and *Riddles in Hinduism*. As India's first Law Minister he chaired the committee that drafted the Constitution, and he resigned from the cabinet on 27 September 1951, when his Hindu Code Bill, which would have reformed Hindu marriage and inheritance law, failed to pass.

By 1950 his choice was plain. In May of that year the Maha Bodhi Society's journal in Calcutta printed his essay "The Buddha and the Future of His Religion." He attended the World Fellowship of Buddhists conference in Ceylon that year and the one in Rangoon in 1954, and in 1955 he founded the Buddhist Society of India (Bharatiya Bauddha Mahasabha). He spent his last years writing *The Buddha and His Dhamma*, a life of the Buddha and a statement of his teaching for new converts. After his first wife's death he had married Dr Sharada Kabir in 1948; she took the name Savita.

On 14 October 1956 he converted at Nagpur. Further mass ceremonies followed at Nagpur the next day and at Chandrapur on 16 October. In November he went to Kathmandu for the fourth conference of the World Fellowship of Buddhists, where he spoke on the Buddha and Karl Marx. He finished the manuscript of *The Buddha and His Dhamma* and died in his sleep at his house in Delhi on 6 December 1956. At his cremation in Bombay the next day, Buddhist monks led the rites. The book was published in 1957.

<aside class="note"><strong>Mahar.</strong> <span class="script" lang="mr">महार</span>. The caste of most of the 1956 converts. Many Buddhists from Mahar families now prefer to be called Buddhists, or Bauddha, and avoid the old caste name.</aside>

## The teaching

Ambedkar came to the Buddha by comparison. Between 1935 and 1956 he read the founders of the great religions as a lawyer reads constitutions, asking what each said about the equality of persons and what it would do for people at the bottom of society. His tests were his own. A religion, he argued in the 1950 essay, had to supply the moral sanction that holds a society together, had to agree with reason and science, had to have a moral code that recognised "the fundamental tenets of liberty, equality and fraternity," and must not "sanctify or ennoble poverty." In the same essay he set the Buddha beside Jesus, Muhammad and Krishna. Each of the other three, he wrote, claimed to be a *mokshadata*, a giver of salvation. "The Buddha was satisfied with playing the role of a Margadata," one who shows the way, and described himself as a human being.

The other religions failed his tests in different ways. Hinduism, he argued, made inequality sacred through the doctrine of varna and the authority of texts like the *Manusmriti*. He gave Islam and Christianity serious thought and said in various places that conversion to them would carry Indian untouchables outside Indian culture, and that caste had followed converts into both. Sikhism he considered seriously in 1936 and then let drop. Buddhism was Indian in origin, had no creator God and no caste in its teaching, and had once been the faith of large parts of the country.

**A book written for converts.** *The Buddha and His Dhamma* is the fullest statement of his religious thought. It is long, written in plain English, and arranged in eight books that tell the Buddha's life and then set out his teaching in short numbered sections, much of it paraphrased from the Pali canon. Ambedkar's unpublished introduction explains why he wrote it. "Indications of a growth in the volume of interest in Buddhism are noticeable in some sections of the Indian people," it begins, and it goes on to list four problems that an honest reader of the Buddhist scriptures would meet.

**The first problem: why the Buddha left home.** The traditional story says that the young prince Siddhartha saw an old man, a sick man and a corpse, was shaken by the fact of suffering, and left his palace in the night. Ambedkar's introduction calls this "absurd on the face of it," since a man of twenty-nine must have seen old age, sickness and death before. In his retelling the Sakya clan voted for war with the neighbouring Koliyas over the waters of the Rohini river. Siddhartha opposed the war, lost the vote and accepted exile as the price. His renunciation began in a political dispute, and his search was for an answer to conflict between people.

**The second problem: the Four Noble Truths.** In the classical teaching the Buddha's first sermon set out four truths: that life involves suffering (*dukkha*), that suffering has a cause in craving, that it can end, and that there is a path to its end. Ambedkar's introduction calls these "a great stumbling block in the way of non-Buddhists accepting the gospel of Buddhism," and asks, "Do they form part of the original gospel, or are they a later accretion by the monks?" "The four Aryan Truths make the gospel of the Buddha a gospel of pessimism," he wrote. In *The Buddha and His Dhamma* the first sermon leads with the middle way and the Noble Eightfold Path, and the Four Noble Truths have a much smaller place. Suffering remains real, but its main sources in his account are poverty, exploitation and injustice, the things human beings do to one another, and the path to its end includes the reform of society.

**The third problem: soul, karma and rebirth.** Indian thought had long tied karma, the moral weight of action, to rebirth, and Hindu law books used the pair to explain birth into a low caste as a consequence of sins in a former life. Ambedkar rejected that use outright. He agreed with the Buddhist tradition that there is no permanent soul. He kept karma as a moral law, the principle that actions have consequences, but he applied it to this life and to the effects of conduct on others, and he said it could not explain a person's station at birth. On rebirth he gave an account in which the physical elements and energies of a person return at death to a common stock from which new lives are formed. Nothing personal passes from one life to the next. Whether this is a fair reading of the Buddha or a new doctrine is the most argued question about the book.

**The fourth problem: the monk.** He asked whether the Buddha meant the *bhikkhu*, the monk, to seek his own perfection or to serve the people, and he gave the monk a social role as teacher and worker among lay followers.

**Dhamma and religion.** One of the book's central distinctions is between religion and *dhamma*. Religion, as he describes it, is a private affair of the individual and God, concerned with worship, ritual and the soul's salvation. "Contrary to this, Dhamma is social," he wrote. It concerns the relations of one person with another, and "the purpose of Dhamma is to reconstruct the world." Its foundations are morality, wisdom (*prajna*), compassion (*karuna*) and loving kindness (*maitri*). In a broadcast on All India Radio on 3 October 1954 he said his social philosophy was "enshrined in three words: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity," and added that he had taken it from the Buddha's teaching. "Let no one, however, say that I have borrowed my philosophy from the French-Revolution," he said.

**Three gurus.** In a speech in Bombay on 28 October 1954 he said, in a recent English translation of the Marathi, "I have three gurus," and named them: the Buddha first and greatest, then Kabir, then Phule. In the same speech he gave his father's Kabir Panth membership as the reason for Kabir's hold on him. The list explains his religion fairly well. Kabir gave him the songs of his childhood and a fierce Indian critique of caste and priestly religion. Phule gave him a reading of Indian history as a long struggle between the Brahmin order and the labouring castes. The Buddha gave him the religion in which these could be gathered.

**The 22 vows.** The vows he gave at Nagpur are a short creed in the form of renunciations and commitments. The converts promised to have no faith in Brahma, Vishnu and Maheshwara (Shiva), in Rama and Krishna or in the other Hindu gods and goddesses, and not to perform the *shraddha* rites for the dead. The fifth vow rejects the Hindu claim that the Buddha was an avatar of Vishnu as "sheer madness and false propaganda." Others commit the convert to reject caste and treat all human beings as equal, and to follow the Eightfold Path, the perfections and the five precepts. The nineteenth renounces Hinduism as "harmful to humanity," and the last vows declare that the convert is reborn into a new life and will live by the Buddha's teaching. Their purpose was to make the break with the old religion final, so that the new Buddhists could not be counted back into Hinduism.

<aside class="note"><strong>Navayana.</strong> <span class="script" lang="sa">नवयान</span>, "new vehicle." Buddhist tradition speaks of vehicles (<i>yana</i>), such as the Theravada and the Mahayana. Followers and scholars use Navayana for Ambedkar's reading. Reference works trace the name to a press conference he gave in Nagpur on 13 October 1956, the day before the conversion, where by later accounts he said his followers would keep clear of the old quarrels between Hinayana and Mahayana and follow a new Buddhism, a Navayana. The reported wording differs from source to source, and the original press report is hard to trace. Many of his followers simply call themselves Buddhists, or Ambedkarite Buddhists.</aside>

I count Ambedkar among the real innovators, and I hold that view knowing that traditional Buddhists would call much of what he added a departure from the Dhamma. What strikes me is how openly he did it. Most reformers in this book claim to have recovered the original teaching. Ambedkar also claimed this in places, but his introduction sets out his doubts as questions and invites readers to answer them. He chose a religion the way he drafted a constitution, by asking what it would do for the people who had to live under it.

## Lineage

Ambedkar had no human guru in the ordinary sense. He took his formal refuges from a monk, but his three named teachers were a figure of the fifth century BCE, a fifteenth-century poet and a nineteenth-century reformer he never met.

<MiniTree id="ambedkar" />

The Buddhist edge is documented by his conversion and his writing. The Kabir edge is documented by his own statements and his father's Kabir Panth practice. The Phule edge rests on the 1954 speech and the dedication of *Who Were the Shudras?* "to the Memory of Mahatma Jotiba Fule." His initiation by U Chandramani is documented by press and eyewitness accounts of the ceremony, after which Ambedkar gave the refuges, precepts and vows to the crowd himself. Gandhi stands on the tree as the man he argued against most sharply about caste and Hinduism. John Dewey, whose courses on ethics and political philosophy he took at Columbia, is an intellectual influence; *Annihilation of Caste* uses Dewey's distinction between rules and principles.

## The practice

Ambedkar taught no meditation method of his own. What he gave his followers was a set of public acts, which Ambedkarite Buddhists still repeat.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Take the refuges.</strong> Recite in Pali the three refuges, in the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha, the formula he took from U Chandramani.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep the five precepts.</strong> Refrain from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech and intoxicants.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Take the 22 vows.</strong> Renounce the Hindu gods and rites, reject caste and commit to the Eightfold Path. At mass conversions the vows are still read aloud and repeated by the crowd.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Read the book.</strong> <i>The Buddha and His Dhamma</i> is read in local viharas, the Buddhist meeting halls that stand in many Dalit neighbourhoods of Maharashtra, often in its Marathi and Hindi translations.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Gather.</strong> Followers come to Deekshabhoomi on the anniversary of the conversion and to Chaityabhoomi in Mumbai on the anniversary of his death, 6 December.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Traditional Buddhists.** For the Theravada traditions of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, the Four Noble Truths are the heart of the first sermon and rebirth is part of what the Buddha taught. Damien Keown and Charles Prebish's <i>Encyclopedia of Buddhism</i> singles out the absence of the Four Noble Truths as perhaps the most jarring feature of the book for traditional Buddhists. The editors of the 2011 critical edition, Aakash Singh Rathore and Ajay Verma, report that the book was received critically within Buddhist circles for its treatment of these doctrines and of the renunciation. The 22 vows, whose renunciations of Hindu gods have no parallel in the traditional refuges and precepts, have no standing outside Ambedkarite Buddhism. Ambedkar's defenders reply that he set out these points as open questions in his introduction, and some scholars point out that the second-century philosopher Nagarjuna also gave the Four Noble Truths a reduced place.

**Scholars of Buddhism.** Rathore and Verma's critical edition traces the book's passages to their canonical sources and shows where Ambedkar paraphrased closely and where he wrote in his own words. Historians of early Buddhism have also questioned the picture of the Buddha as a social reformer on which the book depends. Richard Gombrich, a leading scholar of Theravada, has argued that the Buddha taught for the salvation of those who joined his order and did not set out to reform society. The historian Ananya Vajpeyi, reviewing the critical edition in 2012, put the doctrinal question plainly: "If you do not accept these premises, can you really be called a Buddhist?" Christopher Queen, who has written several studies of Ambedkar's Buddhism, reads the book instead as a serious reinterpretation of the tradition for modern conditions. He has called Ambedkar "the most articulate and perhaps radical spokesman for a new turning of the wheel" and treats his movement as an early form of what is now called engaged Buddhism. The Rohini river story of the renunciation is Ambedkar's own arrangement. In the Pali commentaries and the Kunala Jataka a quarrel over the river's water takes place after the enlightenment, and the Buddha, by then a teacher, makes peace between the clans.

**Dalit critics and the results of conversion.** Within the Dalit movement the questions have mostly been about outcomes. The conversions came overwhelmingly from Mahar families in Maharashtra, and most other Dalit communities did not follow. Writing in 2025 from his own neo-Buddhist background, the writer Vaibhav Wankhede argued that the movement lost direction after Ambedkar's death, that converts lacked the temples, clergy and life-cycle rites that would hold them, and that many Dalit households now keep Ambedkar's portrait beside Hindu gods. "Without his moral and intellectual leadership, the conversion wave lost direction," he wrote. Others have questioned whether a change of religion could end discrimination that was enforced socially. Outside observers have made the same point: Paul Hockings's entry on the Neo-Buddhists in the <i>Encyclopedia of World Cultures</i> says the conversion raised the self-esteem of Mahars and Jatavs but did little to change how higher-ranking Hindus regarded them. Buddhists of Scheduled Caste origin were brought into the national scheme of reserved places only by an amendment of 1990. Inside Ambedkarite Buddhism there is a further argument between those who treat *The Buddha and His Dhamma* and the 22 vows as sufficient and those who have taken up meditation from Burmese and Western teachers, whose teaching of karma and rebirth is more traditional.

**Hindu responses.** Hindu nationalist writers have argued that Buddhism is a branch of Indic religion and that Ambedkar's break with Hinduism was less complete than the vows suggest. The argument goes back to V.D. Savarkar's <i>Essentials of Hindutva</i> (1923), which counted as Hindus all who regarded India as both fatherland and holy land, Buddhists included; according to Keer, Savarkar answered the conversion by declaring that the Buddhist Ambedkar was still a Hindu Ambedkar. Ambedkar's followers reply that the vows were written precisely to rule this reading out. The vows remain politically charged. On 5 October 2022 a Delhi cabinet minister, Rajendra Pal Gautam, attended a mass conversion at Delhi's Ambedkar Bhavan where they were recited; after criticism from the Bharatiya Janata Party he resigned on 9 October.

## Descendants

The Buddhist Society of India, which he founded in 1955, carried on under his son Yashwant and later his family. The Indian monk and Hindi writer Anand Kausalyayan (1905–1988) translated *The Buddha and His Dhamma* into Hindi and worked among the new Buddhists for the rest of his life. The English monk Sangharakshita (1925–2018), who had taken his novice ordination from U Chandramani in 1949, corresponded with Ambedkar from 1950, met him in the 1950s and lectured to the new Buddhists in Nagpur after his death. He later founded a Buddhist movement in London in 1967, now the Triratna Buddhist Community, whose Indian wing, the Triratna Bauddha Mahasangha (formerly the TBMSG), works among Ambedkarite Buddhists. Mass conversions modelled on Nagpur have continued in Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka and elsewhere, and each October hundreds of thousands come to Deekshabhoomi. The 2011 census counted about 8.4 million Buddhists in India, about three quarters of them in Maharashtra, most from families that followed Ambedkar into Buddhism.
