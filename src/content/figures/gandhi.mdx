<section class="scene">
<p>Rajkot, the summer of 1888. Mohandas Gandhi, eighteen, wants to go to London to study law. His father is dead and his mother, Putlibai, is the obstacle. She has heard that young men in England eat meat and drink, and she does not want to lose her son to either.</p>
<p>The family consults Becharji Swami, a Jain monk who had been born, like the Gandhis, a Modh Bania, and who had become an adviser to the household. In Gandhi's later account the monk offered a solution. He would have the boy take three solemn vows in front of his mother: no wine, no women and no meat. Gandhi took them, and his mother gave her permission.</p>
<p>He sailed from Bombay on 4 September 1888, after the elders of his caste had expelled him for crossing the sea. In London he kept the vows through hunger and embarrassment, and went looking for vegetarian restaurants. Forty years later, writing his autobiography, he treated those three promises as the first of his experiments with truth.</p>
</section>

## Life

Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 at Porbandar, a port on the Kathiawar peninsula, the youngest child of Karamchand Gandhi, the chief minister (*dewan*) of the small princely state, and his fourth wife, Putlibai. The family were Vaishnavas, worshippers of Vishnu and Krishna, and went to the local *haveli* temple. Putlibai came from a family of the Pranami sect, a Krishna-centred devotional movement of Gujarat whose scripture draws on the *Bhagavad Gita*, the *Bhagavata Purana* and verses that borrow from Islam as well. She kept long fasts and difficult vows, and in the autobiography her son remembers her piety more than any doctrine. The family moved to Rajkot when he was a boy. His father's visitors included Jain monks, Muslims and Parsis, and Gandhi says he grew up listening to their talk of religion.

<aside class="note"><strong>Kathiawar</strong> was a patchwork of small princely states on the western coast, with large Jain and Vaishnava merchant communities. Ideas of nonviolence and strict vegetarianism were part of ordinary life there, among Hindus as much as among Jains.</aside>

He was married to Kasturba at thirteen. The autobiography is unsparing about his jealousy and his lust in those years, and about the night his father died while he was in the bedroom with his wife. He later traced much of his thinking about celibacy back to that memory.

In London from 1888 to 1891 he trained as a barrister at the Inner Temple. Vegetarianism became a conviction there, after he read Henry Salt's *A Plea for Vegetarianism*, and he joined the London Vegetarian Society. Near the end of his second year two Theosophist brothers asked him to read the *Gita* with them, in Sir Edwin Arnold's verse translation *The Song Celestial*. He was embarrassed to admit he had never read it, even in Sanskrit or Gujarati. He found it, he wrote, a book "of priceless worth." A Christian friend gave him the Bible, and the Sermon on the Mount, with its command to turn the other cheek, went straight to him. He tried, he said, to bring together the *Gita*, Arnold's life of the Buddha *The Light of Asia* and the Sermon on the Mount, and the idea that renunciation is the highest form of religion appealed to him greatly.

Back in Bombay in 1891 he was introduced to Raychandbhai, a young jeweller and Jain poet who is the subject of the profile after this chapter. Gandhi was struck by his memory, his indifference to money in the middle of a large diamond business, and his learning. When Christian friends in South Africa pressed him to convert, and Muslim friends to read the Quran, he wrote to Raychandbhai with his questions, and the replies kept him a Hindu. In the autobiography he wrote that three moderns left a deep impress on his life: Raychandbhai "by his living contact," Tolstoy by *The Kingdom of God is Within You*, and Ruskin by *Unto This Last*.

He went to South Africa in 1893 on a one-year legal contract and stayed, with breaks, until 1914. These were the years in which the religious ideas turned into a way of life. He read Tolstoy's book in 1894 and later said it overwhelmed him. In 1904, on an overnight train from Johannesburg to Durban, he read *Unto This Last*, a book Henry Polak had given him, and decided to live by it: that the good of the individual is contained in the good of all, that a lawyer's work and a barber's have the same value, and that the life of labour on the land is the life worth living. Within weeks he had founded the Phoenix Settlement outside Durban. In 1906 he took a vow of lifelong celibacy (*brahmacharya*). The same year, at a mass meeting in Johannesburg on 11 September, Indians swore in God's name to resist a registration law and accept the penalties. Gandhi later named this method *satyagraha*. He corresponded with Tolstoy in 1909 and 1910, and in 1910 he and Hermann Kallenbach opened a second community, Tolstoy Farm, near Johannesburg. On a voyage back from London in 1909 he wrote *Hind Swaraj*, a short dialogue in Gujarati that condemns modern industrial civilisation as a kind of irreligion.

In India from 1915 he ran his life from ashrams: Kochrab and then Sabarmati in Ahmedabad, and from 1936 Sevagram, near Wardha. Each ran on a list of vows that residents promised to keep. In 1926 he gave a long series of morning talks on the *Gita* at Sabarmati. In 1929 he finished *Anasaktiyoga*, a Gujarati translation of the *Gita* with notes, and in 1930, from prison at Yeravda, he wrote a weekly letter to the ashram explaining each vow. Those letters became *From Yeravda Mandir*.

His last years were the most extreme in his religious life. In the winter of 1946–47 he walked through the riot-torn villages of Noakhali in eastern Bengal, repeating the name of Rama and preaching nonviolence to Hindus and Muslims who had been killing each other. On the evening of 30 January 1948, on his way to his daily interfaith prayer meeting in the garden of Birla House in New Delhi, he was shot three times by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist. The words *He Ram*, "O Rama," carved on his memorial at Raj Ghat, are said to have been his last, though the witnesses' accounts differ.

## The teaching

Gandhi wrote no systematic theology. He wrote newspaper columns, letters and answers to questions, nearly a hundred volumes of them, and he changed his mind in public. Several ideas stay constant through all of it.

**Truth as God.** For most of his life he said that God is Truth. Then he reversed the sentence. At a meeting in Switzerland in December 1931, on his way home from the Round Table Conference in London, he explained that he had long held that for him God is Truth, "But two years ago I went a step further and said that Truth is God." The account was printed in *Young India* on 31 December 1931. His reason was practical. People quarrel endlessly about God, and some, like the atheist Charles Bradlaugh, deny him outright, but almost nobody denies truth. He also pointed to the Sanskrit word for truth, *satya*, which comes from *sat*, that which is. Truth in this sense is reality itself, and to seek it is to seek God whatever name one uses. In *From Yeravda Mandir* he put it plainly: it is "more correct to say that Truth is God than to say that God is Truth."

<aside class="note"><strong>In the original.</strong> <span class="script" lang="sa">सत्</span> (<i>sat</i>) means "being" or "what is." <span class="script" lang="sa">सत्य</span> (<i>satya</i>) is truth in the sense of what is real, as well as what is honestly said.</aside>

This made truth something a person approaches step by step. He called his autobiography the story of his "experiments with truth" and described himself as a seeker who had glimpses of absolute truth and had to live, meanwhile, by the relative truth he could see. He said he admired the Jain doctrine that reality has many sides and that each view catches only part of it. It allowed him to hold firm convictions and to expect that opponents saw something he did not.

**Nonviolence as the means.** If truth is the goal, *ahimsa*, nonviolence, is the way to it. Gandhi took the word from the Jain and Hindu ethics of his childhood, where it meant above all not harming living beings, and stretched it. In his writing ahimsa covers anger, hatred and ill will in thought as well as physical harm, and in its positive form it becomes love, including love of an opponent. He insisted that means and ends cannot be separated. An end won by violence or lies is changed by the means used to reach it.

**Satyagraha as a spiritual discipline.** The word, coined in South Africa, joins *satya*, truth, and *agraha*, firmness or insistence. Gandhi described it as the force that comes from truth and love, and he expected satyagrahis to train for it like ascetics. They were to accept suffering without retaliating, to hold no hatred, to tell the opponent their plans, and to be ready to stop if the campaign turned violent, as he did in 1922 after a crowd killed policemen at Chauri Chaura. Fasting was its sharpest tool. He described his public fasts as acts of self-purification and penance aimed at the conscience of those around him, and he distinguished them from hunger strikes meant to coerce.

**Brahmacharya and the vows.** At Sabarmati and Sevagram residents took eleven vows. The list, as he explained it from Yeravda, began with truth, nonviolence, non-stealing, celibacy and non-possession, the five great vows shared by the yogic and Jain traditions. To them he added bread labour, control of the palate, fearlessness, equal respect for all religions, *swadeshi* (serving one's neighbours and using what they make) and the removal of untouchability. Vinoba Bhave later set the list to a Marathi verse that is still sung at prayer.

For Gandhi *brahmacharya* meant much more than sexual abstinence. He described it as control of all the senses, in thought, word and deed, so that a person's whole energy could go into the service of truth. He believed that semen conserved became a spiritual and physical power, an idea with deep roots in Indian yoga and medicine, and he regarded his own lapses in thought as failures to be confessed and corrected.

**The Gita as dictionary.** "To me the Gita became the guide of conduct," he wrote. "It became my dictionary of daily reference." He memorised thirteen chapters, a few verses at a time, while bathing and brushing his teeth. His reading of it was his own. He held that the battle of Kurukshetra stands for the war inside each person, and that the poem teaches action without attachment to its fruits, which he called *anasakti*. The second chapter's portrait of the *sthitaprajna*, the person of steady wisdom, was for him the summary of the whole book, and it was read every evening at his prayer meetings. He argued that a person who truly gave up the fruits of action could not take up violence, and so he read the *Gita* as a gospel of nonviolence, against its plain setting on a battlefield.

**Equal respect for religions.** Gandhi called himself a *sanatani* Hindu and kept Hindu vocabulary to the end, especially the name of Rama, which a family servant taught him as a child as protection against ghosts. He did not believe that any religion was final or complete. His prayer meetings included readings from the Quran, Christian hymns, Buddhist and Zoroastrian prayers, and he opposed religious conversion on the grounds that each person should grow within the tradition they were born into. He also kept a role for an inner voice, which he said he heard at a few decisive moments and which he took to be the voice of truth.

**Religion as public life.** Gandhi refused the line between religion and politics. He wrote in the autobiography that he could not live a religious life without identifying himself with the whole of mankind, and that this was impossible without taking part in politics. For him spinning, sanitation, the work against untouchability and the national movement were all forms of worship. This is the sense in which he belongs in a part about conduct as a path. He did not teach a meditation technique or a doctrine of the Self. He taught that the disciplined, truthful and nonviolent life is the spiritual practice.

What strikes me most in Gandhi's religious writing is how little of it is about God in the usual sense, and how much is about the next small decision. He treated a diet, a quarrel or a strike as a test of truth. That makes him unusual among the figures in this book, most of whom point inward and away from the world. He pointed into the world and said that the way out of the self runs through it.

## Lineage

Gandhi had no guru and was never initiated into any order. He said he looked for one all his life and that the seat stayed empty. His tree therefore shows influences above him, from his mother's Vaishnava piety and his Jain surroundings to Raychandbhai, the *Gita*, the Sermon on the Mount, Tolstoy and Ruskin, and people shaped by him below.

<MiniTree id="gandhi" />

The strongest line runs to **Vinoba Bhave**, who joined his ashram in 1916 and whom Gandhi chose in 1940 as the first individual satyagrahi of a new campaign. Vinoba carried the vows and the *Gita* talks into a movement of land gifts after Gandhi's death. **B.R. Ambedkar**, the subject of a chapter in Part 11, stands on the tree as a man who reacted against him. Raychandbhai's line continues separately in the Jain revival movements described in the profiles that follow this chapter.

## The practice

Gandhi's daily discipline at Sabarmati and Sevagram can be reconstructed from his letters and from the ashram rules.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Prayer at about four in the morning and again in the evening,</strong> with verses from the second chapter of the <i>Gita</i>, the ashram vows and readings from several religions.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Bread labour,</strong> above all spinning on the <i>charkha</i>, which he treated as a meditation and a vow of solidarity with the poor.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keeping the vows</strong> in small things: plain food without spices, no possessions beyond need, truthful speech, cleaning latrines in turn.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>A weekly day of silence,</strong> Mondays, kept from the 1920s.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Repeating the name of Rama,</strong> which in his last years he called his cure for fear, illness and anger.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Fasting</strong> as penance for one's own failures or those of people one is responsible for.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Caste and Ambedkar.** Gandhi campaigned against untouchability for most of his life and made its removal an ashram vow, but he defended *varna*, the fourfold order of society, as a division of duties by birth that he said implied no ranking. B.R. Ambedkar rejected this as a defence of caste in gentler language. In 1932 Gandhi fasted in Yeravda prison against separate electorates for the Depressed Classes, and Ambedkar, under heavy pressure, signed the Poona Pact, which replaced them with reserved seats. When Ambedkar published *Annihilation of Caste* in 1936, arguing that caste could be destroyed only by rejecting the scriptures that sanctioned it, Gandhi replied in his paper *Harijan*, and Ambedkar answered him in turn. Ambedkar's book *What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables* (1945) is the fullest statement of the case against him. Many Dalit writers reject the name *Harijan*, "people of God," which Gandhi used for untouchables, as patronising. Gandhi's own position on inter-caste marriage and on varna shifted in his last years, and scholars disagree about how far.

**The brahmacharya experiments.** In his last years, especially in Noakhali in 1946–47, Gandhi slept beside young women of his circle, including his grandniece Manu, as what he described as a test and a sacrifice of his celibacy. He spoke about it openly in his prayer meetings. Several close associates objected. His Bengali interpreter, the anthropologist Nirmal Kumar Bose, left his service partly over the issue and described the dispute in *My Days with Gandhi* (1953). His stenographer R.P. Parasuram resigned in protest. Biographers have treated the episode in very different ways, from a spiritual experiment to a misuse of authority over young women. Gandhi maintained that his conduct was a pure act of self-examination.

**Reading the Gita against the text.** Scholars and traditional commentators have pointed out that the *Gita* is spoken on a battlefield to persuade a warrior to fight. Gandhi's allegorical reading was sincere and widely shared, but many readers, including some of his admirers, regard it as a reinterpretation.

**Earlier views.** Historians including Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed (*The South African Gandhi*, 2015) have documented contemptuous remarks about Black Africans in Gandhi's South African writings. His defenders argue that his views changed over time.

**The cost of the vows to others.** His wife Kasturba and his eldest son Harilal were drawn into his experiments without much choice. His own autobiography records his harshness toward them, and later biographers have stressed it.

## Descendants

Gandhi founded no religious order, and he said there was no such thing as Gandhism. His religious descendants are of three kinds. Within India the most direct is **Vinoba Bhave**, along with the network of constructive workers who kept the ashram vows. Abroad, his idea of nonviolent resistance as a spiritual discipline passed to Martin Luther King Jr., to the Ghaffar Khan's Pathan Khudai Khidmatgar movement and to many others. Among Jains, his fame drew attention back to Raychandbhai, whose followers now run large institutions in Gujarat. His formula "Truth is God" has been taken up by people of every religion and none.
