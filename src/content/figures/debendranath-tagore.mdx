## Life

Debendranath Tagore was born in Calcutta on 15 May 1817, in the family house at Jorasanko, the eldest son of Dwarkanath Tagore, one of the richest Indian merchants of his day, and Digambari Devi. He grew up in luxury and entered the Anglo-Hindu College in 1827. His father was a friend of Rammohan Roy and in 1830 became a trustee of Rammohan's Brahmo Sabha, the small society for imageless worship of one God.

He traced his religious life to two moments in his youth. The first was a night beside his grandmother as she lay dying by the Ganga in the late 1830s (1835 and 1838 are both given). Accounts drawn from his autobiography describe a sudden loss of interest in the family's wealth and a new, quiet joy. The second, by his own account, was a stray printed page from the Isha Upanishad that came into his hands, which led him to the Upanishads and to the circle Rammohan had left behind.

On 6 October 1839 he and a group of friends founded the Tattvaranjini Sabha, soon renamed the Tattvabodhini Sabha, to study and spread the Upanishadic religion. He published a Bengali translation of the Katha Upanishad in 1840 and funded the *Tattvabodhini Patrika*, edited from 1843 by the rationalist writer Akshay Kumar Datta. On 21 December 1843, 7 Pous 1765 in the Shaka calendar, Ramchandra Vidyabagish, the Brahmo Sabha's minister, received him and his companions into the society. By his son Satyendranath's account he and nineteen others were the first to sign a written covenant. The date is still marked each year by the Poush Mela at Santiniketan.

The covenant created a membership and a church, the Brahmo Samaj. It also raised the question this part of the book is about. Rammohan had treated the Upanishads as authoritative, and the Samaj at first did the same. In 1845 Debendranath sent four young Brahmins to Benares, each to study one of the four Vedas. Their reports, and pressure from Akshay Kumar Datta and the radical members around the *Patrika*, convinced him that the Vedas could not be defended as free from error. Between about 1847 and 1850 the Samaj gave up the doctrine of Vedic infallibility. In its place he compiled the *Brahmo Dharma*, a book of selected passages, mostly from the Upanishads, with a statement of Brahmo principles. Sources date it to 1848, 1849, 1850 or 1854, probably because it appeared in parts and editions.

In the later 1850s he spent about a year and a half alone in the hills around Simla. While he was away, in 1857, a young man named Keshab Chandra Sen joined the Samaj. Debendranath came to rely on him and in 1862 installed him as *acharya*, or minister. The two soon disagreed about caste, about intermarriage and about whether Brahmins conducting Brahmo services should wear the sacred thread. Keshab withdrew in February 1865 and in November 1866 founded the Brahmo Samaj of India. Debendranath's body became known as the Adi, or original, Brahmo Samaj.

In 1863 he took a permanent lease on twenty bighas of land at Bolpur in Birbhum and built a house he called Santiniketan, the abode of peace. A trust deed of 8 March 1888 dedicated the ashram to the meditation of all who worship the one God and barred image worship on its grounds. A prayer hall of iron and coloured glass followed in 1891. His youngest son, Rabindranath, later founded his school there. The Samaj gave Debendranath the title Maharshi, great sage. He died in Calcutta on 19 January 1905.

## The teaching

Debendranath's God was one, formless and personal: a being to be worshipped, prayed to and loved. Evelyn Underhill, introducing the English translation of his autobiography in 1914, summed up his view as holding that <q>the relation between God and the soul is the relation of closest friendship</q>. He took the Upanishads as his scripture, but he read them as a devotee. He kept God distinct from the soul, rejected the monist formula that the self simply is the supreme deity, and rejected the doctrine of maya when it is taken to mean that the world is an illusion. He also set aside avatars and image worship. He loved the Persian poems of Hafiz, in which, Underhill wrote, he found a congenial expression of his own love of God.

His answer to this part's question was a qualified yes. The Vedas could be reread, and when they were reread honestly they could not all be accepted. Something had to judge between passages, and for Debendranath that judge was the inner witness. Underhill quotes his phrase <q>The pure heart filled with the light of intuitive knowledge</q> as the source he trusted. In practice this meant choosing. The *Brahmo Dharma* keeps the Upanishadic passages that the heart of a worshipper of one God recognises as true and leaves out the rest. Its principles include the line, in his son's translation, <q>In His worship lies our salvation in this world and in the next.</q>

Worship mattered more to him than doctrine. In 1844 he drew up set forms of Brahmo worship, which the Samaj adopted the following year. In social matters he moved slowly. He kept many caste customs and resisted Keshab's call to drop the sacred thread, and this conservatism, as much as theology, is what divided the Samaj.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="debendranath-tagore" />

The edges above him are documented. He took over Rammohan Roy's Brahmo Sabha through Ramchandra Vidyabagish, who received him into it in December 1843, and he read the Upanishads directly. The push to abandon Vedic infallibility came partly from Akshay Kumar Datta, a member of his own circle. Below him, Keshab Chandra Sen served as his minister from 1862 and broke away in 1865–66; Dayananda Saraswati met him in Calcutta in 1872–73 and rejected his position on the Vedas; and his son Rabindranath carried his Upanishadic devotion into poetry.
