## Life

Bimala Prasad Datta was born on 6 February 1874 in Puri, near the Jagannath temple, the seventh of fourteen children of Kedarnath Datta, later known as Bhaktivinoda Thakura. He studied at schools in Calcutta and Serampore and at the Metropolitan Institution, where he earned the title "Siddhanta Sarasvati" for his work in astronomy. Sources disagree on whether he graduated from Sanskrit College in 1895 or left after a dispute with its principal. He taught, edited Sanskrit texts and compiled a Vaishnava encyclopedia, and in the 1900s began preaching on the Bhagavata.

He took initiation from the ascetic Gaura Kishora Dasa Babaji, a renunciant who had refused him at first. Sources give 1900 or January 1901. His father had set him the task of spreading Chaitanya's teaching and of securing Mayapur as a place of pilgrimage. In 1911, at a conference in Balighai, he read a paper titled "Brahmana o Vaishnava," arguing that a devotee of Krishna outranks a Brahmin who lacks devotion, whatever the birth of either.

On 27 March 1918 (some sources say 7 March) he gave himself the renunciant's vows before a picture of his late guru, because no Gaudiya sannyasi was available to initiate him. He founded Sri Chaitanya Math at Mayapur that year. The wider mission was registered on 5 February 1919 and became known as the Gaudiya Math, after its Calcutta centre and its weekly Bengali magazine *Gaudiya*. It grew to sixty-four centres in India and Burma, ran presses and newspapers, including the English monthly *The Harmonist*, and in the early 1930s staged illustrated "exhibitions" of Vaishnava teaching. Its first European centre opened in London in 1933 under Lord Zetland's presidency, followed by a Berlin centre in 1934. Western results were small.

In 1933 he gave initiation to a Calcutta pharmacist and householder, Abhay Charan De, later A. C. Bhaktivedanta Prabhupada. ISKCON accounts date their first meeting to 1922, and say Bhaktisiddhanta asked him to teach in English.

His public position drew opposition. He criticised Smarta Brahmins for claiming hereditary priestly rank, and on 29 January 1925 his party was attacked by an upper-caste crowd in Nabadwip. He died in Calcutta on 1 January 1937, aged sixty-two or sixty-three depending on the account. He named no successor, and the mission split within weeks.

## The teaching

Bhaktisiddhanta held to his father's theology. Krishna is the supreme person, the soul is eternally related to him, and the holy name is the means of the age. His emphasis fell on boundaries. He treated authentic transmission as the test of a teacher, called several Gaudiya groups he considered corrupt *apasampradayas* (deviant lineages), and rejected Advaita Vedanta as impersonalism.

The 1911 paper put the caste argument in print. He accepted varnashrama, the scheme of four social classes and four life stages, as a useful order, and held that devotion rather than birth made a person fit to teach and to receive the sacred thread. In practice the Gaudiya Math initiated people of any birth as Brahmins and sent them out in orange cloth to preach in cities. He called untouchability a cultural habit rather than a religious requirement, in a reply he wrote to Gandhi in 1933.

He also took the Mayapur identification further than Bhaktivinoda did, building the Navadvipa Dhama Pracarini Sabha and a temple complex there. Navadvip's established Vaishnava houses disputed the claim.

## Lineage

Bhaktisiddhanta received the tradition from his father and from Gaura Kishora Dasa Babaji. His line continues through the Gaudiya Math, which divided after 1937. A court split the institution into two administrative bodies in 1948, and several disciples founded independent missions, among them Prabhupada's ISKCON in 1966.
