## Life

Bimala Prasad Datta was born on 6 February 1874 in Puri, near the Jagannath temple, the seventh of fourteen children of Kedarnath Datta, later known as Bhaktivinoda Thakura, and his wife Bhagavati Devi. His infancy was spent at Ranaghat in Nadia. Wikipedia says his father linked the birth to a dream of Jagannath and named him after the goddess Bimala, and devotional biographies add that he memorised the Bhagavad Gita as a small boy, at seven in one and nine in another, and began helping his father proofread the Bengali journal *Sajjana-toshani*. Bhaktivinoda initiated him into chanting the holy name on tulasi beads in the early 1880s. He studied at schools in Calcutta and Serampore and at the Metropolitan Institution, where he studied Sanskrit, mathematics and traditional astronomy and earned the title "Siddhanta Sarasvati."
 Sources disagree on whether he graduated from Sanskrit College in 1895 or left after a dispute with its principal. In 1895 he took a post at the royal court of Tripura, a secretary and historian in Wikipedia's account and an editor of a history of the Tripura ruling line in a devotional biography. He taught, edited Sanskrit texts and compiled a Vaishnava encyclopedia, and in the 1900s began preaching on the Bhagavata.

He took initiation from the ascetic Gaura Kishora Dasa Babaji, a renunciant who had refused him at first. Gaura Kishora, born in 1838 in what is now Bangladesh, had taken up the life of a mendicant after his wife's death and lived at Vrindavan and Navadvip, known for unconventional behaviour. A devotional biography says he put Bhaktisiddhanta off for several visits, saying he needed Chaitanya's approval, and that his instruction was to preach. Sources give 1900 or January 1901 for the initiation, the later date by Bhaktisiddhanta's own testimony. After a year at Puri and a journey through South India in 1904 to study Vaishnava lineages, he settled at Mayapur. In 1905, a devotional biography says, he took a vow to chant a billion names, living in a grass hut, cooking rice once a day in an earthen pot and sleeping on the ground. Wikipedia says the vow took about ten years. In 1911, at a conference in Balighai, he read a paper titled "Brahmana o Vaishnava," arguing that a devotee of Krishna outranks a Brahmin who lacks devotion, whatever the birth of either. A devotional biography says he wrote it on behalf of his ailing father, who was pleased with it. Bhaktivinoda died in 1914, after asking his son to preach and to develop the birthsite of Chaitanya.

On 27 March 1918 he gave himself the renunciant's vows before a picture of his late guru, because no Gaudiya sannyasi was available to initiate him. He founded Sri Chaitanya Math at Mayapur that year. The wider mission was registered on 5 February 1919 and became known as the Gaudiya Math, after its Calcutta centre and its weekly Bengali magazine *Gaudiya*. It grew to sixty-four centres in India, with others in Burma, England and Germany, ran presses and newspapers, including the English monthly *The Harmonist*, and had set up its first press in Calcutta in 1913, and in the early 1930s staged illustrated "exhibitions" of Vaishnava teaching. His monks reached London in 1933, and the Gaudiya Mission Society there was inaugurated in April 1934 with Lord Zetland, the Secretary of State for India, as president. A Berlin centre followed later in 1934. Western results were small.

In 1933, at Allahabad, he gave initiation to Abhay Charan De, later A. C. Bhaktivedanta Prabhupada. ISKCON accounts date their first meeting to 1922, in Calcutta. A 1936 letter from Bhaktisiddhanta encouraged him to preach in English.

He criticised Smarta Brahmins for claiming hereditary priestly rank. He died in Calcutta on 1 January 1937, aged sixty-two (Wikipedia also gives sixty-three). A devotional biography says that shortly before his death he told his leading disciples to preach the message of Rupa and Raghunatha, two of the Vrindavan Goswamis, and to stay united. He named no successor, and the mission split within weeks.

## The teaching

Bhaktisiddhanta held to his father's theology. Krishna is the supreme person, the soul is eternally related to him, and the holy name is the means of the age. His emphasis fell on boundaries. He treated authentic transmission as the test of a teacher, called several Gaudiya groups he considered corrupt *apasampradayas* (deviant lineages), and rejected Advaita Vedanta as impersonalism.

The 1911 paper put the caste argument in print. A Journal of Vaishnava Studies entry describes him as a reformer who combined classical Gaudiya theology with a public, activist religious identity, using publishing and preaching as the tools of reform. He taught what he called *shuddha-bhakti*, pure devotion, and made devotion the basis of knowledge of the divine. He rejected the erotic readings of Krishna's love-play favoured by the sahajiya groups. He took from Rupa Goswami the principle of *yukta-vairagya*, renunciation by engagement, which means using things in service of the divine. He called the printing press a *brihat mridanga*, a large drum, in the devotional biography's report. He accepted varnashrama, the scheme of four social classes and four life stages, as a useful order, and held that devotion, and not birth, made a person fit to teach and to receive the sacred thread. In practice the Gaudiya Math initiated people of any birth as Brahmins and sent them out to preach.

He also took the Mayapur identification further than Bhaktivinoda did, working through the Navadvipa Dhama Pracarini Sabha, which some accounts say he founded and others trace to a gathering around his father in 1891. Navadvip's established Vaishnava houses disputed the claim.

## Lineage

Bhaktisiddhanta received the tradition from his father and from Gaura Kishora Dasa Babaji. His line continues through the Gaudiya Math, which divided after 1937. According to Paul Sherbow (2004), as cited by Wikipedia, the institution divided into two administrative bodies in 1948, and several disciples founded independent missions, among them Prabhupada's ISKCON in 1966.

## The practice

The Math's daily life, as Wikipedia describes it, centred on the holy name, worship, study and publishing. Each part can be tried by a reader.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Chant on beads.</strong> Repeat the Hare Krishna mantra individually (<i>japa</i>), counting on a string of tulasi beads, as Bhaktivinoda taught his son.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Sing in company.</strong> Join <i>kirtan</i>, the public singing of the names, a central practice of the Gaudiya Math.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hear and study.</strong> Attend a lecture on the <i>Chaitanya-charitamrita</i> or the Bhagavata, and read the text. The Math held lectures and study of devotional texts as regular parts of its life.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Offer before eating.</strong> A devotional biography tells of the four-year-old Bimala Prasad, who swore never to eat mangoes again after eating one before it had been offered to Krishna, and kept the vow all his life.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Share the books.</strong> He started a printing press in 1913, and the Math treated publishing as a form of preaching.</div></li>
</ol>
