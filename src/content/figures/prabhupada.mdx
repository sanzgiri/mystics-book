<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> His parents named him Abhay Charan; *abhay* means fearless and *charan* means feet, which the movement reads as fearless at Krishna's feet. Gaudiya scholars gave him the title Bhaktivedanta in 1939, and he took the name A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami at his sannyasa in 1959. His disciples called him Prabhupada, which they explain as the master at whose feet other masters sit, and the Society's official usage is His Divine Grace A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada.</aside>

## Life

Abhay Charan De was born in Calcutta on 1 September 1896, the day after Janmashtami, the festival of Krishna's birth. His father, Gour Mohan De, was a cloth merchant of the Suvarna Banik community and a devout Vaishnava; his mother was Rajani De. His father arranged for the boy to learn the *mridanga* drum and encouraged him to stage a small cart festival for Jagannatha in the lane outside their house, a childhood detail the movement's biographies dwell on.

He entered Scottish Church College in Calcutta in 1916 and finished his studies in English, philosophy and economics in 1920. By then he had taken up Gandhi's call to boycott British institutions, and he declined to accept his diploma. In 1918, while still a student, he married Radharani Datta in a match arranged by his father. They had five children. He went into the pharmaceutical trade, first as a manager for a Calcutta chemical firm and later with his own business in Allahabad.

In 1922 a friend, Narendranath Mullik, took him to meet a Vaishnava monk who was drawing attention in Calcutta: Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati, founder of the Gaudiya Math. In the story as Prabhupada later told it, the monk asked the young nationalist why he did not take Chaitanya's message to the English-speaking world. Abhay argued that India first needed independence. Bhaktisiddhanta answered that political rule is temporary and that the soul's need for Krishna does not wait on it. Abhay left convinced, though he went back to his business and his family.

He was formally initiated by Bhaktisiddhanta at Allahabad in 1933 and given the name Abhay Charanaravinda. In a letter dated 3 December 1936, a month before he died, Bhaktisiddhanta wrote to him: <q>I have every hope that you can turn yourself [into] a very good English preacher.</q> Prabhupada treated this as the order of his life. Bhaktisiddhanta died on 1 January 1937, naming no single successor, and the Gaudiya Math split within weeks into rival factions and lawsuits. Abhay stayed outside the quarrel.

In 1939 Gaudiya elders gave him the title Bhaktivedanta. In 1944, during the war, he began *Back to Godhead*, an English magazine that he wrote, edited, paid for and sold himself, sometimes on the streets of Calcutta and Delhi. In 1952 he founded a League of Devotees in Jhansi, which collapsed within about two years. In 1950 he took *vanaprastha*, the retired stage of life, and moved to Vrindavan, the town on the Yamuna where Krishna is said to have spent his childhood. He lived in two rooms at the Radha-Damodara temple.

On 17 September 1959, at the Keshavji Gaudiya Math in Mathura, he took *sannyasa*, the vow of renunciation, from Bhakti Prajnana Keshava, a fellow disciple of Bhaktisiddhanta. He was sixty-three. He then set himself a task of translation on a scale few would attempt at that age. From 1962 he published the first canto of the *Srimad-Bhagavatam* in English, with the Sanskrit, a word-for-word gloss, a translation and his own commentary, in three volumes printed in Delhi on borrowed money. He sold copies to libraries and officials to pay for the next volume.

<aside class="note"><strong>Vanaprastha.</strong> The third of the four traditional stages of a Hindu life, after student and householder. The householder hands over his affairs and withdraws, often to a holy place, before full renunciation.</aside>

The *Jaladuta* left Calcutta on 13 August 1965. On arrival he stayed for some weeks with the Agarwal family in Butler, Pennsylvania, who had sponsored his visa, and lectured at the local YMCA and in churches. Then he moved to New York. For months he lived in borrowed rooms, a studio on the Upper West Side and then a loft on the Bowery, cooking for whoever came, lecturing on the *Gita* and leading chanting. In 1966 young people from the Lower East Side counterculture helped him rent a former curiosity shop at 26 Second Avenue, still labelled Matchless Gifts. There, in July 1966, he incorporated the International Society for Krishna Consciousness. On 9 October 1966 he and a handful of followers sat under an elm in Tompkins Square Park and chanted for hours; ISKCON dates the public start of the movement to that afternoon. The poet Allen Ginsberg, who lived nearby, joined the chanting in the park, and in 1966 the Swami and his followers recorded a long-playing album of the chanting for a small New York label.

In January 1967 he flew to San Francisco, where a benefit concert called the Mantra-Rock Dance at the Avalon Ballroom put him on a stage with the Grateful Dead and drew about three thousand people. Temples followed in San Francisco, Montreal, Los Angeles and Boston. In 1968 disciples opened a centre in London, and in 1969 George Harrison produced a single of the Hare Krishna mantra sung by the London devotees, which reached the British charts. In 1973 Harrison bought a manor house north of London for the movement, now Bhaktivedanta Manor.

From 1967, despite a stroke that year and chronic illness afterward, Prabhupada travelled almost without stopping. He went around the world more than a dozen times, by the movement's count, and opened more than a hundred temples, farms and schools. He set up a Governing Body Commission (GBC) of senior disciples in 1970 to manage the Society, and the Bhaktivedanta Book Trust (BBT) in 1972 to publish his books. He built large temples in India at Mayapur, Chaitanya's birthplace in Bengal, at Vrindavan, and at Juhu in Bombay. By his disciples' accounts he rose most nights after midnight to dictate translation and commentary into a tape recorder. Over twelve years he produced more than eighty volumes.

His health failed in 1977. He went back to Vrindavan to die there. On 9 July 1977 his secretary, Tamal Krishna Goswami, sent a letter to the Society's leaders naming eleven senior disciples who would perform initiations on his behalf while he was ill. He died on 14 November 1977 at the Krishna Balaram Mandir in Vrindavan, after months of illness. The surgeon who treated him that September later described advanced diabetes and kidney failure. He was eighty-one. He is buried in a shrine beside the temple.

## The teaching

Prabhupada taught one thing in many registers: God is a person, his name is Krishna, and every living being is his eternal servant who has forgotten this and can remember it by chanting his names.

**Krishna as the Supreme Person.** Gaudiya Vaishnavism holds that Krishna, the cowherd of Vrindavan, is God himself and the source of every other form of the divine, including Vishnu. Prabhupada rendered this in English as "the Supreme Personality of Godhead," a phrase his books made familiar to millions. The impersonal Brahman of the Upanishads is real, in this teaching, but it is the effulgence of Krishna's body, a lower and partial realisation. The final reality has form, name, qualities, companions and pastimes.

**The soul as servant.** Each living being, human or animal, is a *jiva*, a tiny eternal spark of spirit. The *jiva* is part of Krishna and is never Krishna himself. Its natural state is loving service. It falls into the material world by wanting to enjoy apart from Krishna, takes body after body by the law of karma, and suffers under *maya*, illusion. Liberation means returning to that service, with a spiritual body, in Krishna's own world. Prabhupada spent much energy attacking what he called *Mayavada*, the Advaita teaching that the self and Brahman are finally identical. In his view it was the subtlest form of the illusion, because it erased the one who loves and the one who is loved.

<aside class="note"><strong>Achintya-bhedabheda.</strong> "Inconceivable simultaneous oneness and difference." The Gaudiya account, worked out by Jiva Goswami in the sixteenth century, of how the soul and the world relate to God: one with him in quality, as sparks are one with fire, and different from him in quantity and power, in a way the mind cannot fully grasp.</aside>

**The holy name.** Chaitanya taught that in the present age, the Kali Yuga, the most effective practice is to chant God's names. Prabhupada made one sixteen-word mantra the centre of his movement: *Hare Krishna Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna Hare Hare, Hare Rama Hare Rama, Rama Rama Hare Hare*. He taught that the name is non-different from Krishna himself, so that chanting it is direct contact with God and cleans the heart over time. Chanting together in public, with drums and hand cymbals, is *sankirtana*, which he saw as Chaitanya's own mission. Distributing his books was, to him, a form of the same thing.

**Bhakti as a whole life.** He gave his disciples a full regimen. They rose before dawn, chanted sixteen rounds of the mantra on a string of 108 beads (about two hours), attended temple worship, and kept four rules: no meat, fish or eggs; no intoxicants, including tea, coffee and tobacco; no sex outside marriage, and within marriage only for children; and no gambling. They ate only food first offered to Krishna. Men took up *brahmana* initiation with the sacred thread regardless of birth, which Prabhupada justified by the *Gita*'s statement that the four social orders are made by quality and work. He taught that caste by birth was a degradation of a system he called *varnashrama*, and he hoped to rebuild that system on farm communities.

**Scripture as it is.** His commentaries present themselves as transmission, the teaching of a succession of masters passed on without alteration. The title *Bhagavad-gita As It Is* states the claim. In his reading the *Gita* has one plain meaning: Krishna is God and the reader should surrender to him. He criticised commentators who read the text as allegory, as a manual of ethics or as a guide to the impersonal Absolute, and he named Radhakrishnan among them. His commentaries draw heavily on earlier Gaudiya commentators, especially Vishvanatha Chakravarti and Baladeva Vidyabhushana, so that the claim to be passing on a received reading is substantially accurate for the Gaudiya school.

What is distinctive in Prabhupada is less doctrine than its delivery: an uncompromising, literal, cheerful theism, set out in plain English, with a daily practice that anyone could start the same afternoon. He asked converts to change their whole lives, and he gave them a script for every hour of the day.

I find him one of the least original thinkers in this book and one of the most consequential. Vivekananda took Vedanta west by translating it into the vocabulary of universal religion. Prabhupada refused that translation. He brought Krishna west in a dhoti, with the dietary rules, the temple schedule and the Sanskrit names, and he asked Americans to adopt them. That this worked at all, among the young of 1966, is the surprise of his story, and my verdict on his originality says nothing about the size of what he built.

## Lineage

Prabhupada stands at the end of a chain he printed at the front of the *Bhagavad-gita As It Is*: from Krishna through Brahma, Narada and Vyasa to Madhva, then through Madhavendra Puri and Ishvara Puri to Chaitanya, and down through Bhaktivinoda Thakura, Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji and Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati to himself.

<MiniTree id="prabhupada" />

His own initiation by Bhaktisiddhanta in 1933 is documented, as is his sannyasa from Bhakti Prajnana Keshava in 1959 and the formative influence of Bhaktivinoda's writings, which Bhaktisiddhanta published and taught. Two links further back are disputed. The first is the joining of Chaitanya's movement to Madhva's Brahma sampradaya, which Gaudiya writers have defended since the eighteenth century and which, as the Vaishnava scholar Steven J. Rosen notes, has been debated, particularly by scholars of the Madhva tradition, since Chaitanya's theology differs from Madhva's on central points. The second is the shape of the modern chain itself. Bhaktivinoda's formal initiation came from Bipin Bihari Goswami; Bhaktisiddhanta took initiation from Gaurakishora Dasa Babaji (1900 or January 1901 in different accounts) and in 1918 conferred sannyasa on himself before a picture of his guru. The line from Bhaktivinoda to Bhaktisiddhanta is therefore one of instruction (*shiksha*) more than formal initiation (*diksha*), and Gaudiya families who trace hereditary initiation lines have not always accepted it. ISKCON calls it a *bhagavata parampara*, a succession of realised teachers.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Chant on beads.</strong> Sixteen rounds of the Hare Krishna mantra daily on a string of 108 beads, with attention on hearing the sound. Newcomers are encouraged to start with fewer.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep the four principles.</strong> No meat, fish or eggs; no intoxicants; no illicit sex; no gambling. Initiation is given only to those who keep them.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Offer food.</strong> Cook vegetarian food for Krishna, offer it before an image or picture, and eat it as <i>prasadam</i>, sanctified food.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hear and read.</strong> Daily study of the <i>Gita</i> and the <i>Bhagavatam</i> in his translations, often in a morning class at the temple.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Sankirtana.</strong> Chant together in public and share the books, which Prabhupada treated as a service to others and a practice for oneself.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Succession and the zonal acharyas.** After his death the eleven disciples named in the July 1977 letter began initiating in their own right, each over a geographical zone, and received the elaborate honours Prabhupada had received, including a raised seat and daily worship. Critics inside the movement call the years from 1978 to the mid-1980s the zonal acharya period. Several of the eleven left or were removed. Jayatirtha left for the Gaudiya Math in 1982 and was expelled by the GBC that year. Kirtanananda Swami, head of the New Vrindaban community in West Virginia, was expelled by the GBC on 16 March 1987. He was indicted in federal court in 1990 on racketeering and mail-fraud charges and on charges of conspiring to murder two critics, convicted on nine of eleven counts in 1991 (the jury did not reach a verdict on the murder charges), had those convictions overturned on appeal, and in 1996 pleaded guilty to one racketeering count and was sentenced to twenty years. A reform movement led by Ravindra Svarupa Dasa and others from 1984 pressed the GBC to end the zonal system; by 1987 the number of initiating gurus had been widened and their ceremonial status reduced, under GBC oversight. Scholars and former members, among them Angela Burt and Henry Doktorski, have written book-length studies of the period.

**The ritvik dispute.** One group reads the July 1977 letter as appointing the eleven to initiate on Prabhupada's behalf permanently, so that every later initiate is his disciple. Its proponents, organised from the 1990s as the ISKCON Revival Movement, reject the post-1977 gurus. The GBC rejects the ritvik reading. The disagreement became tied to a long property case between ISKCON Bangalore, led by Madhu Pandit Dasa, and ISKCON (registered in Mumbai). In 2011 the Karnataka High Court ruled for ISKCON Mumbai. On 16 May 2025 the Supreme Court of India set aside that order and held that the Hare Krishna temple in Bengaluru belongs to the ISKCON Bangalore society. ISKCON stated that the case concerned property and never decided the ritvik question, and its India communications director said it would be <q>exploring all available legal remedies</q>. ISKCON Mumbai's review petition drew a split decision from two judges in late 2025 and was referred to the Chief Justice; as of October 2026 it was still pending before the Supreme Court.

**Abuse in the gurukulas.** ISKCON ran boarding schools, called *gurukulas*, from 1971, first in Dallas and later in Los Angeles, New Vrindaban, Seattle, Vrindavan and elsewhere, partly so that parents could work full-time on book distribution. In June 1998 ISKCON's own *ISKCON Communications Journal* published a study by the sociologist E. Burke Rochford, Jr., with Jennifer Heinlein, "Child Abuse in the Hare Krishna Movement: 1971–1986." It documented beatings, psychological abuse, neglect and sexual molestation, linked them to untrained staff and weak oversight, and said <q>no one knows how many of ISKCON's children were abused</q>. The movement set up a child protection office around the same time. On 12 June 2000 the Dallas lawyer Windle Turley filed suit in the US District Court in Dallas on behalf of 44 former students (later more than ninety), naming ISKCON, members of the GBC and Prabhupada's estate, and seeking $400 million. The federal court dismissed the federal racketeering claims in 2001, and the plaintiffs refiled in Texas state court. In 2002 eleven ISKCON temples and entities in California and West Virginia filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. Federal bankruptcy courts confirmed a reorganisation plan in May 2005 that set aside $9.5 million for claimants, about 450 of them in Hinduism Today's report and 535 in the Los Angeles Times's, with individual awards of up to $50,000. ISKCON's spokesman Anuttama Dasa said: <q>On behalf of our entire society, I apologize to these young people.</q> The allegations concerned the schools, their staff and the leaders responsible for them; Prabhupada's estate was named as a defendant alongside the institution.

**The poisoning allegation.** From about 1997 some former members argued, from whispered words on recordings made in his room in 1977 and later from tests on hair samples, that Prabhupada had been poisoned by people around him. No one has been charged with any crime in connection with his death. The GBC commissioned investigations in 1997 and 2001, and a GBC resolution of 2000 stated <q>there is no evidence at this time to support the allegations of poisoning</q>. In April 2025 ISKCON's communications office called the claims unfounded, citing forensic audio examination that it said did not support the allegation, its own reading of the disputed whisper as "the swelling is going down," testimony from his caregivers, his documented diabetes and renal failure, and doubts about hair testing. Proponents continue to dispute this.

**Statements on women, race and caste.** Transcripts of his conversations contain remarks about women's intelligence and independence, about Black people and Jews, and about democracy, that critics, including former members and some scholars, describe as misogynist and racist. Defenders inside ISKCON say these were informal remarks, drawn from the norms of his Bengali upbringing and from traditional texts, and that his practice was more egalitarian: he initiated women and men, gave women temple service, and gave brahmana status to converts of every race and background, which caste-orthodox Hindus criticised. The scholar Ekkehard Lorenz, in a 2004 Columbia University Press volume, traced his statements about women in his commentaries to their sources and found that most of them had no parallel in the earlier commentaries he drew on. In March 2000 the GBC passed a resolution in which its members <q>offer their humble apologies to the women of Srila Prabhupada's society</q> and called for equal facilities for women. His insistence that caste should follow quality and conduct, and not birth, is itself a position for which he was attacked from the other side.

**The texts after his death.** In 1983 the BBT published a revised *Bhagavad-gita As It Is* with many changes, which it describes as bringing the text closer to his original manuscript. Some followers regard the changes as unauthorised, and since 2001 the 1972 edition has also been reprinted under licence. Separately, in 2011 prosecutors in Tomsk, Russia, asked a court to list the Russian edition as extremist material. The court rejected the request on 28 December 2011, and an appeal court upheld that decision on 21 March 2012.

## Descendants

ISKCON is the main branch, governed by the GBC, with initiating gurus who are his disciples and their disciples. The Society says it has hundreds of temples, farms and restaurants worldwide; in India it is now led and funded largely by Indians, and speakers such as Gaur Gopal Das reach large audiences online. Several groups split away: the ritvik groups; ISKCON Bangalore and its associated Akshaya Patra midday-meal charity; and followers of Bhaktivedanta Narayana Maharaja (1921–2010), a disciple of Prabhupada's sannyasa guru who, by his followers' account, performed the rites at Prabhupada's burial and later gathered many of his Western disciples, a relationship the GBC has disputed. The wider Gaudiya Math branches in India, from which he came, have grown in the West partly through people he first brought to Krishna.
