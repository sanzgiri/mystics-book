<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> RAH-muh-KRISH-nuh. His childhood name was Gadadhar. Some accounts say the monk Totapuri gave him the name Ramakrishna at his initiation; others hold that his father already called him by it, and Ramakrishna himself remembered his father calling him "Ramakrishnababu." Devotees call him Thakur ("the Master" or "Lord") and add the title Paramahamsa, used for the highest class of renunciate.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Dakshineswar, on the east bank of the Ganges a few miles north of Calcutta, early in 1882. Mahendranath Gupta, a schoolteacher in his late twenties with an English education, is walking through the temple gardens with his friend Sidhu. It is a Sunday, a few days after the priest's birthday, and dusk. The music of the evening service is coming from the temples. They find a room full of people seated on the floor. A man sits on a wooden couch facing east, talking about God.</p>
<p>Gupta comes back a few days later, in the morning. The man on the couch, Ramakrishna, asks whether he believes in God with form or without form. Gupta, who has absorbed the reformed theism of the Brahmo Samaj, says, <q>Sir, I like to think of God as formless.</q> Ramakrishna answers, <q>Very good. It is enough to have faith in either aspect.</q> Then he adds, <q>Remember that God with form is just as true as God without form.</q></p>
<p>Gupta objects that the people worshipping the clay image of Kali in the temple next door are worshipping clay. Ramakrishna turns sharp. <q>That's the one hobby of you Calcutta people,</q> he says, <q>giving lectures and bringing others to the light!</q> Gupta went home and wrote it all down. He went on doing so for four years, and those notes became the main record of what Ramakrishna said.</p>

## Life

Gadadhar Chattopadhyay was born on 18 February 1836 in Kamarpukur, a village in the Hooghly district of Bengal, about sixty miles northwest of Calcutta. His parents, Khudiram Chattopadhyay and Chandramani Devi, were poor and pious Brahmins. The family deity was Raghubir, a form of Rama. Devotees tell of visions that came to both parents before his birth, including a dream his father had at Gaya in which Vishnu promised to be born as his son. The name Gadadhar, "bearer of the mace," is a name of Vishnu at Gaya.

His father died in 1843. The boy went to the village school, learned to read and write Bengali, and lost interest in the rest. He spent his time with wandering monks who stopped at the village rest house, learned the songs and stories of the religious plays, and painted and modelled clay images. The earliest of the ecstatic states that marked his life came when he saw a flight of white cranes against a dark rain cloud and fell unconscious in a field. The movement's chronology places this around 1842 or 1843, when he was about six; other accounts make him ten or eleven.

Around 1852 he went to Calcutta to help his elder brother Ramkumar, who ran a small Sanskrit school and served as a household priest. In 1855 a wealthy widow, Rani Rasmani, finished a large temple complex at Dakshineswar dedicated to Kali, the dark goddess Bengalis worship as the Mother. Rasmani belonged to a caste that orthodox Brahmins considered low, and priests were reluctant to serve her temple. Ramkumar agreed to officiate. The temple was dedicated on 31 May 1855. Within a year Ramkumar was dead, and Gadadhar, not yet twenty, became priest of the Kali shrine.

<aside class="note"><strong>Kali.</strong> The fierce dark goddess of Bengali Shakta worship, garlanded with skulls and standing on Shiva. Her devotees address her as Mother. In Ramakrishna's language she is God's power at work in the world, the same reality the Vedantins call Brahman when it is at rest.</aside>

What followed was, by his own later account, a period of desperate longing. He wanted to see the Mother as a living presence and could not bear that he had not. He wept on the riverbank, stopped eating and sleeping properly, and threw off his sacred thread and clothes during worship. He later told his disciples that one day, in despair, he seized the sword that hung in the shrine to end his life, and that the Mother appeared to him as an ocean of light. After that, according to the accounts of those who knew him, he worshipped the image as a living person, fed it, talked to it and laughed with it. Temple staff thought he had gone mad. Rasmani's son-in-law, Mathuranath Biswas, called Mathur Babu, who managed the estate, protected him and paid for much of what came next.

His family in Kamarpukur, alarmed at the reports, arranged a marriage in the hope of steadying him. In 1859 he married Saradamani, a girl of about five from the nearby village of Jayrambati. He was twenty-three. The marriage was never consummated, and Sarada later joined him at Dakshineswar as a young woman, living near him as his first disciple. In 1872 he performed a ritual worship of her as the Divine Mother in his room, the Shodashi puja. Under the name Sarada Devi she became, after his death, the mother figure of his order.

### The sadhanas

The years from about 1861 to 1874 are the part of his life most often cited when people say that all religions are one, and the evidence for each episode deserves to be stated plainly. Almost everything we know about them comes from what Ramakrishna told his disciples in the 1880s and from the biography his monastic disciple Swami Saradananda wrote between 1909 and 1919, *Sri Sri Ramakrishna Lilaprasanga* (in English, *Sri Ramakrishna, the Great Master*). Neither source is contemporary with the events.

<aside class="note"><strong>Sadhana.</strong> A course of spiritual discipline undertaken to reach a particular realization. A <i>bhava</i> is an emotional attitude toward God, such as that of a servant, a child, a parent or a lover, which a practitioner may cultivate deliberately.</aside>

**Tantra, from 1861.** That year a woman in ochre robes came to Dakshineswar by boat. She was a Brahmin from the Jessore district, learned in Tantric and Vaishnava texts, and she is remembered only by her title, the Bhairavi Brahmani. Saradananda's account makes her about forty; Nikhilananda says she was slightly over fifty. She told him she had been sent to teach him. Under her direction, by the standard account, he went through the sixty-four principal disciplines of the Tantric texts, spending about three days on each and finishing in 1863. The movement's biographies say that at her request Mathur convened a gathering of pandits, among them Vaishnavcharan and Gauri, who agreed with her that he was an incarnation of God. She stayed in the area for years, longer than any of his other teachers, and then left; where and when she died is unknown.

**Vaishnava devotion, 1864 and after.** Before the Bhairavi came, he had already worshipped Rama in the attitude of Hanuman, the devoted servant. Around 1864 a wandering Vaishnava monk named Jatadhari came to Dakshineswar carrying a small metal image of the child Rama, Ramlala. Ramakrishna took up the attitude of a parent toward the child, and the accounts say the image became a living boy to him; Jatadhari left it with him. He also cultivated *madhura bhava*, the attitude of a lover toward Krishna in the manner of Radha and the cowherd women, and Saradananda's biography says that for about six months he wore women's clothes and ornaments, which Mathur supplied, while he did so.

**Advaita, 1864 to 1865.** At the end of 1864 a naked wandering monk, probably from the Punjab, Totapuri, arrived at Dakshineswar. He belonged to the Puri order founded on Shankara's Advaita, and he had spent decades in meditation on the formless Brahman. He offered to teach Ramakrishna, who said he would need to ask the Mother. He then took formal *sannyasa*, renunciation, from Totapuri. Ramakrishna later described how he could not get past the form of Kali in meditation until Totapuri pressed a sliver of glass into the skin between his eyebrows and told him to fix his mind there; he cut the form in two with the sword of discrimination and passed into *nirvikalpa samadhi*, absorption without any object. Totapuri, who usually stayed no more than three days in one place, stayed about eleven months. The standard account ends with a reversal. Ill with dysentery and unable to meditate, Totapuri walked into the Ganges to drown himself, found himself on the far bank instead, and came back convinced that the Mother he had dismissed was the same as the Brahman he had meditated on all his life. After Totapuri left, by Ramakrishna's account, he remained in that absorbed state for some six months, kept alive by a monk who forced food into his mouth.

**Islam, 1866.** Toward the end of 1866 a Hindu who had become a Muslim and followed a Sufi path, Govinda Roy, was living in the temple garden. Ramakrishna asked him for instruction. For three days, by the accounts, he dressed as a Muslim, repeated the name of Allah, said the Islamic prayers, stopped visiting the temples and lived outside the temple precincts. Swami Nikhilananda, summarising the tradition, writes that he then <q>saw the vision of a radiant figure, perhaps Mohammed,</q> which merged into him. The hedge "perhaps" is in the source.

**Christianity, 1873 or 1874.** Nikhilananda dates this to November 1874; other accounts place it at the end of 1873. A devotee, Shambhu Charan Mallick, read to him from the Bible. Sitting in Jadu Mallick's garden house next to the temple at Dakshineswar, he fixed his gaze on a painting of the Madonna and Child and, by his account, was overwhelmed by it. On the fourth day, walking in the Panchavati grove, he saw a stranger with large eyes and fair skin, and a voice told him this was Christ. The figure, again, merged into him. A picture of Christ was kept in his room afterward.

He also spoke with respect of the Buddha, the Jain Tirthankaras and the Sikh Gurus, but there is no account of a formal discipline in any of those traditions.

### Calcutta comes to him

In 1875 (Wikipedia's article on Keshab gives 1876) Ramakrishna went to meet **Keshab Chandra Sen**, the most famous preacher of the Brahmo Samaj, at a garden house in Belgharia, a few miles from Dakshineswar. Nikhilananda reports that he opened by saying, <q>People tell me you have seen God; so I have come to hear from you about God.</q> The two became close, and Keshab and his circle spoke and wrote about the priest of Dakshineswar to Calcutta's English-educated public. Keshab's later teaching of the Motherhood of God and his New Dispensation of 1880–1881, which aimed to combine the world's religions, may owe something to Ramakrishna, though Keshab was already moving that way.

From about 1879 the young men who would become his monastic disciples began to arrive, along with householders, actors, scholars and the merely curious. William Hastie, principal of the General Assembly's Institution (now the Scottish Church College), is said to have told his students that if they wanted to understand the trance Wordsworth describes, they should visit Ramakrishna. One of those students was Narendranath Datta, later Swami Vivekananda, who first met him in November 1881 at a devotee's house in Calcutta and then came to Dakshineswar. Narendra asked whether he had seen God. Ramakrishna's reply, in the standard form, was <q>Yes, I have seen God. I see Him as I see you here, only more clearly.</q> Mahendranath Gupta came in February or March 1882 (Nikhilananda says March; Gupta's dated entries begin in late February).

In 1885 he developed a sore throat that turned out to be cancer. His disciples moved him first to a house at Shyampukur in north Calcutta and then, on 11 December 1885, to a garden house at Cossipore. The young disciples nursed him there, and during these months he gave ochre cloth to several of them, an act the Ramakrishna Order counts as the beginning of its monastic line. He died in the early hours of 16 August 1886, aged fifty. Accounts of his last word differ, and some give it as Kali.

## The teaching

Ramakrishna wrote nothing and gave no lectures. He talked, sang, danced and fell into trance in front of whoever was in the room, and he taught mainly through parables drawn from village life, such as the kite that rises when it drops the fish in its beak, the maidservant who looks after her master's children and knows they are not hers. Everything below comes from his disciples' records.

**God is real and can be seen.** The centre of his teaching is that God can be known directly, as plainly as one person sees another, and that a human life has no higher purpose. The way there is longing. He often said that a person who weeps for God with the intensity a mother feels for a lost child, or a miser for lost gold, will see God.

**Brahman and Shakti are one.** He refused the choice between the formless Absolute of Advaita and the personal Mother of devotion. When God is inactive, he said, we call it Brahman; when God creates, preserves and destroys, we call it Shakti or Kali. They are the same reality, as fire and its power to burn are one. This is why he told Gupta that God with form is as true as God without form. A seeker may begin with either. Modern scholars who take his thought seriously as philosophy, especially Ayon Maharaj (Swami Medhananda) in *Infinite Paths to Infinite Reality* (2018), call the state he valued most *vijnana*. In his usage it means a fuller knowledge that comes after the formless absorption of Advaita, in which the knower comes back to the world and sees the one reality as both personal and impersonal, present in everything.

<aside class="note"><strong>Vijnana.</strong> In ordinary Sanskrit, "discernment" or "specific knowledge." In Ramakrishna's talk it meant a realization richer than knowing Brahman alone: knowing that Brahman has also become the world and its creatures, so that the knower can live and love in it without forgetting God.</aside>

**As many faiths, so many paths.** The phrase most associated with him is the Bengali *yato mat, tato path*. In words Nikhilananda quotes in his introduction to the *Gospel*, he says he had practised all religions, Hinduism, Islam and Christianity, and found <q>that it is the same God toward whom all are directing their steps, though along different paths.</q> His most repeated image is a pond with several bathing places. Hindus draw from one and call it jal, Muslims from another and call it pani, Christians from a third and call it water; in his words, <q>The substance is One under different names.</q>

He did not draw from this the conclusion that a seeker should mix traditions. He told people to keep to their own path with full sincerity. What he condemned was the conviction that one's own path alone is true, a fault he called dogmatism and found among Brahmos, Christians and Hindu sectarians alike. His harmony also had a clear centre. The God every path reaches is the God he knew as Kali and as Brahman, and the evidence he offered was his own experience.

**Renunciation, and the householder.** He spoke constantly against *kamini-kanchan*, "woman and gold," by which he meant lust and greed, the two attachments he thought held a man away from God. He addressed this to the men around him, and for the young men he chose as monks he demanded complete renunciation. To householders he was gentler. They should live in the world like the maidservant, doing their duties with care and knowing that their real home lay elsewhere, and they should go into solitude now and then to pray and weep for God.

**The guru as mother.** He tested, teased and scolded his visitors and treated their temperaments as different paths. A devotee might worship God as a child, a friend or a lover; a philosopher like Narendra might reason his way to the formless. He fitted the instruction to the person.

I find Ramakrishna's originality in his method more than his doctrine. The equality of Brahman and Shakti was available to any Bengali Shakta who read the Tantras, and the idea that sages call one truth by many names is in the Rig Veda. What no one before him had done, as far as the record shows, was to set out deliberately to walk each path to its end and then report back. The report is short on the non-Hindu paths, and it is framed entirely in his own Hindu terms, but it gave Vivekananda something to stand on in Chicago in 1893. When the Ramakrishna movement says all religions are true, it is pointing to these years at Dakshineswar.

## Lineage

Ramakrishna had several teachers, each for a particular discipline, and no single guru in the usual sense. He was a Shakta devotee of Kali before any of them arrived, and he remained one after all of them left.

<MiniTree id="ramakrishna" />

The edges to the **Bhairavi Brahmani** (Tantric initiation, 1861) and **Totapuri** (sannyasa, 1864 or 1865 in different sources) are documented by his own later testimony and by Saradananda's biography; no independent record of either teacher survives. **Jatadhari** and **Govinda Roy** are known only through the same sources. The edge to **Keshab Chandra Sen** runs from Ramakrishna: Keshab and his colleague P. C. Mozoomdar acknowledged learning from him, and Mozoomdar credited the friendship with developing Keshab's idea of the Motherhood of God. In the other direction Keshab's role was publicity, bringing him to the notice of Calcutta's educated public. Below him, the edges to **Vivekananda**, **Sarada Devi** and **Mahendranath Gupta** are fully documented. The Ramakrishna Order, which Vivekananda organised, carries the lineage forward.

## The practice

Ramakrishna gave no single method. These are the instructions that recur most often in the *Gospel*, in the order he tended to give them to householders.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Chant the name and sing to God.</strong> He sang devotional songs daily and told visitors to repeat the name of God and clap their hands while doing so.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Cultivate longing.</strong> Pray with tears, as a child cries for its mother. He held that intense longing alone is enough.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Discriminate.</strong> Practise <i>viveka</i>, telling the real from the unreal, by reminding yourself that God alone is permanent.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Go into solitude from time to time.</strong> A few days away from family and work, to pray and reflect, even for householders.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Live in the world as a servant does in a rich house.</strong> Do every duty, and know it all belongs to God.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep to your own path.</strong> Choose the aspect of God that draws you, follow it sincerely, and do not condemn other paths.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

No recording of Ramakrishna exists; he died before sound recording reached Calcutta. A small number of photographs survive. His words come to us through *Sri Sri Ramakrishna Kathamrita*, which Mahendranath Gupta published in five Bengali volumes between 1902 and 1932 under the pen name "M." Gupta kept dated diary notes of his visits from February 1882 to April 1886 and wrote them out, as he said, from memory after returning home. Swami Nikhilananda's English translation, *The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna* (1942), carried a foreword by Aldous Huxley and is the version most English readers know. Saradananda's biography and earlier accounts by Ram Chandra Datta and others fill in the life.

## Critiques and questions

**Every source is a disciple.** Ramakrishna left no writing, and nearly everything about him was recorded by devotees from Calcutta's educated middle class. The historian Sumit Sarkar points out that the *Kathamrita* appeared fifteen to fifty years after the conversations it records, covers only about 186 days, and draws its air of authenticity from its careful dates; the full original diary has never been published. The account of the sadhanas rests largely on Saradananda, writing a generation later.

**The non-Hindu sadhanas.** The Islamic and Christian disciplines lasted, by the tradition's own account, about three or four days each, and both end with the founder of the other religion merging into Ramakrishna. Jeffrey Kripal has written that such an experience would have been heretical for most Muslims, and other critics have argued more generally that the harmony he taught places other religions inside a Hindu frame of reference. Defenders, notably Ayon Maharaj, argue that his model gives full reality to both personal and impersonal ultimates and so treats other faiths as more than partial versions of Vedanta. The question of whether his harmony is pluralist or inclusivist is still argued.

**Who made the Advaitin?** Walter Neevel (1976), Narasingha Sil and others have argued that his disciples, above all Vivekananda, presented him after his death as a calm Advaitin and played down the Tantric worship of Kali that dominated his own life. Sil argues that Vivekananda revised and mythologised his image. The Ramakrishna Order replies that his realization of Brahman under Totapuri is part of the record and that he himself insisted on both sides.

**Kali's Child.** In 1995 the University of Chicago Press published Jeffrey Kripal's *Kali's Child: The Mystical and the Erotic in the Life and Teachings of Ramakrishna*, which won the American Academy of Religion's History of Religions Prize for the best first book of that year. Reading the Bengali sources through psychoanalysis, Kripal argued that Ramakrishna's world was a Tantric one, that his mystical states were bound up with homoerotic desires he did not accept in himself and possibly with childhood trauma, and that the movement's English translations had softened or left out the passages of "secret talk." The historian Brian Hatcher has noted that most academic reviews were positive. In India the book caused deep offence, and attempts to have it banned in 1996 and 2001 failed. Swami Atmajnanananda (1997) and then Swami Tyagananda and Pravrajika Vrajaprana in *Interpreting Ramakrishna: Kali's Child Revisited* (2010) argued that key readings depended on mistranslating Bengali words, such as rendering *vyakulata*, longing, as "erotic torment" and treating the maternal "lap" as sexual, and that the Ramakrishna Order had not suppressed Ram Chandra Datta's early biography, which it had reprinted many times. Kripal corrected what he called minor translation errors in a 1998 second edition and maintained that most of his readings were legitimate. He acknowledged that he had overstated the suppression, denied that he had called Ramakrishna a homosexual or claimed that he abused children, and said he deeply regretted the offence to Hindus. Decades earlier, Romain Rolland had rejected the idea that his experiences were pathological.

## Descendants

His direct heir was **Swami Vivekananda**, who organised the young disciples into a monastic order, took the message of the harmony of religions to the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893, and founded the Ramakrishna Mission in 1897. **Sarada Devi** became the order's spiritual mother, and disciples such as Brahmananda and Saradananda led it after Vivekananda's death. Through his disciples his life reached Max Müller, who wrote *Ramakrishna: His Life and Sayings* (1898), Romain Rolland (1929), Aldous Huxley and Christopher Isherwood, whose *Ramakrishna and His Disciples* appeared in 1965. Monks of the order still teach today, among them **Swami Sarvapriyananda** in New York. More broadly, the conviction that all religions lead to one goal, which most of the Neo-Vedanta figures in this part share or argue with, entered modern Hindu thought chiefly through him.
