<section class="scene">
<p>Visitors in the late 1970s describe the same room. It is about eight feet by twelve, a mezzanine built into a flat in Khetwadi, a crowded neighbourhood of workshops and small traders in central Bombay, and it is reached by a steep ladder. People sit on the floor with their knees almost touching. On one wall hang photographs of the gurus of the lineage, garlanded with fresh flowers. Incense mixes with the smoke of bidis, the cheap leaf cigarettes the old man on the cushion sold for most of his working life and smokes while he talks.</p>
<p>A visitor from Europe asks a long question about meditation and the self. The old man listens, then cuts him short in Marathi, and an interpreter puts it into English. Before asking how to reach the Self, find out who is asking. What were you before you were born?</p>
<p>Some visitors left that room angry and did not come back. Others stayed for months, and a few went home and spent the rest of their lives teaching what they had heard there.</p>

## Life

Maruti Kambli was born in Bombay on 17 April 1897. The day was Hanuman Jayanti, the festival of the monkey god, so his parents named him Maruti, one of Hanuman's names. His father, Shivrampant, had worked as a domestic servant in the city and later moved the family to a small farm at Kandalgaon in the Konkan, the coastal strip south of Bombay. Maruti grew up there with two brothers and four sisters. The family belonged to the Varkari tradition, the Marathi devotional movement centred on the god Vithoba at Pandharpur, and the boy learned its songs early. He had little formal schooling.

After his father died, he followed his elder brother back to Bombay to support the family. The sources give the year variously as 1915, 1918 and 1920. He started as a junior clerk in an office, then opened a small shop selling bidis. The business grew into a modest chain of retail shops selling tobacco, clothes and household goods, with several dozen employees. In 1924 he married Sumatibai. They had three daughters and a son.

<aside class="note"><strong>Bidi.</strong> A thin cigarette of tobacco rolled in a tendu leaf and tied with thread. Cheaper than a cigarette, and the working man's smoke across India.</aside>

In 1933 a friend, Yashwantrao Baagkar, took him to hear Siddharameshwar Maharaj, a teacher of the Inchegiri line who visited Bombay to give talks. Maruti was thirty-six and went at his friend's urging. Siddharameshwar initiated him with a mantra and gave him an instruction he repeated for the rest of his life. "My Guru ordered me to attend to the sense 'I am' and to give attention to nothing else," he told an interviewer forty years later. "I just obeyed."

He practised in every free moment, in the shop and at home. He also began writing devotional poems, and was pleased with them. His guru told him to stop, because he was enjoying the poems too much. The pen name he had used for them, Nisargadatta, stayed with him. He later said it had taken about three years for the practice to bear fruit.

Siddharameshwar died on 9 November 1936. The next year Nisargadatta left his family and his shops and set out on foot as a wandering renunciant, meaning to go to the Himalayas. On the road he met a fellow disciple who argued that renunciation in the middle of an ordinary working life was the harder and better path. After about eight months he came home. Most of the business had collapsed while he was away. He kept one small shop and ran it for nearly thirty more years to support his family. His wife died in 1942 and a daughter in 1948.

For a long time he taught almost no one. In 1951, after what he described as an inner prompting from his guru, he began to give initiation. He retired from the shop in 1966 and from then on received visitors daily in the loft above his flat at Vanmali Bhavan on 10th Lane, Khetwadi. The routine there was fixed. There was morning meditation, devotional singing four times a day, and question-and-answer sessions in the morning and evening. Every morning he cleaned and garlanded the photographs of his gurus and performed the evening worship, the *arati*, in front of them. Visitors who came for Advaita were sometimes surprised to find themselves in a small Marathi temple.

The change in his life came through a Polish engineer. Maurice Frydman had come to India in the 1930s, lived for a time near Ramana Maharshi, became a friend of J. Krishnamurti, and took Indian citizenship. Around 1970 he began attending the talks in Khetwadi, recording them and translating them into English. The result, *I Am That*, published in 1973, collected 101 conversations from 1970 to 1972. It was edited with great care and reads as a single sustained argument. Within a few years Western seekers were climbing the ladder to the loft in numbers it could not hold. Nisargadatta complained that his home had turned into a railway platform. By the end of the decade visitors were limited to stays of a few weeks.

In 1980 he was diagnosed with cancer of the throat. He kept teaching, often for hours a day, as his voice grew hoarser. The talks of these last years were recorded and later edited by the American Jean Dunn as *Seeds of Consciousness*, *Prior to Consciousness* and *Consciousness and the Absolute*. He died at home on 8 September 1981.

## The teaching

Nisargadatta's teaching starts with the instruction he received in 1933 and moves, over forty years, a long way past it.

The starting point is simple. Everyone has the sense "I am." It is present before any thought about what or who you are, and it is the one thing you cannot doubt. Hold your attention on it. Whatever you can perceive, your body, your feelings, your thoughts, your history, is something you observe, and the observer cannot be any of these. As attention stays with the bare sense of being, the identifications that cover it fall away. "You are not what you take yourself to be," his guru had told him. "Find out what you are."

This much is close to Ramana's inquiry, and readers often treat the two as one teaching. The difference is in the instrument. Ramana asked where the "I" comes from. Nisargadatta asked his visitors to stay with the "I am" itself, as a felt fact, and to let it show them its own nature. He called this "meditating on the meditator."

In *I Am That* the "I am" is presented as the gateway to everything. In the last years the tone changes, and this later teaching is the part that is most his own.

He began to speak of the sense "I am" as a product. Consciousness, he said, depends on the body, and the body is made of food. When the food body is nourished, the sense of being appears, the way a flame appears where there is oil and a wick. When the body dies, the sense of being goes out. "I am" is therefore a temporary condition, a kind of illness that came on at birth, and the whole world you know appears inside it. What you really are comes before it, untouched by birth and death. He called this the Absolute, *Parabrahman*, and said it cannot be known as an object, because it is what is left when the knower and the known both subside.

On this reading the "I am" practice is a stage. You hold to the sense of being until you understand it completely, and in understanding it you see that you are prior to it. Visitors who had read *I Am That* and came expecting encouragement to dwell in pure being were sometimes told bluntly that being itself was a passing show.

He combined this severe metaphysics with practices he never gave up. He initiated people with the mantra of his line, sang devotional songs four times a day and spoke of his guru with complete devotion. When asked how this fitted with the teaching that the self is unreal, he said the singing and worship went on by themselves and did no harm.

### The manner

His style of teaching shaped the teaching itself. He took no interest in theory for its own sake and none in his visitors' spiritual histories. He refused to discuss methods at length, cut off long questions, contradicted people, shouted at them, and sometimes sent them away. Those who stayed describe the anger passing as quickly as it came, often followed by laughter. He smoked bidis through the sessions until his illness forced him to stop.

Of all the teachers in this part, Nisargadatta is the one whose ideas changed the most during his teaching life, and the one whose English reputation depends most on other people's editing. *I Am That* gave the world a calm, lucid sage. The tapes from 1978 to 1981 give us a dying man pushing his listeners past every position they tried to occupy, including the one *I Am That* had given them. The later teaching is harder to like. It is also the part no one had put in quite those terms before, and it explains why his descendants range from devotional teachers who still give his mantra to Western speakers who insist there is no one there to practise anything.

## Lineage

Nisargadatta's line, the Inchegiri Sampradaya, takes its name from a village in northern Karnataka. Its first great teacher, Bhausaheb Maharaj (1843–1914), was a disciple of Nimbargi Maharaj (1789–1875), a saint whose tradition drew on both Lingayat Shaivism and Marathi devotion. The line describes itself as a branch of the Navnath tradition, the "Nine Masters" of medieval yoga, though the early links are hard to document. Bhausaheb taught meditation on the divine name, which his followers called the "ant's way": slow, steady and sure.

Siddharameshwar, Bhausaheb's disciple, broke with this emphasis around 1920 and taught what he called the "bird's way," understanding the Self directly through inquiry and discrimination. Several fellow disciples opposed him. Nisargadatta inherited the bird's way, but he also kept the mantra, the songs and the worship of the ant's way, and he passed both on.

His descendants split roughly along that line. **Ramakant Maharaj**, initiated in 1962, teaches in Mumbai in the traditional way, with mantra and devotion. **Ramesh Balsekar**, the retired president of the Bank of India who interpreted for him from 1978, became a teacher himself and drew from the late talks a doctrine in which no individual exists to act and all events simply happen. Western teachers influenced by the books, such as Jean Dunn, Robert Powell, Alexander Smit and Stephen Wolinsky, carried different parts of the teaching into Europe and North America.

His fellow disciple **Ranjit Maharaj** (1913–2000), who had been with Siddharameshwar since boyhood, taught in Bombay in the same line and was visited by many of the same Westerners after 1981.

## The practice

As he gave it to visitors, the practice can be put in a few steps. He often refused to call it a practice at all.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Find the sense "I am."</strong> It is the plain feeling of existing, present before any thought about who you are.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Stay with it.</strong> Whenever attention drifts to a thought or a sensation, come back. Treat everything you can observe as an object, and so as something other than yourself.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Watch the "I am" itself.</strong> Notice that it comes in the morning and goes in deep sleep, and that it depends on the body being alive.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Ask what knows the "I am."</strong> Whatever is aware of the sense of being, even when it comes and goes, is closer to what you are.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep devotion if it is there.</strong> He told people who loved a mantra or a guru to continue. In his own life the singing never stopped.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Which Nisargadatta?** Readers of *I Am That* and readers of *Prior to Consciousness* meet two different teachers. Some see a natural deepening. Others see a man whose illness darkened his view of the body and of life. The editors' choices make the difference look sharper still, since Frydman shaped his material into polished essays while Dunn kept closer to the raw exchanges.

**Translation.** He spoke Marathi to people who mostly did not. Frydman, Balsekar, Saumitra Mullarpattan and others interpreted on the spot, and their vocabulary is now his vocabulary in English. Terms such as "beingness" and "the Absolute" are as much theirs as his.

**Fatalism.** Balsekar and several Western teachers took the late teaching to mean that there is no doer and so nothing to be done. Critics, including some within the lineage, point out that Nisargadatta himself sang, worshipped, initiated people and told them to practise, and that a teaching of no doer was never meant as permission for indifference.

**Harshness.** His temper was real. Some visitors were hurt by it. His admirers describe it as a tool; his critics as a temper.

## Descendants

He named no formal successor. His influence runs through **Ramakant Maharaj**, who continues the traditional lineage in Mumbai; through **Ramesh Balsekar** and the Western "non-duality" speakers who drew on Balsekar's reading; and above all through *I Am That*, which has stayed in print for fifty years and is often the first book of Indian spirituality a Western reader finds. With Ramana, he is one of the two fixed points of this part of the book.
