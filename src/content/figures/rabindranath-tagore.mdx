## Life

Rabindranath Tagore was born on 7 May 1861 in the family mansion at Jorasanko in north Calcutta, the youngest son of Debendranath Tagore, who led the Brahmo Samaj, the reformed Hindu society that worshipped one formless God through the Upanishads. Rabindranath grew up hearing Upanishadic verses at the family's services and later wrote hymns for the Brahmo congregation. One of them, "Bharoto Bhagyo Bidhata," supplied the words of India's national anthem.

In 1890 his father sent him to manage the family's estates in eastern Bengal, around Shilaidaha on the Padma river. He spent much of the following decade on a houseboat and in the estate house, and his wife and children joined him there in 1898. These were the years in which he came to know rural Bengal and its wandering singers, the Bauls. In 1901 he moved to Santiniketan, the retreat his father had founded in Birbhum, and opened an experimental school. Its foundation as a university, Visva-Bharati, was laid in 1918, and the university opened in 1921.

The Bengali *Gitanjali*, a book of devotional songs, appeared in 1910. Tagore made his own English prose versions of a selection in 1912, and in November 1913 he learned that he had been awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature. Lectures delivered in America were collected as *Sadhana: The Realisation of Life* (1913). In May 1930 he gave the Hibbert Lectures at Manchester College, Oxford, and published them in 1931 as *The Religion of Man*, with a conversation he had held with Albert Einstein in 1930 printed as an appendix. He died at Jorasanko on 7 August 1941.

## The teaching

Tagore did not write a system. His religious ideas run through songs, letters, plays and lectures, and he said more than once that he came to them as a poet. The core of them is that the infinite is met in the finite, and above all in human beings.

**The Lord of life.** In poems of the 1890s, most famously in the collection *Chitra* (1896), Tagore addressed a figure he called the *jivan devata*, the god of his own life. This presence was neither the remote Brahman of the philosophers nor a temple deity. It was a companion who shaped his life from within and used it, the way a musician uses an instrument, to express something larger than the poet. Tagore later described this sense of being used by a larger being as the root of his religion. Readers have argued ever since about whether the jivan devata is God, the poet's own deeper self, or a muse, and Tagore left the question open.

**The Man of my Heart.** The Bauls, wandering singers of rural Bengal drawn from both Hindu and Muslim backgrounds, sing about the *moner manush*, the Man of the Heart, a beloved who lives inside the body and cannot be found in temple or mosque. Tagore heard them at Shilaidaha, collected their songs and made the poet Lalon Fakir, who died in 1890, known to educated Bengal. In "An Indian Folk Religion" (1922) he wrote that the phrase meant for him that "the supreme truth of all existence is in the revelation of the Infinite in my own humanity." He made the same point in *The Religion of Man*, where the Baul chapter stands at the center of the book.

**God's humanity and man's divinity.** *The Religion of Man* turns these intuitions into an argument about evolution. Tagore accepted Darwin's account of life, but read it as the work of a shaping spirit. Life evolves bodies; in human beings it begins to evolve mind and spirit. Each person feels a surplus, a part of the self that exceeds what survival requires and reaches toward a universal humanity, which Tagore called the Universal Man or the Supreme Person. Religion, art and science are the ways this surplus works itself out. God, in this view, becomes known in human history and human relationships, and humanity grows toward God as it widens its sympathies beyond race and nation. He set this apart from the Vedantic experience of formless Brahman, which he did not deny but treated as lying outside religion as he understood it.

The conversation with Einstein, printed at the end of the book, shows the position under pressure. Tagore argued that truth and beauty have no meaning apart from human consciousness; Einstein held that truth would exist even if there were no humans. Neither persuaded the other.

**Disputes with Gandhi.** Tagore's humanism put him at odds with Gandhi more than once. In 1934, when Gandhi called the Bihar earthquake a divine punishment for untouchability, Tagore rebuked him in public for attributing a physical catastrophe to moral causes. The exchange shows how far Tagore's God was from a judge who intervenes in nature.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="rabindranath-tagore" />

Tagore's descent is through his family and his reading, never through initiation. The documented line runs from the Upanishads through Rammohan Roy's Brahmo Sabha to his father Debendranath, in whose services he grew up. The Baul tradition reached him at Shilaidaha through the singers themselves and through collectors such as Kshitimohan Sen, who also supplied the Kabir texts behind *One Hundred Poems of Kabir* (1914), translated by Tagore with Evelyn Underhill. Tagore admired Aurobindo, saluted him in the poem "Namaskar" when Aurobindo was on trial in 1907, and visited him in Pondicherry on 29 May 1928, but neither shaped the other's teaching. Tagore had no successors as a teacher; Visva-Bharati is his institutional heir.
