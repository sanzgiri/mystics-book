## Life

Krishna Menon was born on 8 December 1883 at Cherukulathu House in Peringara, near Tiruvalla, in the princely state of Travancore, now part of Kerala. He studied law and joined the state service, rising to Government Advocate and later to Inspector and District Superintendent of Police. He stayed in government service until 1939. His eldest son, K. Padmanabha Menon, later carried his teaching forward, and the sources read for this entry say little more about his family. He used to say that police work was a good setting for spiritual practice, and one account has him describing his working life as offering "the maximum temptations and obstacles."

As a young man he practised devotion, yoga and philosophical study. He prayed, by the same account, to meet an authentic teacher. In 1919 he met, in Calcutta, a monk, Swami Yogananda, an obscure figure unrelated to the author of *Autobiography of a Yogi*; Stillness Speaks says the meeting lasted one night, and Wikipedia calls it day-long. Krishna Menon later said the meeting "paralyzed my ego." He went on practising, and in 1923 he took the name Shri Atmananda and began to teach. His students called him Gurunathan. Philip Renard, in his portrait, tells how the noise of a horse and carriage once disturbed his meditation. He moved to sit elsewhere, then reconsidered and called the noises in: "Come in, come in, come in! All disturbances: come in, come in!"

After retiring he lived at Anandawadi, his family home at Malakkara on the Pampa river, and taught there and in Trivandrum. Wikipedia reports that the comparative mythologist Joseph Campbell sought him out on a trip to India in the 1950s. He wrote in Malayalam and English. Wikipedia dates his early devotional poem *Radhamadhavam* to 1919 and a sequel, *Atmaramam*, to 1935. *Atma Darshan* appeared in Malayalam in 1945 and in English in 1946, and *Atma Nirvriti* followed in 1951 and 1952. A student, S. Balakrishna Pillai, writing as Nitya Tripta, kept notes of his talks from 1950 to 1959, published in 1963. His son compiled a further volume of talks from tape recordings, published in 1973. Atmananda died in Trivandrum on 14 May 1959, aged seventy-five.

## The teaching

Atmananda called his method the direct path. He contrasted it with the cosmological path, the long route of most traditions, which begins with the world, God and the soul and works slowly inward. The direct path starts from the one certainty in every experience, the knowing of it.

It works in two stages. First, you separate consciousness from its objects. Every object, a chair, a sensation, a thought, is known, and what knows it is not another object. The student learns to rest as this knowing, which Atmananda called the witness. Second, you examine the objects themselves and find that nothing of them can be found apart from the knowing. "Existence has the chair," he said: what is real in the chair is the existence and consciousness through which it appears. At that point the witness itself dissolves, and only consciousness remains.

An online summary of his discourses breaks this into eight stages. The first sets the universal against the individual and the cosmological approach against the direct one. The second begins from the three ordinary states of waking, dream and deep sleep. The third turns attention to the "I" and arrives at "I am consciousness." The fourth watches thoughts and finds in them change against a changeless background. The fifth sees existence behind objects, the sixth recognises that happiness comes from the real "I" and not from objects, and the seventh names the background in which all experience arises, stays and subsides. The eighth merges back, which the summary renders as "Sleep in consciousness."

His tools were questions and close reasoning, which he called higher reason to distinguish it from argument for its own sake. A typical question asked what is present between two thoughts. He rendered the old triad *sat-chit-ananda*, in Renard's report, as existence, knowledge and peace, and defined peace as happiness without interruption.

He taught householders and had no ashram in the usual sense. His teaching kept to the path of knowledge, and he criticised devotion and yoga as methods, although he had practised both himself and had written devotional poetry, and his students describe deep love for him as their guru. N. Narayana Pillai, whose study of him appeared in 2019, notes that he taught through personal contact and did not set out to institutionalise his approach or to publicise himself.

The eight-stage summary arranges familiar Advaita material into a sequence a seeker could take one step at a time, beginning from ordinary experience and needing no preliminary course in yoga or ritual. The claim that objects have no reality apart from consciousness is classical Advaita. The ordering is his own.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="atmananda-krishna-menon" />

Atmananda's influence in India passed through his son **K. Padmanabha Menon** (Sri Adwayananda), who taught at Anandawadi until 2001, and through students such as **Nitya Tripta**. In the West it travelled through the Englishman **John Levy**, who studied with him, published his work and wrote *The Nature of Man According to Vedanta*, through the Dutch teacher **Wolter Keers**, who also studied with Ramana Maharshi, and through Ananda Wood, a direct disciple who has written about him. **Jean Klein** is usually placed in this lineage, although the teacher Klein himself named was in Bangalore. Through Klein, the direct path reached **Francis Lucille** and **Rupert Spira**, who teach it today in Europe and America. Wikipedia names Klein, Lucille, Spira and Stephan Bodian as prominent members of this lineage.

## The practice

Atmananda left no daily routine for students to follow. The steps below restate the stages of the direct path as an exercise a reader could try, using the eight-stage summary cited in the references.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Start from the three states.</strong> Take waking, dream and deep sleep, and ask what is present in all of them.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Turn to the "I."</strong> Let any experience, a sound, a sensation, a thought, be known, and rest in what knows it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Watch change and the changeless.</strong> Notice that thoughts come and go, and ask what is present between two thoughts.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>See existence behind objects.</strong> Look at an ordinary object and notice that its being is not separate from the knowing of it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Notice where happiness comes from.</strong> The summary has him locating happiness in the real "I" and not in objects or the mind, so ask where it appears to come from and what is present when it does.</div></li>
</ol>
