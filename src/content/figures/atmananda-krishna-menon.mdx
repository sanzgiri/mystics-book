## Life

Krishna Menon was born on 8 December 1883 at Peringara, near Tiruvalla, in the princely state of Travancore, now part of Kerala. He studied law and joined the state service, rising to Government Advocate and later to Inspector and District Superintendent of Police. He held that post until he retired in 1939, and he used to say that police work, with its pressures and temptations, was a good setting for spiritual practice.

As a young man he practised devotion, yoga and philosophical study, and searched for a teacher. In 1919 he spent a single night with a wandering monk, Swami Yogananda of Calcutta, an obscure figure unrelated to the author of *Autobiography of a Yogi*. Krishna Menon later said the meeting "paralysed my ego." He went on practising for four more years, and in 1923 he took the name Shri Atmananda and began to teach. His students called him Gurunathan.

After retiring he lived at Anandawadi, his family home at Malakkara on the Pampa river, and taught there and in Trivandrum. He wrote in Malayalam and English. *Atma Darshan* appeared in 1945 in Malayalam and in English the next year, and *Atma Nirvriti* followed in 1951–1952. A student, S. Balakrishna Pillai, writing as Nitya Tripta, kept notes of his talks from 1950 to 1959, published in 1963. Atmananda died in Trivandrum on 14 May 1959.

## The teaching

Atmananda called his method the direct path. He contrasted it with what he called the cosmological path, the long route of most traditions, which begins with the world, God and the soul and works slowly inward. The direct path starts with the one thing that is certain in every experience, the knowing of it.

It works in two stages. First, you separate consciousness from its objects. Every object, a chair, a sensation, a thought, is known, and what knows it is not another object. The student learns to rest as this knowing, which Atmananda called the witness. Second, you examine the objects themselves and find that nothing of them can be found apart from the knowing. "Existence has the chair," he said: what is real in the chair is the existence and consciousness through which it appears. At that point the witness itself dissolves, and only consciousness remains.

His tools were questions and close reasoning, which he called "higher reasoning" to distinguish it from argument for its own sake. "Where are you between two thoughts?" was a typical question. He rendered the old triad *sat-chit-ananda* as existence, consciousness and peace, and defined peace as happiness without interruption.

He taught householders and had no ashram in the usual sense. In his own teaching he set aside the devotion and yoga he had once practised, and he said so plainly.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="atmananda-krishna-menon" />

Atmananda's influence in India passed through his son **K. Padmanabha Menon** (Sri Adwayananda), who taught at Anandawadi until 2001, and through students such as **Nitya Tripta**. In the West it travelled through the Englishman **John Levy**, who studied with him and published his work, and through the Dutch teacher **Wolter Keers**. **Jean Klein** is often placed in this lineage; Klein named a different teacher in Bangalore as his own guru, and the connection is disputed. Through Klein, the direct path reached **Francis Lucille** and **Rupert Spira**, who teach it today in Europe and America.
