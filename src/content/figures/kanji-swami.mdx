## Life

Kanji Swami was born in Umrala, in the Kathiawar peninsula of Gujarat, to a Sthanakvasi family. Wikipedia and the SOAS research page give 1890, with 21 April in Wikipedia; two other accounts give 1889, one of them as Vikram Samvat 1946, a year that overlaps both. His mother died when he was thirteen and his father when he was seventeen. In 1913 he was initiated as a Sthanakvasi monk by Hirachand. The Sthanakvasis are a Svetambara tradition that rejects temple images and wears a cloth over the mouth, the *muhpatti*, to avoid harming small life.

About 1921 he read Kundakunda's *Samayasara*, a Prakrit text of the Digambara tradition on the nature of the self, and by his followers' account it changed his life. He went on to study the works of the Digambar scholar Todarmal and of Shrimad Rajchandra. For some years, according to one summary, he remained a Sthanakvasi monk in outward form while holding Digambar views.

On Mahavir Jayanti in 1934, at Songadh in Saurashtra, he put aside the *muhpatti* and declared himself a Digambar layman. The Sthanakvasi community reacted with anger, and one summary says he took shelter in a house owned by a follower. He never took Digambar monastic initiation and remained a lay teacher, though he was celibate. In 1937 his followers built the Digambar Svadhyaya Mandir (study hall) at Songadh, which became the centre of the movement. In a 1977 interview, reported on Wikipedia, he denied hostility to traditional monasticism, and he consecrated Jain images, though the Sthanakvasi tradition he came from rejects them. He died in Bombay on 28 November 1980.

## The teaching

Kanji Swami taught that the soul is by nature pure, a knower, and separate from body, mind and karma. The path begins with discerning this "true pure knowledge self" from "the other," in the summary on Wikipedia, and everything else in Jain practice depends on it. Right faith, *samyag-darshana*, is the first direct experience of that soul, and conduct that precedes it has no standing. Wikipedia reports him as saying that vows, giving and fasting were ultimately worthless if performed without any understanding of the soul. One account of his teaching quotes him as saying that "For those who are engaged in personal effort to secure salvation, there are not many lives to live."

His method rests on a Jain pair of viewpoints. *Nishchaya naya* describes things as they are in their own nature, and *vyavahara naya* describes them as they appear in ordinary life. Kanji Swami gave priority to the first, and one scholarly summary says he went further here than Kundakunda himself. Many of his followers took lifelong celibacy vows while remaining householders, which the SOAS research project reads as a turn from institutional monasticism to individual effort.

In 1943 he published a commentary on Rajchandra's *Atmasiddhi*, and his movement counts Rajchandra among its influences. Rajchandra died in 1901, when Kanji Swami was about eleven, so the link is through texts. Overseas the two groups have been converging, according to Bauer, cited on Wikipedia.

## Critiques and questions

**The Sthanakvasi break.** The Sthanakvasi community objected to his teaching and to his departure in 1934. Summaries add that local Sthanakvasis later reconciled with him.

**One-sidedness.** Neerah Jain, cited on Wikipedia, argues that the movement's weight on *nishchaya naya* produced a sharp division within Jainism in northern and central India, because it conflicts with *anekantavada*, the Jain principle that truth has many sides. Followers and sympathetic scholars read the same emphasis as a recovery of Kundakunda's own priority. Within the Digambar community his teaching on causation, the relation of the external cause (*nimitta*) to the thing's own material cause (*upadana*), drew criticism; a 2023 SOAS Jaina Studies workshop on him included a paper revisiting those criticisms. Detailed Digambar responses are reported in few English sources.

**Past lives.** He spoke of having been present in a previous life when Kundakunda visited Mahavideha, a realm Jains place beyond this world, and received teaching from the Jina Simandhara. Wikipedia says the idea was suggested to him by Campabahen Mataji, a woman disciple who said she had been there too. Balcerowicz, cited there, notes that the Kundakunda story is first attested in Jayasena (c. 1150 to 1200). Gujarati Svetambaras criticised the claims about his next birth, according to one summary of Dundas.

**Standing.** Dundas, summarised in the same source, held that much of Jainism's future may lie with the Panth outside India. The SOAS Centre of Jaina Studies says that no English-language academic study of Kanji Swami had been done by 2023, and a first article appeared that year.

## Lineage

Kanji Swami stands under the Jain tradition, with Sthanakvasi training and Digambar texts as his sources. No living teacher gave him the doctrine.

The edge from Rajchandra is one of influence through writings. The sources attest it, and no meeting took place. Campabahen (1914 to 1990, by the movement's own dating) was one of his disciples, and one summary describes her as having led the Panth.
