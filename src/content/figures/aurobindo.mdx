<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Aurobindo is the Bengali form of the Sanskrit <i>aravinda</i>, the lotus. Sri is an honorific. His English records spell him Aravinda or Aurobindo Ghose, and the later books all carry the name Sri Aurobindo.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Alipore jail, outside Calcutta, 1908. A thirty-five-year-old journalist and former college principal is awaiting trial for conspiracy to wage war against the King. The police say a bomb factory was run from a garden house that belonged to him and his brothers. He has been held since May, much of the time alone, and he is reading the Bhagavad Gita.</p>
<p>A year later, released, he told a crowd at Uttarpara what had happened to him there. He looked at the walls, he said, and "it was no longer by its high walls that I was imprisoned; no, it was Vasudeva who surrounded me." He walked under the tree in front of his cell, "but it was not the tree." He saw Krishna in the warders and in the other prisoners: "it was Narayana whom I found in these darkened souls and misused bodies."</p>
<p>The speech is his first public account of the turn in his life. Much of what he taught later, about a Divine present in matter and waiting to emerge, goes back to that year.</p>
</section>

## Life

Aurobindo Ghose was born in Calcutta on 15 August 1872. His father, Krishna Dhun Ghose, was a doctor. His mother, Swarnalata Devi, was a daughter of Rajnarayan Bose, a Brahmo writer and early Bengali nationalist. The family were Kayasthas from Konnagar, on the Hooghly. Aurobindo was the third of the sons. He was sent first to the Loretto Convent School in Darjeeling, and in 1879, aged seven, he and his two elder brothers were taken to England so that they could grow up without Indian influences and enter the Indian Civil Service.

In Manchester the boys lodged with the Reverend W. H. Drewett and his family. Aurobindo was taught at home. In September 1884 he entered St Paul's School in London, where he became a strong classicist, and in October 1890 he went up to King's College, Cambridge, on a scholarship. In May 1892 he took a First in Part One of the Classical Tripos. He had already passed the written Civil Service examination, eleventh of 250 candidates, but in November 1892 he was disqualified for failing the riding test. Most biographers read the failure as deliberate. At Cambridge he had joined a small Indian student society called the Lotus and Dagger, whose members swore to work for India's freedom, and he had begun learning Bengali.

He sailed for India on 12 January 1893 and landed at the Apollo Bunder in Bombay on 6 February. In later accounts he said that a "vast calm" descended on him as he stepped ashore. His father was not there to meet him. According to the family's account, Krishna Dhun had been told that his son was aboard a steamer wrecked off Portugal, and he died soon after hearing the news, a few weeks before Aurobindo landed.

For thirteen years he worked for the Maharaja of Baroda, Sayajirao Gaekwad, first in the revenue and secretariat offices, then as a teacher of French and English at Baroda College, where he rose to vice-principal. He taught himself Sanskrit and Bengali, read the Upanishads, the Gita and the Mahabharata, and wrote poetry in English. On 30 April 1901, in Calcutta, he married Mrinalini Bose, who was fourteen. They lived together very little. She died in Calcutta in the influenza epidemic, on 17 December 1918.

In Baroda he also began to write anonymous articles attacking the moderate leaders of the Indian National Congress, and he made contact with secret revolutionary groups in western India and Bengal. His younger brother Barindra (Barin) became a recruiter for them in Calcutta. Around 1904 he began to practise yoga on his own, starting with breath control, guided only by a few rules from a friend.

The partition of Bengal in 1905 brought him to Calcutta. In August 1906 he became principal of the new Bengal National College and joined the daily *Bande Mataram* as an assistant editor, soon its main voice. His editorials called for complete independence and for boycott of British goods and institutions. They also spoke of the nation as a mother and a goddess. The historian Barbara Southard has shown how he and other militant leaders drew on Shakta imagery and presented the nation as an incarnation of Kali.

In December 1907 the Congress split at Surat, with Aurobindo on the extremist side. Barin then brought him to a Maharashtrian yogi, Vishnu Bhaskar Lele. In January 1908 they sat together for three days in a house in Baroda. Lele's instruction, as Aurobindo recalled it, was "Sit in meditation, but do not think, look only at your mind." Lele told him that thoughts would come into the mind from outside, and that he should throw them away. Aurobindo wrote that his mind "became silent as a windless air on a high mountain summit" and that within three days he was free of thought. He described the experience as the silent Brahman of Advaita: the world seemed empty and unreal, an appearance against an infinite stillness. Lele soon wanted to guide him further. Aurobindo declined, saying he "had received the command from within that a human Guru was not necessary for me."

On 30 April 1908 Khudiram Bose threw a bomb at a carriage in Muzaffarpur meant for the magistrate Douglas Kingsford, and killed two Englishwomen travelling in it, Mrs Kennedy and her daughter. In the early morning of 2 May the Calcutta police raided the family's garden house at Muraripukur Road in Maniktala and arrested Barin and thirteen others, and arrested Aurobindo at 48 Grey Street. He was charged with the rest. The trial before the Additional Sessions Judge C. P. Beachcroft ran for 131 days. Chittaranjan Das defended him. On 6 May 1909 Beachcroft acquitted him. Barin was sentenced to death, a sentence reduced on appeal.

The year in Alipore, mostly in solitary confinement, was the turn described in the scene above. He came out with a second realisation to set beside the silence of Baroda. The empty Brahman now appeared to him as the Divine present in all things. In his own account, the voice of Vivekananda, dead six years, spoke to him in meditation for about two weeks in the jail and pointed him toward planes of consciousness above ordinary mind. Devotees treat this as literal; he wrote of it as an experience, without explanation.

At Uttarpara, on 30 May 1909, he told his audience that "it is the Sanatana Dharma which for us is nationalism." From June 1909 he edited a new English weekly, *Karmayogin*, and soon afterwards a Bengali one, *Dharma*. In February 1910, hearing that he would be arrested again, he left Calcutta for Chandernagore, a French enclave on the Hooghly, and on 4 April 1910 he landed at Pondicherry, another French territory on the Tamil coast. A British warrant for his arrest, issued that April, could not reach him there. He never returned to British India, and he declined later invitations to return to political leadership.

For four years he lived quietly in Pondicherry with a few young Bengali companions, practising yoga and keeping a diary of his experiences, later published as *Record of Yoga*. On 29 March 1914 a French couple called on him, Paul Richard and his wife Mirra. Mirra Alfassa, born in Paris on 21 February 1878, had trained as a painter at the Académie Julian, studied occultism with Max Théon in Tlemcen, Algeria, in 1905 and 1906, and led a small group of seekers in Paris. By the ashram's account she "at once recognised" him as the guide she had met inwardly for years. With Paul Richard he started a monthly review, *Arya*, whose first issue appeared on 15 August 1914, his forty-second birthday.

*Arya* ran until January 1921. Almost everything Aurobindo is known for appeared in it first, in monthly instalments written straight for the press: *The Life Divine*, *The Synthesis of Yoga*, *Essays on the Gita*, *The Secret of the Veda*, *The Human Cycle*, *The Ideal of Human Unity*, and *The Foundations of Indian Culture*. The war took the Richards back to France in 1915 and then to Japan. Mirra returned to Pondicherry alone on 24 April 1920 and stayed.

A household of disciples gathered round them. On 24 November 1926, after an evening meditation, Aurobindo withdrew into seclusion. His followers call it the Day of Siddhi, the day of accomplishment, and say it marked "the descent of Krishna, the Overmind Godhead, into the physical." From then on he gave the whole charge of the community to Mirra, whom he now called the Mother, and the Sri Aurobindo Ashram dates its founding from that month. For twenty-four years he saw his disciples only on a few darshan days each year, sitting beside the Mother while they filed past. He spent his nights writing letters, thousands of them, answering disciples' questions about their practice in close detail, and revising *Savitri*.

On 24 November 1938 he fell and broke his right thigh. The accident widened his circle a little, since a few attendants now looked after him and recorded his conversations. In the Second World War he publicly supported the Allies, and in 1942 he urged the Congress to accept the Cripps proposals. When India became independent on 15 August 1947, on his seventy-fifth birthday, All India Radio broadcast a message from him. It named five dreams, ending with "a step in evolution which would raise man to a higher and larger consciousness," and said of the partition that "the division must go."

He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1943 and for the Peace Prize in 1950. In April 1950 the Magnum photographer Henri Cartier-Bresson was allowed to photograph him, at a darshan and in his room; they are among the last pictures taken. He died in the early hours of 5 December 1950, aged seventy-eight, of kidney failure. Thousands filed past the body; Pavitra, a senior disciple, wrote on 14 December of several thousand, and later accounts give about sixty thousand. Disciples recorded that it showed no sign of decay for more than four days, and on 9 December it was laid in a vault in the ashram courtyard. The Mother led the ashram until her own death on 17 November 1973. In 1968 she founded Auroville, an international township a few miles north of Pondicherry.

<aside class="note"><strong>Supermind.</strong> Aurobindo's English word for a consciousness above mind that knows by identity, holding the One and the many together without dividing them. He linked it to the Vedic <i>ritam</i>, the truth-order, and to the Upanishadic <i>vijnana</i>.</aside>

## The teaching

Aurobindo's starting point was a disagreement with both sides of his education. The European science he had grown up with treated matter as the only reality and consciousness as its late byproduct. The Indian schools he came to in Baroda, and Shankara's Advaita in particular, treated the Absolute as the only reality and the world as an illusion to escape. Two early chapters of *The Life Divine* are titled "The Materialist Denial" and "The Refusal of the Ascetic," and the book sets out to show that both had seized half the truth.

**Involution and evolution.** His answer begins with a cosmology. Before anything evolves, he argued, the Divine has descended into the forms of the world and hidden itself there. He called this descent involution. Spirit, which he described in Vedantic terms as Sachchidananda (existence, consciousness and bliss), steps down through a series of planes until it reaches its own apparent opposite, inert matter, the Inconscient. Evolution is the return journey. Life emerges out of matter because it was secretly there already; mind emerges out of life for the same reason. In the first chapter of *The Life Divine* he puts it plainly: "Life is already involved in Matter and Mind in Life." Darwin had shown the order of the steps. Aurobindo claimed to show why the steps happen, and where they are going.

**Man as a transition.** If life and mind were folded into matter, so is what lies above mind. The next step in evolution would bring out a new principle of consciousness, which he called the Supermind, and with it a new kind of being. "Man is a transitional being; he is not final," he wrote. The human mind divides and approximates. It knows the world by looking at it from outside, piece by piece. The Supermind knows by identity, seeing the whole and its parts at once, and would act without the ignorance and division that run through human life. Between mind and Supermind he mapped intermediate planes, in rising order Higher Mind, Illumined Mind, Intuition and Overmind. In his scheme the Overmind is the plane of the cosmic gods of the religions. It is vast and luminous, and it still divides the One into many separate truths.

**Spiritual evolution as a collective event.** Earlier Indian liberation was, in his reading, an individual escape from the round of birth into an Absolute untouched by the world. He wanted the Divine to manifest here, in a body, in society, on earth. If it be true that spirit is involved in matter, he wrote, then "the manifestation of the divine in himself and the realisation of God within and without are the highest and most legitimate aim possible to man upon earth." Liberation stays necessary, as a first step. He had had the silent Brahman experience himself and called it real. He regarded it as a foundation for the work that follows. The aim of his yoga was a life divine on earth, and eventually a transformed earthly nature, down to the cells of the body.

**The triple transformation.** In the late chapters of *The Life Divine* he described the change in three movements. The first is psychic. Behind the surface personality, he taught, each person has a soul that evolves from life to life, which he called the psychic being (in Sanskrit, *chaitya purusha*). Once it comes forward it begins to guide the mind, the emotions and the body from within. The second is spiritual: the opening upward to the higher planes, so that their peace, light and force descend into the whole being. The third is supramental, the descent of the Supermind itself, which would change even the body.

**Integral Yoga.** He called his path *purna yoga*, integral or complete yoga. *The Synthesis of Yoga* takes the classical paths of works, knowledge and devotion from the Gita, and the yoga of self-perfection he associated with Tantra, and tries to combine them, keeping what each offers and leaving out its exclusions. Its opening chapter states the principle: "All life is Yoga." Practice does not require leaving the world. Work, relationships and art all become fields of the yoga. There are no fixed techniques, no mantra given to everyone, and no single posture or breathing method. The effort of the seeker and the grace of the Divine work together, and in *The Mother* (1928) he names the seeker's part as aspiration, rejection and surrender.

**The Mother.** In the ashram's teaching the Mother has a central place. Aurobindo wrote that the Divine Shakti, the conscious force behind creation, works through her, and from 1926 he directed disciples to her for everything. Disciples regard the two as one consciousness in two bodies. Scholars of the movement agree, at least, that from 1926 onward the daily practice of the yoga, and much of its later development, came from her.

**The Veda read again.** Aurobindo also offered a new reading of the oldest Indian texts. In *The Secret of the Veda* he argued that the Rig Veda hymns are a psychological and spiritual code, in which cows stand for rays of light and knowledge, horses for force, and the sacrifice for the inner offering of the self. He credited Dayananda Saraswati with "the first discoverer of the right clues," while going his own way.

**Savitri.** His long poem *Savitri: A Legend and a Symbol* turns a three-hundred-verse episode from the Mahabharata, in which a princess follows her husband's soul to Death and wins him back, into an epic of nearly 24,000 lines of blank verse in twelve books and forty-nine cantos. He drafted it from 1916 and kept revising it until his death. Savitri becomes the divine Mother incarnate, who confronts Death on behalf of the world, and the poem is the most personal statement of his hope that death itself can be conquered. Disciples read it as scripture.

I give this verdict knowing how much he drew from older sources, and he was open about it. Every part of the system has an Indian ancestor, and some have European cousins, Hegel and Bergson among them, though he denied that Hegel influenced him. What I cannot find before him is the whole system, in which a Vedantic Absolute descends into matter in order to climb back, and a yoga exists to help the climb. Whether the Supermind exists is beyond what this book can judge, and my verdict concerns only the shape of the thought.

## Lineage

Aurobindo had no guru in the formal sense and founded no succession. He sits in the tree as a confluence of texts, two decisive encounters, and one collaborator.

<MiniTree id="aurobindo" />

The documented edges run from the Vedic hymns, the Upanishads and the Gita, all of which he translated or wrote commentaries on. His debt to Dayananda is in his own essay. The meeting with Lele in January 1908 is documented in his letters and was brief; he did not accept Lele as guru. Vivekananda's influence is partly literary and partly, by Aurobindo's own testimony, an experience in Alipore jail. He reacted against Shankara's Advaita, with which much of *The Life Divine* argues, while keeping its realisation as a stage. The edge to Mirra Alfassa, the Mother, is the strongest in his tree: from 1920 they lived and worked together, and the ashram and Auroville were built by her. Sri Chinmoy, who grew up in the ashram from 1944, is the best-known teacher shaped by it.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Aspiration.</strong> A steady call from within toward the Divine, kept up, in his phrase, as "an aspiration vigilant, constant, unceasing."</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Rejection.</strong> Noticing desires, fears and habits of the "lower nature" as they rise and refusing them assent, without suppressing them by force.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Surrender.</strong> Offering "oneself and all one is and has" to the Divine, including work, feelings and the body, so that the higher force can act.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Finding the psychic being.</strong> Turning inward, often by concentration in the heart, to contact the soul behind the personality and let it lead.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Opening above.</strong> Quieting the mind, in the manner Lele taught him, so that peace and force from above the head can descend.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Work as offering.</strong> Doing ordinary work, in the ashram and elsewhere, as part of the yoga, with attention to its quality and without attachment to its fruit.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

No recording of his voice is known, and I have found no film of him in life. The surviving images of him are photographs, among them Cartier-Bresson's of April 1950. His voice survives in writing, at enormous length, nearly all in English: the *Arya* works, the letters to disciples, and *Savitri*. His attendants also recorded his evening conversations, published as *Evening Talks* by A. B. Purani and *Talks with Sri Aurobindo* by Nirodbaran. The Mother's recorded voice, by contrast, survives on many tapes.

## Critiques and questions

**The promised transformation.** Aurobindo taught that the Supermind would transform even the body, and *Savitri* imagines death defeated. He died of kidney failure in 1950, and the Mother died in 1973. Disciples answer that his death was a deliberate sacrifice. K. D. Sethna, a poet and senior disciple, wrote that it left "a question-mark glaring in the face of every disciple," and argued that "there was no failure on Sri Aurobindo's part." Outside the movement, readers have asked whether a claim about the future of evolution can be tested at all.

**Reading Shankara.** Aurobindo's case against "Illusionism" in *The Life Divine* treats Shankara's Advaita as world-denial. Defenders of Shankara, among them the philosopher R. Puligandla, have argued that this misrepresents a doctrine in which the world is neither real nor unreal, and is never simply dismissed.

**Religion and nationalism.** His Calcutta writings of 1905 to 1910 fused Indian nationalism with Hindu religious imagery. Barbara Southard, in *Modern Asian Studies* (1980), traced his use of Shakta symbolism and the figure of the nation as Kali, and the Uttarpara speech identifies the Sanatana Dharma with nationalism. Hindu nationalist groups have since claimed him as a forerunner. Other readers, including the ashram, point to his later turn away from politics, his support for world union, and his call in 1947 for the partition to be undone by agreement.

**The biography dispute.** Peter Heehs, an American-born historian who worked for decades in the ashram's archives, published *The Lives of Sri Aurobindo* with Columbia University Press in 2008. Many devotees objected to the book, and its publication in India was delayed. In April 2009 the Odisha government ordered the book forfeited under Section 95 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, on the ground that it insulted devotees' religious beliefs (Section 295A of the Indian Penal Code). Heehs challenged the order in the Orissa High Court, where the case was still pending in 2016; no ruling has been reported. Heehs answered that the book suggests no improper relation between Aurobindo and the Mother and that he judged Aurobindo "eminently sane." The ashram's trustees declined to expel him. In 2012 the Ministry of Home Affairs at first declined to entertain his application to extend his visa, then granted a one-year extension after scholars and a Union minister protested. Some devotees sought to have the ashram's trustees removed for failing to act against the book; a lower court gave them leave to sue under Section 92 of the Code of Civil Procedure and the Madras High Court declined to interfere, but on 5 January 2016 the Supreme Court revoked that leave, holding that the trustees' mild response to a book critical of their guru was no ground for removing them.

**Auroville.** The township has had repeated disputes over governance and land. In 2008 a BBC *Newsnight* report alleged that paedophiles had been tolerated there; Auroville complained of bias, and Ofcom did not uphold the complaint. In December 2021 work began, under the Auroville Foundation's administration, on clearing trees for the Crown Road, the ring road in the Mother's plan. Many residents protested, In April 2022 the National Green Tribunal ordered the Foundation to prepare a proper township plan and obtain environmental clearance; in March 2025 the Supreme Court set that order aside, allowing development under the existing master plan.

## Descendants

The Sri Aurobindo Ashram remains in Pondicherry, with its school, the Sri Aurobindo International Centre of Education, founded by the Mother in 1952. Auroville, inaugurated on 28 February 1968 before delegates from 124 nations, had about 3,300 residents in 2021 and has been run since 1988 by a foundation under an Act of Parliament. Sri Chinmoy, who grew up in the ashram, took what he learned there to New York in 1964. The Mother's own late experiences, recorded by the French writer Satprem in the thirteen volumes of *Mother's Agenda*, started a further stream. In the West, Michael Murphy of the Esalen Institute, the philosopher Haridas Chaudhuri at the California Institute of Integral Studies, and the integral theorist Ken Wilber all built on his evolutionary vision, Wilber with large changes that some Aurobindo scholars dispute. Later Indian teachers who speak of consciousness evolving often use his vocabulary.
