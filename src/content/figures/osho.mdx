<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> He was born Chandra Mohan Jain. As a lecturer and travelling speaker in the 1960s he was Acharya Rajneesh, "acharya" meaning teacher. From 1971 he was Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh, "bhagwan" meaning blessed one or lord. In 1989 he took the name Osho Rajneesh and, a few months later, simply Osho. This chapter calls him Rajneesh for the years before 1989 and Osho after.</aside>

<section class="scene">

<p>Koregaon Park, Poona, in the late 1970s. Visitors describe the ashram day beginning in the early morning with Dynamic Meditation. Hundreds of people, most of them Western and almost all dressed in orange or red, stand with their eyes closed and breathe hard and chaotically through the nose. Ten minutes later the breathing gives way to screaming, weeping, shaking and laughing. Then they raise their arms and jump, shouting "Hoo! Hoo! Hoo!" until a voice calls "Stop!" and the whole crowd freezes where it stands.</p>

<p>Later in the morning, many of the same people sit on the floor of the open Buddha Hall while a bearded man in a robe sits in a chair before them. He speaks slowly, with long pauses, for an hour or more. On one morning the subject might be a Zen story, on another a Sufi teaching tale or a sutra from a Tantric text. Microphones carry his voice to tape recorders. Within months the talk will be a book. In the afternoon the visitors go to therapy groups; in the evening, a few at a time, they sit before him to receive a new name and a necklace of wooden beads with his picture in a locket.</p>

<p>One follower who was there in 1978 remembered seven or eight thousand people, all in red. <i>Time</i> magazine called the place the Esalen East.</p>

## Life

Chandra Mohan Jain was born on 11 December 1931 at his maternal grandparents' home in Kuchwada, a village in what is now the Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh. His father, Babulal, was a cloth merchant, and Chandra Mohan was the eldest of eleven children. The family were Digambar Jains who followed the teaching of the medieval reformer Taran Svami. He lived with his grandparents until he was about eight, then moved to his parents' town of Gadarwara after his grandfather died.

He later told his childhood at length, in dictated reminiscences published as *Glimpses of a Golden Childhood*. In that telling he was a contrary, questioning boy who argued with priests and teachers and who was befriended by a series of wandering holy men, named in his account as Pagal Baba, Magga Baba and Masto. He also counted his grandmother, his Nani, among his early guides. These stories come from him and from the movement that publishes him, and they are reported here as his account.

He began college in Jabalpur in 1951 at Hitkarini College, left after quarrels with an instructor, and completed a BA in philosophy at D. N. Jain College in 1955. He took an MA in philosophy, with distinction, from the University of Saugar in 1957.

By his own account the decisive event came before either degree. He said that on 21 March 1953, aged twenty-one, while sitting under a tree in the Bhanvartal garden in Jabalpur, he became enlightened. In the movement's telling, Magga Baba confirmed it by touching his feet and weeping, and his grandmother became his first disciple. He had no formal initiation from any teacher, and he never claimed one.

After the MA he taught briefly at the Sanskrit college in Raipur. In 1958 he became a lecturer in philosophy at the University of Jabalpur and was made a professor in 1960. (Britannica dates the start of his Jabalpur teaching to 1957.) In those years he also travelled India as a public speaker under the name Acharya Rajneesh. His positions were deliberately provocative. He said socialism would only socialise poverty, and argued that India needed capitalism, science, technology and birth control. From 1962 he led meditation camps of three to ten days, and small centres called Jivan Jagruti Kendra (Life Awakening Centres) grew up around him. In 1966 he resigned his professorship.

<aside class="note"><strong>Acharya and Bhagwan.</strong> Acharya is the ordinary Hindi and Sanskrit word for a teacher, used for professors and for heads of religious lineages. Bhagwan is the word for God or the Lord, used of deities and, by devotees, of some saints.</aside>

The talks that made him notorious came in 1968 in Bombay. On 28 August, at the end of a lecture series organised by a Jain youth group at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan, he was asked to speak on love and spoke about sex and bliss. He asked for a hall where he could finish the argument, and from 28 September to 2 October he gave further talks at the Gowalia Tank maidan, where the movement says twenty-five to thirty thousand people came. Published as *From Sex to Superconsciousness*, the talks argued that sex repressed becomes an obsession, while sex understood and lived with awareness can open onto meditation. The Indian press began calling him the sex guru.

At the end of June 1970 he left Jabalpur for Bombay, and that December moved into an apartment in the Woodlands building. Earlier that year, at public meditation camps, he had introduced the method that became Dynamic Meditation. On 26 September 1970, at a camp in Manali in the Himalayan foothills, he gave the first group of followers a new kind of initiation he called neo-sannyas. (Some accounts place this first initiation in Bombay.) In 1971 he took the title Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh.

In 1974, on the twenty-first anniversary of his Jabalpur experience, he moved to a property in Koregaon Park, Poona, bought with the help of a follower, Ma Yoga Mukta (Catherine Venizelos). The Poona ashram was the setting for most of the teaching that survives. Each morning began with Dynamic Meditation and continued with his discourse in Buddha Hall. From 1975, after therapists from the American human potential movement arrived, therapy groups became a central part of ashram life and one of its main sources of income. By 1981 about thirty thousand visitors a year were coming.

On 10 April 1981 he stopped speaking in public, a silence that lasted until 30 October 1984. On 1 June 1981 he flew to the United States. His secretary, Ma Anand Sheela, arranged the purchase of the Big Muddy Ranch in central Oregon, about sixty-four thousand acres bought for 5.75 million dollars, and he moved there on 29 August 1981. Residents voted to incorporate a city, Rajneeshpuram, in May 1982, and at its height around seven thousand people lived there, not all full time. Rajneesh, silent, drove daily past lines of followers in one of a fleet of Rolls-Royces that eventually numbered ninety-three.

In September 1985 the commune's leadership left and Rajneeshpuram came apart. In November 1985 he left the United States.

He landed in Delhi on 17 November 1985. A months-long world tour followed. He returned to Bombay at the end of July 1986 and to the Poona ashram in January 1987. In February 1989 he became Osho Rajneesh and in September 1989 simply Osho. His last public discourse was in April 1989. He died at the ashram on 19 January 1990, aged fifty-eight. His samadhi there carries an inscription saying that he was never born and never died, and only visited this planet Earth between 11 December 1931 and 19 January 1990 (the exact wording varies between the sources seen).

## The teaching

Osho never wrote a system. He talked, almost daily, for about twenty years, in Hindi to Indian audiences and increasingly in English to foreign ones, and the talks were taped and edited into books. Most of his published work is transcription. A few books were dictated instead of delivered as public talks, among them *Glimpses of a Golden Childhood*, *Notes of a Madman* and *Books I Have Loved*, recorded during the Oregon years. The total runs to hundreds of titles. Because the books are edited speech on whatever text or question was in front of him that day, they often contradict one another. Read across the whole body, a small set of ideas comes back again and again.

**The witness.** The centre of the teaching is awareness, which he usually called witnessing. The mind, with its conditioning, desires and fears, runs on its own; the meditator learns to watch it without fighting it or following it. He drew this from many sources, the Jain and Buddhist emphasis on watchfulness among them, and he applied it to everything. Of Dynamic Meditation he said, "This is a meditation in which you have to be continuously alert, conscious, aware of whatsoever you do." The goal he described was a state of no-mind, in which watching continues and the chatter has stopped.

**Catharsis first.** His most practical claim was that modern people cannot simply sit down and meditate. Their bodies and minds are too full of suppressed anger, grief and sexual energy, and sitting still just pushes it down further. So most of his methods start with an active phase that throws this out, through fast breathing, shouting, shaking, dancing or laughing, and end in silence. He said that he never told people to begin by just sitting. The active phase prepares the body for the stillness that follows, and the stillness lets the meditator notice the madness the active phase brought up. Writers on the movement have pointed to the debt this owes to Wilhelm Reich's body-centred therapy, and have compared it with Gurdjieff's movements and Sufi dance.

**Acceptance of the body and the world.** Against the renunciation of his Jain upbringing and of most Indian monastic traditions, he taught that desire, sex, money and pleasure were energies to be lived fully and with awareness until they transformed by themselves. This was the argument of the 1968 talks, and it ran through his readings of Tantra. Its emblem was his phrase Zorba the Buddha, after the life-loving hero of Nikos Kazantzakis's novel joined to the Buddha's stillness. "The new man I talk about, and the new humanity, will not be Eastern or Western," he said. "It will not believe in this world only or in that world only, it will believe in the totality of man." Hugh Urban, the scholar of religion who has written the fullest study of the movement, notes that he called himself a "materialist spiritualist" and the "rich man's guru."

**Against belief and institution.** He attacked organised religion, priests, scripture taken as authority, nationalism and the family as instruments of conditioning. His followers' own handbook described the movement as "a religionless religion." He presented himself as a catalyst, one who provokes and then gets out of the way. At the same time he gave initiations, names and a uniform, and in Poona and Oregon he presided over large and tightly run institutions.

**Neo-sannyas.** Traditional sannyas means renouncing home, family and property and wandering as a monk in ochre. Osho kept the outward signs and reversed the meaning. His neo-sannyasins took a new name from him, wore orange or red, and wore a mala of beads with his photograph in a locket. They stayed in the world, working, loving and enjoying themselves while they meditated. From 1985 the colours and the mala became optional, and most followers later dropped them.

<aside class="note"><strong>Mala.</strong> A string of beads, usually 108, used in India for counting repetitions of a prayer or mantra. Osho's mala carried his picture in a locket and served as the visible sign of initiation.</aside>

**His readings of other traditions.** Most of his discourse series take a text from someone else and speak on it line by line. He spoke on the *Vijnana Bhairava Tantra*, a Kashmiri Shaiva text listing 112 methods of meditation, in the volumes published as *The Book of Secrets*, and on the Tibetan yogi Tilopa's Song of Mahamudra. He gave series on the Gita, on several Upanishads and Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, on the poems of Kabir, on Lao Tzu and Chuang Tzu, on Heraclitus, on Sufi stories, and on the sayings of Jesus and the Gospel of Thomas. From early 1988 his talks dealt almost entirely with Zen, and his last series were Zen series. In each case he read the text as an account of meditation and awareness, and in each case the text came out sounding like Osho. Admirers find this his gift: he made distant traditions feel like live instructions. Critics find it a flattening, in which every tradition is made to teach the same thing he was teaching.

**Gurdjieff and therapy.** Two Western sources shaped the Poona work as much as any Indian one. From the Armenian-Greek teacher G. I. Gurdjieff he took the picture of ordinary human beings as machines running on habit, and the idea of a community where hard, unpaid work under abrasive supervisors becomes part of the training. The Poona work departments were modelled on Gurdjieff's community in France. From the human potential movement, centred on the Esalen Institute in California, he took encounter groups, primal therapy, bodywork and dozens of other techniques, run at the ashram by trained Western therapists who had become sannyasins.

I find it hard to name a single idea in Osho that has no older source, and he would not have minded; he spent most of his career commenting on other people's texts. What I cannot find before him is the particular sequence he built: an hour that starts with chaos and ends in stillness, designed for the restless urban body and taught in the same programme as Western encounter groups. That design has outlived the Oregon commune and is practised today by people who know little about him.

## Lineage

Osho stands outside any initiatory lineage. His Jain birth is documented, his teachers in childhood are known only from his own account, and his debts to Tantra, Zen, Sufism, Gurdjieff and Western therapy are visible in the discourses themselves.

The documented edges are his Jain upbringing, his discourses on Kashmiri Shaiva, Buddhist and Sufi texts, the Gurdjieff model for the Poona work, and the therapies brought by the human potential movement. The holy men he named as guides in childhood are claims, and the tree marks them as such. Downstream, the edges run through the Oregon commune and to the Osho International Foundation, which controls his published words.

## The practice

Dynamic Meditation, as the movement publishes it, lasts an hour and is done in the morning. Four of its five stages are accompanied by music.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Breathe (10 minutes).</strong> Breathe chaotically through the nose, fast and deep, concentrating on the exhalation and letting the body take care of the inhalation.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Explode (10 minutes).</strong> Let out whatever needs to come out: scream, cry, shake, jump, dance, laugh. The instruction is to hold nothing back and keep the mind out of it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hoo (10 minutes).</strong> With arms raised, jump up and down shouting "Hoo! Hoo! Hoo!" so that each landing drives the sound down into the sex centre below the navel, until exhausted.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Stop (15 minutes).</strong> At the call, freeze exactly where you are, without adjusting the body, and watch whatever happens.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Celebrate (15 minutes).</strong> Dance in gratitude and carry the mood into the day.</div></li>
</ol>

He designed other active meditations on the same pattern. Kundalini Meditation has four stages of fifteen minutes: shaking, free dancing, sitting or standing still, and lying still. Nadabrahma is built on humming. A silent version of Dynamic exists for places where noise is impossible. All of them end in the same thing: watching without doing.

## In his own voice

Osho is one of the most recorded teachers in this book. Almost every discourse from the Poona years onward was taped, and many from the later 1970s and 1980s were filmed. The Osho International Foundation controls these recordings and publishes them through its own channels, including video on its official YouTube channel. The texts of the published books are the edited form of the same talks. Independent archives hold Oregon-era material, and two documentaries filmed during his lifetime show the movement from the outside.

## Descendants

The Osho International Meditation Resort in Pune, run under the Osho International Foundation, continues his programme of active meditations, therapy groups and recorded discourses, and press reports in the 2000s put its visitors at about two hundred thousand a year. Independent Osho centres and communes around the world run the same meditations under their own management; Osho Friends International in Delhi is among the most active. His books, mostly transcriptions, remain widely sold in India in Hindi and English. The 2018 Netflix series *Wild Wild Country* brought Rajneeshpuram to a new generation.
