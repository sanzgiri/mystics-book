<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> He was born Chandra Mohan Jain. As a lecturer and travelling speaker in the 1960s he was Acharya Rajneesh, "acharya" meaning teacher. From 1971 he was Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh, "bhagwan" meaning blessed one or lord. In 1989 he took the name Osho Rajneesh and, a few months later, simply Osho. This chapter calls him Rajneesh for the years before 1989 and Osho after.</aside>

<section class="scene">

<p>Koregaon Park, Poona, in the late 1970s. Visitors describe the ashram day beginning in the early morning with Dynamic Meditation. Hundreds of people, most of them Western and almost all dressed in orange or red, stand with their eyes closed and breathe hard and chaotically through the nose. Ten minutes later the breathing gives way to screaming, weeping, shaking and laughing. Then they raise their arms and jump, shouting "Hoo! Hoo! Hoo!" until a voice calls "Stop!" and the whole crowd freezes where it stands.</p>

<p>Later in the morning, many of the same people sit on the floor of the open Buddha Hall while a bearded man in a robe sits in a chair before them. He speaks slowly, with long pauses, for an hour or more. On one morning the subject might be a Zen story, on another a Sufi teaching tale or a sutra from a Tantric text. Microphones carry his voice to tape recorders. Within months the talk will be a book. In the afternoon the visitors go to therapy groups; in the evening, a few at a time, they sit before him to receive a new name and a necklace of wooden beads with his picture in a locket.</p>

<p>One follower who was there in 1978 remembered seven or eight thousand people, all in red. <i>Time</i> magazine called the place the Esalen East.</p>

## Life

Chandra Mohan Jain was born on 11 December 1931 at his maternal grandparents' home in Kuchwada, a village in what is now the Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh. His father, Babulal, was a cloth merchant, and Chandra Mohan was the eldest of eleven children. The family were Digambar Jains who followed the teaching of the medieval reformer Taran Svami. He lived with his grandparents until he was about eight, then moved to his parents' town of Gadarwara after his grandfather died.

He later told his childhood at length, in dictated reminiscences published as *Glimpses of a Golden Childhood*. In that telling he was a contrary, questioning boy who argued with priests and teachers and who was befriended by a series of wandering holy men, named in his account as Pagal Baba, Magga Baba and Masto. He also counted his grandmother, his Nani, among his early guides. These stories come from him and from the movement that publishes him, and they are reported here as his account.

He began college in Jabalpur in 1951 at Hitkarini College, left after quarrels with an instructor, and completed a BA in philosophy at D. N. Jain College in 1955. He took an MA in philosophy, with distinction, from the University of Saugar in 1957.

By his own account the decisive event came before either degree. He said that on 21 March 1953, aged twenty-one, while sitting under a tree in the Bhanvartal garden in Jabalpur, he became enlightened. In the movement's telling, Magga Baba confirmed it by touching his feet and weeping, and his grandmother became his first disciple. He had no formal initiation from any teacher, and he never claimed one.

After the MA he taught briefly at the Sanskrit college in Raipur. Its vice-chancellor reportedly asked for him to be moved, judging him a danger to the students' morals and religion. In 1958 he became a lecturer in philosophy at the University of Jabalpur and was made a professor in 1960. (Britannica dates the start of his Jabalpur teaching to 1957.) In those years he also travelled India as a public speaker under the name Acharya Rajneesh. His positions were deliberately provocative. He called Gandhi a masochist who worshipped poverty, said socialism would only socialise poverty, and argued that India needed capitalism, science, technology and birth control. From 1962 he led meditation camps of three to ten days, and small centres called Jivan Jagruti Kendra (Life Awakening Centres) grew up around him. In 1966, after a controversial speaking tour, he resigned his professorship at the university's request.

<aside class="note"><strong>Acharya and Bhagwan.</strong> Acharya is the ordinary Hindi and Sanskrit word for a teacher, used for professors and for heads of religious lineages. Bhagwan is the word for God or the Lord, used of deities and, by devotees, of some saints.</aside>

The talks that made him notorious came in 1968 in Bombay. On 28 August, at the end of a lecture series organised by a Jain youth group at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan, he was asked to speak on love and spoke about sex and bliss. Many of his Jain hosts were outraged. He asked for a hall where he could finish the argument, and from 28 September to 2 October he gave further talks at the Gowalia Tank maidan, where the movement says twenty-five to thirty thousand people came. Published as *From Sex to Superconsciousness*, the talks argued that sex repressed becomes an obsession, while sex understood and lived with awareness can open onto meditation. The Indian press began calling him the sex guru.

At the end of June 1970 he left Jabalpur for Bombay, and that December moved into an apartment in the Woodlands building. Earlier that year, at public meditation camps, he had introduced the method that became Dynamic Meditation. On 26 September 1970, at a camp in Manali in the Himalayan foothills, he gave the first group of followers a new kind of initiation he called neo-sannyas. (Some accounts place this first initiation in Bombay.) In 1971 he took the title Bhagwan Shree Rajneesh.

In 1974, on the twenty-first anniversary of his Jabalpur experience, he moved to a property in Koregaon Park, Poona, bought with the help of a follower, Ma Yoga Mukta (Catherine Venizelos). The Poona ashram was the setting for most of the teaching that survives. Each morning began with Dynamic Meditation and continued with his discourse in Buddha Hall. From 1975, after therapists from the American human potential movement arrived, therapy groups became a central part of ashram life and one of its main sources of income. By 1981 about thirty thousand visitors a year were coming.

On 10 April 1981 he stopped speaking in public, a silence that lasted until 30 October 1984. On 1 June 1981 he flew to the United States on a tourist visa, ostensibly for medical treatment. His secretary, Ma Anand Sheela, arranged the purchase of the Big Muddy Ranch in central Oregon, about sixty-four thousand acres bought for 5.75 million dollars, and he moved there on 29 August 1981. Residents voted to incorporate a city, Rajneeshpuram, in May 1982, and at its height around seven thousand people lived there, not all full time. The commune fought constant legal battles with its neighbours and the state. Rajneesh, silent, drove daily past lines of followers in one of a fleet of Rolls-Royces that eventually numbered ninety-three. In 1984 members of the commune's leadership carried out a mass food poisoning in the nearby town of The Dalles, described under Critiques below.

In September 1985 Sheela and nineteen other leaders left for Europe. On 16 September Rajneesh called them a "gang of fascists" and invited police and federal investigators into the commune. A federal grand jury indicted him on immigration charges on 23 October. On 28 October he was arrested when two chartered jets carrying him and a few followers landed at Charlotte, North Carolina; the authorities said the party was heading for Bermuda. In mid-November 1985 he pleaded guilty to two counts in federal court in Portland, received a suspended sentence and a fine, and left the United States the same day.

He landed in Delhi on 17 November 1985. A months-long world tour followed. He was arrested by Greek intelligence and expelled from Crete after a few days, refused entry or turned away in several European countries and in Canada, allowed a short stay in Ireland and Uruguay, and asked to leave Uruguay and then Jamaica. Twenty-one countries refused him. He returned to Bombay at the end of July 1986 and to the Poona ashram in January 1987. In February 1989 he became Osho Rajneesh and in September 1989 simply Osho. His last public discourse was in April 1989. He died at the ashram on 19 January 1990, aged fifty-eight. His samadhi there carries an inscription saying that he was never born and never died, and only visited this planet Earth between 11 December 1931 and 19 January 1990 (the exact wording varies between the sources seen).

## The teaching

Osho never wrote a system. He talked, almost daily, for about twenty years, in Hindi to Indian audiences and increasingly in English to foreign ones, and the talks were taped and edited into books. Most of his published work is transcription. A few books were dictated instead of delivered as public talks, among them *Glimpses of a Golden Childhood*, *Notes of a Madman* and *Books I Have Loved*, recorded during the Oregon years. The total runs to hundreds of titles. Because the books are edited speech on whatever text or question was in front of him that day, they often contradict one another. Read across the whole body, a small set of ideas comes back again and again.

**The witness.** The centre of the teaching is awareness, which he usually called witnessing. The mind, with its conditioning, desires and fears, runs on its own; the meditator learns to watch it without fighting it or following it. He drew this from many sources, the Jain and Buddhist emphasis on watchfulness among them, and he applied it to everything. Of Dynamic Meditation he said, "This is a meditation in which you have to be continuously alert, conscious, aware of whatsoever you do." The goal he described was a state of no-mind, in which watching continues and the chatter has stopped.

**Catharsis first.** His most practical claim was that modern people cannot simply sit down and meditate. Their bodies and minds are too full of suppressed anger, grief and sexual energy, and sitting still just pushes it down further. So most of his methods start with an active phase that throws this out, through fast breathing, shouting, shaking, dancing or laughing, and end in silence. He said that he never told people to begin by just sitting. The active phase prepares the body for the stillness that follows, and the stillness lets the meditator notice the madness the active phase brought up. Writers on the movement have pointed to the debt this owes to Wilhelm Reich's body-centred therapy, and have compared it with Gurdjieff's movements and Sufi dance.

**Acceptance of the body and the world.** Against the renunciation of his Jain upbringing and of most Indian monastic traditions, he taught that desire, sex, money and pleasure were energies to be lived fully and with awareness until they transformed by themselves. This was the argument of the 1968 talks, and it ran through his readings of Tantra. Its emblem was his phrase Zorba the Buddha, after the life-loving hero of Nikos Kazantzakis's novel joined to the Buddha's stillness. "The new man I talk about, and the new humanity, will not be Eastern or Western," he said. "It will not believe in this world only or in that world only, it will believe in the totality of man." Hugh Urban, the scholar of religion who has written the fullest study of the movement, notes that he called himself a "materialist spiritualist" and the "rich man's guru."

**Against belief and institution.** He attacked organised religion, priests, scripture taken as authority, nationalism and the family as instruments of conditioning. In September 1985 he denied that he was a religious teacher, and his followers' own handbook described the movement as "a religionless religion." He presented himself as a catalyst, one who provokes and then gets out of the way. At the same time he gave initiations, names and a uniform, and in Poona and Oregon he presided over large and tightly run institutions.

**Neo-sannyas.** Traditional sannyas means renouncing home, family and property and wandering as a monk in ochre. Osho kept the outward signs and reversed the meaning. His neo-sannyasins took a new name from him, wore orange or red, and wore a mala of beads with his photograph in a locket. They stayed in the world, working, loving and enjoying themselves while they meditated. From 1985 the colours and the mala became optional, and most followers later dropped them.

<aside class="note"><strong>Mala.</strong> A string of beads, usually 108, used in India for counting repetitions of a prayer or mantra. Osho's mala carried his picture in a locket and served as the visible sign of initiation.</aside>

**His readings of other traditions.** Most of his discourse series take a text from someone else and speak on it line by line. He spoke on the *Vijnana Bhairava Tantra*, a Kashmiri Shaiva text listing 112 methods of meditation, in the volumes published as *The Book of Secrets*, and on the Tibetan yogi Tilopa's Song of Mahamudra. He gave series on the Gita, on several Upanishads and Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, on the poems of Kabir, on Lao Tzu and Chuang Tzu, on Heraclitus, on Sufi stories, and on the sayings of Jesus and the Gospel of Thomas. From early 1988 his talks dealt almost entirely with Zen, and his last series were Zen series. In each case he read the text as an account of meditation and awareness, and in each case the text came out sounding like Osho. Admirers find this his gift: he made distant traditions feel like live instructions. Critics find it a flattening, in which every tradition is made to teach the same thing he was teaching.

**Gurdjieff and therapy.** Two Western sources shaped the Poona work as much as any Indian one. From the Armenian-Greek teacher G. I. Gurdjieff he took the picture of ordinary human beings as machines running on habit, and the idea of a community where hard, unpaid work under abrasive supervisors becomes part of the training. The Poona work departments were modelled on Gurdjieff's community in France. From the human potential movement, centred on the Esalen Institute in California, he took encounter groups, primal therapy, bodywork and dozens of other techniques, run at the ashram by trained Western therapists who had become sannyasins.

I find it hard to name a single idea in Osho that has no older source, and he would not have minded; he spent most of his career commenting on other people's texts. What I cannot find before him is the particular sequence he built: an hour that starts with chaos and ends in stillness, designed for the restless urban body and taught in the same programme as Western encounter groups. That design has outlived the movement's scandals and is practised today by people who know little about him.

## Lineage

Osho stands outside any initiatory lineage. His Jain birth is documented, his teachers in childhood are known only from his own account, and his debts to Tantra, Zen, Sufism, Gurdjieff and Western therapy are visible in the discourses themselves.

The documented edges are his Jain upbringing, his discourses on Kashmiri Shaiva, Buddhist and Sufi texts, the Gurdjieff model for the Poona work, and the therapies brought by the human potential movement. The holy men he named as guides in childhood are claims, and the tree marks them as such. Downstream, the edges run to his secretary Ma Anand Sheela, through the Oregon commune, and to the Osho International Foundation, which controls his name and published words.

## The practice

Dynamic Meditation, as the movement publishes it, lasts an hour and is done in the morning. Four of its five stages are accompanied by music.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Breathe (10 minutes).</strong> Breathe chaotically through the nose, fast and deep, concentrating on the exhalation and letting the body take care of the inhalation.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Explode (10 minutes).</strong> Let out whatever needs to come out: scream, cry, shake, jump, dance, laugh. The instruction is to hold nothing back and keep the mind out of it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hoo (10 minutes).</strong> With arms raised, jump up and down shouting "Hoo! Hoo! Hoo!" so that each landing drives the sound down into the sex centre below the navel, until exhausted.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Stop (15 minutes).</strong> At the call, freeze exactly where you are, without adjusting the body, and watch whatever happens.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Celebrate (15 minutes).</strong> Dance in gratitude and carry the mood into the day.</div></li>
</ol>

He designed other active meditations on the same pattern. Kundalini Meditation has four stages of fifteen minutes: shaking, free dancing, sitting or standing still, and lying still. Nadabrahma is built on humming. A silent version of Dynamic exists for places where noise is impossible. All of them end in the same thing: watching without doing.

The Osho International Foundation controls these recordings and publishes them through its own channels, including video on its official YouTube channel <em>[link to locate]</em>. ## Critiques and questions

**The therapy groups in Poona.** In the late 1970s some encounter groups at the Poona ashram allowed physical aggression. Richard Price, a co-founder of the Esalen Institute, left the movement after seeing what he described as brutality in the groups; the therapist David Boadella wrote of "violence, bone-breaking presented as good for the soul." One woman who did a group in 1978 later told the writer Win McCormack that the leader had told the men to beat another participant because she was not participating; when she pulled one of the men away, he turned on her, and she came out with "two cracked ribs, a concussion, and a subconjunctival hemorrhage in each eye." Wolfgang Dobrowolny's 1981 film *Ashram in Poona* shows violent encounter sessions; its opening title notes that followers condemned it, and in the film one participant says the group helped her overcome her fear of men. Later accounts of the movement report that the ashram announced an end to physical violence in the groups in January 1979. These are accounts from participants and observers; no criminal case arose from them.

**The 1984 salmonella attack.** In September and October 1984, members of the Rajneeshpuram leadership contaminated salad bars in restaurants in The Dalles, Oregon, with *Salmonella* Typhimurium, in an attempt to depress turnout in the November county elections. Seven hundred and fifty-one people fell ill and forty-five were treated in hospital; none died. A 1997 study in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* documented the outbreak, which is often described as the largest bioterrorist attack in United States history. Ma Anand Sheela and Ma Anand Puja (Diane Onang) were charged with offences including product tampering, attempted murder of Rajneesh's physician, assault, wiretapping and immigration fraud. In July 1986 both entered pleas and were convicted and sentenced to prison. Later accounts describe the pleas as Alford pleas, guilty pleas entered without admitting the facts, while some press reports say simply that they pleaded guilty; the court record has not been checked here. Accounts of the sentences also differ. The Oregon Historical Society says Sheela was sentenced to ten years in federal prison and released on 13 December 1988, after about two and a half years. Rajneesh was never charged with any offence connected to the poisoning. It was he who, in September 1985, publicly accused Sheela's group of crimes and invited investigators in. The commune's former mayor, David Knapp, who cooperated with prosecutors, told the FBI that Sheela told him Rajneesh had said "it was best not to hurt people, but if a few died not to worry." That is Knapp's account of Sheela's account. A 1994 study of former commune members by Carl Latkin and colleagues in *Sociology of Religion* is reported to have found that most of them believed Rajneesh knew of Sheela's illegal activities, while the writer Frances FitzGerald, in *Cities on a Hill*, is cited for the observation that most followers believed him incapable of violence.

**The 1985 immigration case.** The federal indictment of 23 October 1985 charged Rajneesh with thirty-four or thirty-five counts (sources differ) of violating immigration law. On 8 November he pleaded not guilty before Judge Edward Leavy in Portland and was released on bail. In November he changed his plea on two counts: that he had concealed an intention to remain permanently when he applied for his visa in 1981, and that he had conspired to arrange a sham marriage. Contemporary news reports say he pleaded guilty. Some later accounts call it an Alford plea, in which the defendant accepts conviction without admitting guilt, but the court record has not been checked here. Judge Leavy imposed a ten-year suspended sentence, five years' probation and 400,000 dollars in fines and costs, and Rajneesh agreed to leave the country and not return for five years without the Attorney General's permission. (One reference summary gives ten years for the exclusion, which probably confuses it with the ten-year suspended sentence; the plea agreement itself has not been read here.) He left the same day, and most sources speak of his departure as a deportation. After his death the commune said that he had been poisoned in American jails and that this had made life in his body a hell. That claim is the movement's; no court has examined it.

**The death and its certification.** Osho died on the afternoon of 19 January 1990. A commune spokesman told reporters, "Osho left his body at 5:30 p.m. The doctors say it's heart failure." He was cremated within about an hour. In 2016 the doctor who signed the death certificate, Gokul Gokani, a surgeon and sannyasin, said that he had been urged to come to the ashram that afternoon and was taken to Osho's room only at about 5 p.m., that he had not seen the death, and that the foundation's leaders Amrito and Jayesh had asked him to record a cardiac cause so as to avoid a post-mortem. The journalist Abhay Vaidya's book *Who Killed Osho?* collects these and other doubts. Asked about such claims, Ma Amrit Sadhana of the Pune resort replied, "Anyone can say anything. What's the proof?" No court has ruled on the cause of death.

**The will, the name and the land.** The Osho International Foundation, a Swiss-registered foundation based in Zurich, claims copyright in all his published and unpublished words and has registered OSHO as a trademark. Rival groups of disciples have contested both for decades. In the United States, after a ten-year challenge by the Delhi-based Osho Friends International, the trademark office cancelled the foundation's OSHO marks in January 2009, finding that "The primary significance of Osho is as a religious or meditation movement." In Europe the outcome went the other way: on 11 October 2017 the General Court of the European Union upheld the foundation's EU trademark against a challenge by the Osho Lotus Commune of Cologne. In March 2025 Osho Friends International asked the Delhi High Court to bar registration of trademarks containing the name; the court sought responses from the government and the trademark registry. Separately, a will dated 1989 and said to bear Osho's signature was produced in 2013. A Pune disciple, Yogesh Thakkar, filed a police complaint that year alleging that it was forged and that crores of rupees had been misappropriated. According to Vaidya, the foundation's leaders later withdrew the will. Pune police filed a closure report, and in October 2018 the Bombay High Court declined to order a CBI investigation, noting that petitioners could challenge the closure before the magistrate. Asked about the will, Ma Amrit Sadhana said, "It's completely wrong. I cannot comment on it as the matter is in court." Most recently, in 2020 the foundation sought permission from the charity authorities to sell two plots of the Pune campus, which it said it needed to do after losses from the pandemic, for 107 crore rupees to Rajivnayan Bajaj and the Rishab Family Trust. Disciples objected. In December 2023 Mumbai's Joint Charity Commissioner refused permission, ordered a special audit of the foundation's accounts from 2005, and recorded prima facie findings that trustees had diverted funds to private companies they had set up. On 8 April 2024 the Bombay High Court upheld that order, agreeing that the foundation had not shown a "genuine and compelling necessity" for the sale. The foundation appealed to the Supreme Court, where the case was listed for hearing in July 2024; no ruling on that appeal had been reported by October 2026.

## Descendants

The Osho International Meditation Resort in Pune, run under the Osho International Foundation, continues his programme of active meditations, therapy groups and recorded discourses, and press reports in the 2000s put its visitors at about two hundred thousand a year. Independent Osho centres and communes around the world, many of them estranged from the foundation, run the same meditations under their own management; Osho Friends International in Delhi is among the most active. His books, mostly transcriptions, remain widely sold in India in Hindi and English. The 2018 Netflix series *Wild Wild Country* brought Rajneeshpuram to a new generation and, with it, renewed argument over how much he knew.
