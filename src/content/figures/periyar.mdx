## Life

E. V. Ramasamy was born on 17 September 1879 in Erode, into a prosperous merchant family. He joined the family business at twelve and at nineteen married Nagammai, who worked beside him until her death in 1933. The usual account dates his turn against religion to a pilgrimage to Kashi in 1904, where rest houses fed only Brahmins and he went hungry.

He joined the Indian National Congress in 1919. In April 1924 he went to Vaikom in Travancore, where a campaign was under way to open the roads around the temple to lower castes, the same campaign that appears in the chapter on Narayana Guru. The Madras Congress later hailed him as the Hero of Vaikom. He quarrelled with the party over a Congress-funded school at Cheranmadevi that seated Brahmin and non-Brahmin children apart, and over his demand for reserved places for non-Brahmins. When the chair of the 1925 provincial conference at Kanchipuram refused to let him move that resolution, he walked out of the Congress. In 1927 he and a friend met Gandhi and, by Periyar's account, named the tasks ahead as the destruction of the Congress, of the Hindu religion and of Brahmin domination.

That year he started the weekly *Kudi Arasu* and the Self-Respect Movement. Its marriages had no Brahmin priest, no sacred fire and no rituals that made the wife subordinate. The state government legalised them in 1967. A women's conference in Madras on 13 November 1938 asked that his name be prefixed with the title Periyar, "the great one." He joined the anti-Hindi agitation of 1938 and took over the Justice Party in 1939. On 27 August 1944 it became the Dravidar Kazhagam, the Dravidian Federation, which demanded a separate Dravida Nadu. On 9 July 1949 he married Maniammai, a party worker nearly forty years younger. C. N. Annadurai led a walkout and founded the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam on 17 September 1949. He died in Vellore on 24 December 1973, still campaigning at ninety-four. Maniammai led the Kazhagam after him.

## Why this figure appears here

The other people in this part question the guru from inside the religious search. Periyar rejected the search itself. The slogan he popularised, now inscribed under many of his statues, reads in the historian M. S. S. Pandian's translation <q>He who created god is a fool, he who propagates god is a scoundrel, and he who worships god is a barbarian.</q>

His reasoning ran from caste to god. Pandian quotes him: <q>If there is no varnashrama dharma, there is no ground to talk about Hindu religion.</q> Varnashrama dharma, the order of castes and life stages, was in his view the content of Hinduism, the scriptures were its charter and the Brahmin priest its agent, so all three had to go. <q>God and fate are the direct enemies of reason</q>, he said.

His argument resembles Jyotirao Phule's reading of the Puranas as the record of a conquest (Part 1). He shared much with B. R. Ambedkar (Part 11). Periyar's paper *Kudi Arasu* began serialising a Tamil translation of Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* in July 1936, and it appeared as a book in 1937, according to V. Geetha, and the two first met in Bombay in January 1940 (on the 6th or the 8th in different accounts) and again in Madras in 1944. Ambedkar ended in Buddhism; Periyar never converted.
