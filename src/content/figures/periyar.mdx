## Life

E. V. Ramasamy was born on 17 September 1879 in Erode, then in Coimbatore district, to Venkatappa Nayakar and Chinnathayee Muthammal. Wikipedia describes the family as Kannada-speaking Balija merchants, and he grew up speaking Kannada, Telugu and Tamil. He attended school for about five years, joined the family trade at twelve and at nineteen married Nagammai, who worked beside him until her death in 1933. The usual account dates his turn against religion to a pilgrimage to Kashi in 1904, where the rest houses fed only Brahmins. By the common telling he tried a Brahmin eating house wearing a sacred thread and was pushed out when his moustache gave him away.

He joined the Indian National Congress in 1919, under the influence of C. Rajagopalachari according to ThePrint, and went on to head its Madras Presidency committee. He took part in the work for khadi, against liquor and against untouchability, and was jailed during the non-cooperation campaign. In April 1924 he went to Vaikom in Travancore, where a campaign was under way to open the roads around the temple to lower castes, the same campaign that appears in the chapter on Narayana Guru. The Madras Congress later hailed him as the Hero of Vaikom. He quarrelled with the party over a Congress-funded school at Cheranmadevi that seated Brahmin and non-Brahmin children apart, and over his demand for reserved places for non-Brahmins. When the chair of the 1925 provincial conference at Kanchipuram refused to let him move that resolution, he walked out of the Congress. In 1927 he and a friend met Gandhi and, by Periyar's account, named the tasks ahead as the destruction of the Congress, of the Hindu religion and of Brahmin domination.

He had started the weekly *Kudi Arasu* in 1925, the year he left the Congress, and the Self-Respect Movement grew around it. In 1928 he added an English journal, *Revolt*, for readers outside Tamil. At the first provincial Self-Respect conference, held at Chengalpattu in 1929, he announced that he was dropping the caste title Naicker from his name. He travelled to Malaya from December 1929 to January 1930, and from December 1931 he toured Europe and spent about three months in the Soviet Union, returning in November 1932. Wikipedia reports that the journey persuaded him that communist systems suited India's social problems, though he did not call for an end to private ownership.

After the tour he began a series called *Unmai* (Truth), which ThePrint says he modelled on the Bolshevik *Pravda*. He also wrote on the Soviet republic in *Kudi Arasu*.

The movement's marriages had no Brahmin priest, no sacred fire and no rituals that made the wife subordinate. The state government legalised them in 1967. A women's conference in Madras on 13 November 1938 resolved that he should be addressed as Periyar, "the great one." ThePrint credits a Dalit woman it names as Meenabal with first addressing him so at that gathering, while DT Next reports that he had been called Periyar more than a decade earlier, in a 1927 essay and a 1929 book. He argued that making Hindi compulsory was an attempt to impose an Aryan language on Dravidian culture, and in 1938 he joined the anti-Hindi agitation and took over the Justice Party in 1939. On 27 August 1944 it became the Dravidar Kazhagam, the Dravidian Federation, which demanded a separate Dravida Nadu. On 9 July 1949 he married Maniammai, a party worker nearly forty years younger. C. N. Annadurai led a walkout and founded the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam on 17 September 1949. He died in Vellore on 24 December 1973, still campaigning at ninety-four. Maniammai led the Kazhagam after him.

## Why this figure appears here

The other people in this part question the guru from inside the religious search, and Periyar rejected the search itself. The slogan he popularised, now inscribed under many of his statues, reads in the historian M. S. S. Pandian's translation <q>He who created god is a fool, he who propagates god is a scoundrel, and he who worships god is a barbarian.</q>

His reasoning ran from caste to god. Pandian quotes him: <q>If there is no varnashrama dharma, there is no ground to talk about Hindu religion.</q> Varnashrama dharma, the order of castes and life stages, was in his view the content of Hinduism, the scriptures were its charter and the Brahmin priest its agent, so all three had to go. <q>God and fate are the direct enemies of reason</q>, he said.

His argument resembles Jyotirao Phule's reading of the Puranas as the record of a conquest (Part 1). He shared much with B. R. Ambedkar (Part 11). Periyar's paper *Kudi Arasu* began serialising a Tamil translation of Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* in July 1936, and it appeared as a book in 1937, according to V. Geetha, and the two first met in Bombay in January 1940 (on the 6th or the 8th in different accounts) and again in Madras in 1944. Ambedkar ended in Buddhism; Periyar never converted.

He applied one criterion, reason. The summary of his writings in Wikipedia has him tracing caste and inequality to a lack of rationalism and wisdom and placing neither god nor fate behind them. He treated self-respect and freedom as nearly the same thing, and counted any belief a person could not defend by reason as a form of slavery. In practice the method comes down to two questions asked of any custom: who made it, and whom does it serve. He asked them of scripture and of family ritual alike.

Women's rights stood close to the centre of the programme. He argued for education, property rights and, in reasonable circumstances, divorce for women, and for widow remarriage and birth control, a taboo subject in his day. He opposed child marriage, dowry and the devadasi system, and arranged the remarriage of a niece who had been widowed as a child, and held that rules of chastity should bind men and women equally or bind neither. He also proposed simplifying the Tamil script for printing, with fewer letters and plainer forms.

When the movement registered its institution in 1952, the stated objects included ending caste and untouchability, preventing child marriage, promoting widow and inter-caste marriage, securing equal rights for women, and running schools and orphanages. The Self-Respect wedding described above was its most visible form.
