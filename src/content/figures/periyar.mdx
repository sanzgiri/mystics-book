## Life

E. V. Ramasamy was born on 17 September 1879 in Erode, into a prosperous merchant family. He joined the family business at twelve and at nineteen married Nagammai, who worked beside him until her death in 1933. The usual account dates his turn against religion to a pilgrimage to Kashi in 1904, where rest houses fed only Brahmins and he went hungry.

He joined the Indian National Congress in 1919. In April 1924 he went to Vaikom in Travancore, where a campaign was under way to open the roads around the temple to lower castes, the same campaign that appears in the chapter on Narayana Guru. He was arrested and jailed twice, and the Madras Congress later hailed him as the Hero of Vaikom. He quarrelled with the party over a Congress-funded school at Cheranmadevi that seated Brahmin and non-Brahmin children apart, and over his demand for reserved places for non-Brahmins. When the chair of the 1925 provincial conference at Kanchipuram refused to let him move that resolution, he walked out of the Congress.

That year he started the weekly *Kudi Arasu* and the Self-Respect Movement. Its marriages had no Brahmin priest, no sacred fire and no rituals that made the wife subordinate. The state government legalised them in 1967. A women's conference in Madras on 13 November 1938 asked that his name be prefixed with the title Periyar, "the great one." He was jailed in the anti-Hindi agitation of 1938 and took over the Justice Party in 1939. On 27 August 1944 it became the Dravidar Kazhagam, the Dravidian Federation, which demanded a separate Dravida Nadu. On 9 July 1949 he married Maniammai, a party worker nearly forty years younger. C. N. Annadurai led a walkout and founded the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam on 17 September 1949. He died in Vellore on 24 December 1973, still campaigning at ninety-four. Maniammai led the Kazhagam after him.

## Why this figure appears here

The other people in this part question the guru from inside the religious search. Periyar rejected the search itself. The slogan he popularised, now inscribed under many of his statues, reads in the historian M. S. S. Pandian's translation <q>He who created god is a fool, he who propagates god is a scoundrel, and he who worships god is a barbarian.</q> In 2019 the Madras High Court refused to have such inscriptions removed, finding that he had held these views from 1928.

His reasoning ran from caste to god. Pandian quotes him: <q>If there is no varnashrama dharma, there is no ground to talk about Hindu religion.</q> Varnashrama dharma, the order of castes and life stages, was in his view the content of Hinduism, the scriptures were its charter and the Brahmin priest its agent, so all three had to go. <q>God and fate are the direct enemies of reason</q>, he said.

His argument resembles Jyotirao Phule's reading of the Puranas as the record of a conquest (Part 1). He shared much with B. R. Ambedkar (Part 11). Periyar helped bring Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* into Tamil in 1937, and the two first met in Bombay in January 1940 (on the 6th or the 8th in different accounts) and again in Madras in 1944. Ambedkar ended in Buddhism; Periyar never converted.

## Critiques and questions

**Brahmins.** Supporters such as the writer Olivannan Gopalakrishnan say he attacked Brahminical authority and not individuals. Critics cite his words. The writer P. A. Krishnan quotes, in his own translation, a speech of 31 August 1959: <q>Brahminism came from Brahmins and hence it is the Brahmins who should be annihilated.</q> A Tamil saying about killing a Brahmin before a snake is widely attributed to him; I could not trace it to a dated speech or text.

**The gods.** In 1953 he announced that the Kazhagam would break images of Pillaiyar, the elephant-headed Ganesha; Bala Jeyaraman's biography dates the announcement to 27 May, Buddha Purnima. This was a separate campaign from the one against Rama. In 1956 he organised a procession to burn pictures of Rama on the Marina in Madras and was arrested; one account also records an arrest for burning such pictures in 1955. Accounts of a 1971 rally at Salem, where a picture of Rama was struck with a slipper, still differ. In 1957 he urged followers to burn copies of the Constitution and was sentenced to six months in prison. Jawaharlal Nehru, in a letter to K. Kamaraj of 5 November 1957, called him a lunatic.

**Gandhi.** In 1927 he and a friend met Gandhi and, by Periyar's account, named the tasks ahead as the destruction of the Congress, of the Hindu religion and of Brahmin domination. Gandhi's answer did not satisfy them. After Gandhi's assassination in 1948 he proposed that India be renamed Gandhi Desh, then resumed his attacks. In his last speech, in December 1973, he listed his aims, in Krishnan's translation, as <q>annihilation of God, annihilation of religion, annihilation of Gandhi, annihilation of Congress and annihilation of Brahmins</q>.

**Tamil.** He is widely quoted, from his book *Tamizhum Tamizharum* (Tamil and the Tamils), as calling Tamil a barbarian language; the original wording and date have not been traced here to a scholarly source. In the passage usually cited he explains that he meant the language needed reform more than reverence, and his defenders say the phrase is quoted out of context. Since late 2024 Seeman, leader of the Naam Tamilar Katchi, has said that Periyar insulted Tamil, and he rejects the Dravidian label itself: <q>We will never accept that we are Dravidians. We are Tamils.</q>

**The marriage.** He presented the marriage to Maniammai as a legal arrangement to secure his property and the trust that would carry on his work. Annadurai and others objected to it, and it occasioned the 1949 split, though the writer Puthiya Parithi has argued that older quarrels over elections, cinema and the meaning of Independence would have split them anyway.
