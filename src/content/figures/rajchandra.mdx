## Life

Rajchandra Mehta was born on 9 November 1867 in Vavania, a port village in the Morbi region of Kathiawar. His father, Ravjibhai, was a Vaishnava Hindu; his mother, Devbai, was a Sthanakvasi Jain, so he grew up in a house where both traditions were present. Gujarati devotees call him Shrimad Rajchandra, with *Shrimad* an honorific, and Gandhi's English spelling is Raychandbhai.

The movements that revere him tell of an unusual childhood. By their account he recalled hundreds of past lives at the age of seven after seeing a neighbour's cremation, finished seven school years in two, and was known as a child poet. Wikipedia dates the recollection to 1874 and the Shrimad Rajchandra Mission to 1875. These are devotional claims and no outside source confirms them. What is documented is that he learned the memory discipline called *avadhana*, the performance of many tasks at once, after seeing it at Morbi in 1884. On 22 January 1887 he performed a hundred-fold *shatavadhana* at the Framji Cowasji Institute in Bombay and earned the title Sakshat Saraswati, "Saraswati in person." He then declined to continue, because he believed fame would hinder his spiritual life. In the same year he published *Mokshamala*, a short primer of Jain teaching for young readers.

He married Jhabakben in 1887 (Wikipedia) or 1888 (the Mission), and worked for roughly a decade in the pearl and diamond trade in Bombay. Gandhi, then a young barrister back from London, was introduced to him in Bombay in 1891. Gandhi recalled being struck by his memory, and described a test in which Rajchandra repeated back words in the precise order in which Gandhi had given them. When Gandhi went to South Africa the two kept up a correspondence. The movement dates Rajchandra's own self-realisation to 1890, at the age of twenty-two or twenty-three.

From the middle of the 1890s he withdrew from business for long stretches, staying in the hills of Idar and in forest villages of Gujarat. In 1896 he wrote the *Atmasiddhi* at Nadiad. He died on 9 April 1901, aged thirty-three. A letter of 1888 on the six truths of the soul, the *Chha Padno Patra*, is the seed of the poem. His disciple Saubhagbhai is said to have asked for it in verse because the letter was hard to memorise. By the tradition's account Rajchandra wrote all 142 verses in about ninety minutes one night while a follower, Ambalal, held a lantern.

## The teaching

The *Atmasiddhi*, "the accomplishment of the self," is a dialogue between a seeker and a teacher. The teacher takes the seeker through six points, called *padas*, which the poem treats as the minimum a person must hold to be on the path:

1. The soul exists.
2. It is permanent.
3. It is the doer of its own actions.
4. It experiences the results of those actions.
5. Liberation exists.
6. There is a way to reach it.

Each point answers a doubt that a sceptic or a materialist would raise, and the poem argues for each in turn. The six points are standard Jain positions, put into Gujarati verse short enough to memorise.

Two themes give the poem its edge. The first is the bigot. In verses 24 to 33 Rajchandra describes the person who clings to ritual, to a sect, or to a teacher's outward form, and who mistakes these for the goal. Against him stands the seeker (verses 34 to 42), marked by calm, a lessening of passion and a wish for liberation that is real. The second theme is the living teacher. The poem insists that a person cannot see the self from books alone, and that one who has seen it must point the way. The right view, *samyaktva*, comes by being in the presence of such a person and then doing the work oneself.

Liberation, in his account, depends on neither caste, sect, sex nor monastic status, and can be reached by a layperson (Wikipedia's summary). That placed him outside the sectarian quarrels of his day, and may help explain why followers from several Jain backgrounds have claimed him.

## Lineage

Rajchandra sits under the Jain tradition on the tree and has no recorded guru. His strongest line runs forward in two directions.

<MiniTree id="rajchandra" />

The best-documented edge is to Gandhi. Gandhi's autobiography lists "three moderns" who left a deep impress on his life, and Rajchandra is the first. Gandhi also wrote that "I could not enthrone him in my heart as my Guru," and that "In my moments of spiritual crisis, therefore, he was my refuge." The other line runs to Kanji Swami, who was born in 1890 or 1889 and never met him. Kanji Swami read Rajchandra's writings in the 1920s and published a commentary on the *Atmasiddhi* in 1943. A third line, which the sources attest, runs to the Shrimad Rajchandra Mission at Dharampur, to Dada Bhagwan and to other lay movements that cite him.
