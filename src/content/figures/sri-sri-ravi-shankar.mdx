<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Followers call him Gurudev, or Sri Sri, a doubled honorific that also keeps him apart from the sitar player Ravi Shankar (1920–2012). The Art of Living is the name of his foundation and of its basic course.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>In 1982 Ravi Shankar was twenty-six and had been teaching meditation for several years, first as part of Maharishi Mahesh Yogi's organisation and then on his own. That year he went to Shimoga, in the hills of western Karnataka, and kept silence for ten days on the banks of the Bhadra river. The organisation's biography adds that he fasted.</p>
<p>He has described what came out of those days in plain terms. <q>During a ten-day period of silence, the Sudarshan Kriya came like an inspiration,</q> runs his account on the foundation's site, and a 2007 <i>Reader's Digest</i> profile has him saying it came <q>like a poem, an inspiration</q>. He learned it himself and began teaching it. He called it the Sudarshan Kriya, and almost everything his organisation has done since is built around it.</p>
</section>

## Life

Ravi Shankar was born on 13 May 1956 in Papanasam, a town in the Thanjavur district of Tamil Nadu, to R.S. Venkat Ratnam and Vishalakshi. His family named him Ravi, "sun," because he was born on a Sunday, and Shankar after the eighth-century philosopher Shankara, whose birthday in the traditional calendar fell on the same day. The Art of Living's biography says he could recite parts of the *Bhagavad Gita* by the age of four. His first teacher was Sudhakar Chaturvedi, a Vedic scholar who had been close to Gandhi. He took a degree in physics from St Joseph's College in Bangalore in 1973, at seventeen, and his foundation adds that he also studied Vedic literature.

From the mid-1970s he worked with Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, the founder of Transcendental Meditation, whose chapter appears earlier in this part. He travelled with Maharishi, gave talks, arranged conferences on what Maharishi called Vedic science, and helped set up meditation and Ayurveda centres. He was one of many young Indians in that circle, and the apprenticeship taught him how a meditation teaching could be packaged into courses, taught by trained instructors and carried to many countries at once.

<aside class="note"><strong>Maharishi's model.</strong> Transcendental Meditation is taught in a fixed course by certified teachers, for a fee, with the technique kept private to each student. The Art of Living took over much of this structure, with breath in the place of the mantra.</aside>

He left Maharishi's organisation at the start of the 1980s. The Art of Living Foundation dates its founding to 1981, a year before the silence at Shimoga, so the organisation existed in a small way before its central practice did. After 1982 the course took shape around the Sudarshan Kriya. The first course outside India was held in Switzerland in 1983, and the first in North America at Apple Valley, California, in 1986. The foundation has held consultative status with the United Nations Economic and Social Council since 1996. In 1997 he and the foundation set up a sister body, the International Association for Human Values, for development and relief work and for programmes in schools and prisons.

The movement grew very fast. Its centre is an ashram on the Kanakapura Road outside Bengaluru. The foundation's own pages give reach figures that change from page to page and year to year; one English version speaks of more than 370 million people in 180 countries. These numbers cannot be checked independently. What is clear is that the basic course, with the Sudarshan Kriya at its heart, is taught in a great many countries by volunteer teachers trained by the foundation, and that his talks fill stadiums.

He has also spent a good deal of time on public mediation. He met Colombian government and FARC delegations in Havana in 2015, visited Iraq several times between 2007 and 2014, and in March 2019 the Supreme Court of India named him one of three members of a panel asked to mediate the Ayodhya land dispute. The panel did not produce a settlement, and the court decided the case itself later that year. He received the Padma Vibhushan, India's second-highest civilian honour, in 2016.

## The teaching

Sri Sri Ravi Shankar's teaching rests on a simple claim about the breath. Every emotion, he says, has a matching rhythm of breathing. Anger, fear and sadness each change how we breathe, and by working the other way, changing the rhythm of the breath on purpose, a person can change the state of the mind. The breath is the link between body and mind that anyone can reach, and a skilled sequence of breathing can do in minutes what years of sitting meditation try to do slowly.

This is an old idea in yoga. The *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, a fifteenth-century manual, says that when the breath moves the mind moves, and when the breath is still the mind is still. What Sri Sri added was a specific, fixed sequence, given a name, taught the same way everywhere, and placed at the centre of a short course for ordinary people with jobs and families.

**The Sudarshan Kriya.** The name means roughly "purifying action that gives right vision." Published descriptions, including those by researchers who have studied it, describe a session that begins with slow breathing against a slight constriction in the throat, moves to forceful "bellows" breathing, then to chanting Om, and ends with the kriya itself: cycles of breathing in slow, medium and fast rhythms, followed by rest lying down. The foundation teaches the full practice only in its courses, through its own teachers, and asks students to practise a shorter home version daily.

**Vedanta in a light voice.** Around the breathing sits a talk-based teaching that is broadly Vedantic. He tells his audiences that the self is consciousness, that the world is a play of that consciousness, and that suffering comes from the mind's grip on the past and its worry about the future. He has published commentaries on classic texts, among them the *Ashtavakra Gita*, a radical Advaita dialogue, and the *Narada Bhakti Sutra*, a manual of devotion, and he draws freely on both. His tone is playful. Many of his talks are short answers to audience questions, and he laughs often.

**Service and human values.** The third strand is what he calls human values: belonging, compassion, a sense of a shared humanity across religions. His courses end with a call to volunteer, and the foundation's relief work, rural programmes and prison courses are presented as the teaching put into practice. The focus of this book is the teaching, so these programmes appear here only as the frame in which the breathing is taught.

### What the research shows

The Sudarshan Kriya has been studied more than almost any other branded practice in this book, and the evidence deserves a careful summary.

The earliest clinical work came from India in the late 1990s and early 2000s, including small trials at the National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences in Bengaluru on people with depression. A 2013 review in the *International Journal of Yoga* by Sameer and Rakesh Zope gathered this literature and concluded that there was <q>mounting evidence to suggest that SKY can be a beneficial, low-risk, low-cost adjunct</q> to treatment for stress, anxiety, depression and related conditions. The wording is careful, and the review did not grade the quality of the studies it collected.

Later trials were better designed. In a 2020 randomised trial at Yale, 131 students were divided among a no-treatment control group and three eight-week programmes, one of them the foundation's student course. Compared with the control group, the breathing course showed the largest gains in measures of mental health and well-being. The groups were small, between 21 and 47 people each. In a 2022 trial at a United States Veterans Affairs centre, 85 veterans with post-traumatic stress were randomised to the Sudarshan Kriya course or to cognitive processing therapy, a standard treatment. The breathing course was not worse than the therapy on the main measure of post-traumatic symptoms, at the end of treatment and a year later. On secondary measures of depression and mood, it fell behind the therapy at one year. Only 59 of the 85 finished, and the authors asked for caution.

The fair summary is that a structured breathing course appears to reduce stress, anxiety and some depressive symptoms in the short term, with effects comparable to some established treatments in a few small trials. The studies are mostly small, many are short, and blinding participants to a breathing practice is very hard. Some researchers have worked closely with the foundation or its sister organisations, which does not invalidate their work but is a reason for independent replication. A 2022 *Financial Times* profile noted that one paper described the core technique as rhythmic hyperventilation. None of this research addresses the larger claims of the teaching about consciousness or liberation, and it could not.

I find Sri Sri useful for thinking about this part's question. He takes the oldest insight of yoga, that breath and mind move together, and turns it into something close to a technology, standardised, teachable in a weekend and measurable in a lab. The research suggests the technology does something real for stress. Whether it does what the old texts promised the breath could do, the stilling of the mind into its source, is a question his trials were never designed to answer, and he himself treats the breathing as a beginning.

## Lineage

His tree is short. Above him are his childhood teacher Sudhakar Chaturvedi and, more importantly, Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, with whom he lived and worked for several years. Maharishi in turn was the disciple of Brahmananda Saraswati, the Shankaracharya of Jyotirmath, so a formal Advaita line runs above Sri Sri through his teacher's teacher. He has not claimed initiation into that line himself, and the Sudarshan Kriya comes, by his account, from his own experience at Shimoga.

## The practice

The full Sudarshan Kriya is taught only by trained teachers, and the foundation does not publish the instructions. The outline below follows published research descriptions and is meant to show the shape of the practice. It is no substitute for instruction.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Sit upright and breathe slowly through a slightly narrowed throat</strong> (<i>ujjayi</i>), so that the breath is audible, in a set count.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Breathe forcefully in and out through the nose</strong> (<i>bhastrika</i>, "bellows"), in short rounds, with rests between.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Chant Om</strong> a few times, slowly.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Do the kriya itself:</strong> cycles of breathing in slow, medium and fast rhythms, guided by a teacher or a recording.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Lie down and rest.</strong> Teachers describe this as the point where the effects are felt.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Practise the short home version daily,</strong> and attend a longer group session weekly, as the foundation recommends.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**The World Culture Festival, 2016.** From 11 to 13 March 2016 the foundation held a festival to mark its thirty-fifth year on the floodplain of the Yamuna in Delhi, near the DND Flyway, on land allotted by the Delhi Development Authority. Environmental campaigners challenged it before the National Green Tribunal. On 9 March 2016 the tribunal let the event go ahead and directed the foundation to deposit 5 crore rupees as interim compensation. In 2017 an expert committee appointed by the tribunal reported that about 180 hectares of floodplain had been damaged, and estimated restoration at over 42 crore rupees and at least ten years. The foundation called the report <q>flawed, unscientific and biased</q>, and pointed out that two committee members had not signed it. Sri Sri said that if any fine had to be levied, it should fall on <q>the Central and state governments and the NGT itself</q>, since they had granted permission.

On 7 December 2017 the tribunal held the foundation responsible for the damage, ordered that the 5 crore rupees be used by the Development Authority for restoration, and allowed the Authority to recover more if the cost was higher. It imposed no further penalty, citing the foundation's undertaking to help restore the site. It also found the Development Authority guilty of contributory negligence for allowing the event. The foundation appealed, through its associated body Vyakti Vikas Kendra India. On 22 August 2026 the Supreme Court, in a bench of Justices Satish Chandra Sharma and N. Kotiswar Singh, set the tribunal's order aside. It found the expert report <q>of a sweeping nature</q>, held that the floodplain was already damaged before the site was handed over, and said that damage, <q>if any, cannot be attributed to the appellant</q>. It ordered the Development Authority to refund the 5 crore rupees within four weeks, said that permission <q>to hold the event on an active floodplain of the river cannot be approved</q>, and directed the Authority to continue the rehabilitation work. No further proceedings had been reported as of October 2026.

**How strong is the science?** As the research section above shows, the evidence for short-term benefits is real but rests on small trials. The foundation's publicity sometimes presents it as settled, and some critics note that several researchers have links to the foundation's network. The stronger claims made for the practice, for example about immunity or long-term health, rest on thinner evidence.

**Criticism of the organisation.** From about 2009 two anonymous former members, writing as "Skywalker" and "Klim," ran blogs criticising the foundation's practices and its leader, and posted excerpts from its course manuals. In November 2010 the foundation's United States branch sued them in federal court in California for copyright infringement, misuse of trade secrets and defamation, saying the blogs published its confidential teaching materials. In June 2011 the court dismissed the defamation claims, finding the statements to be opinion. In 2012 the parties settled and the suit was dismissed with prejudice.

**Public statements.** Some of his remarks on public questions have drawn criticism. In 2012 he linked government schools to the growth of Naxalism, the Maoist insurgency, and later said his words had been misunderstood. In 2018 he said India could "turn into Syria" if the Ayodhya dispute was not resolved, which critics called inflammatory. In October 2019 a group of students and faculty at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru objected to a talk he was invited to give there, calling his organisation's claims about mental health unscientific. He gave the talk and replied that a person with prejudice could not call himself a scientist. His involvement in the Ayodhya mediation and his public closeness to political leaders of several parties are matters of record. This book states them without judgement.

## Descendants

Sri Sri has no named successor, and his descendants are the teachers and members of the Art of Living, which the foundation says reaches 180 countries. His influence on other teachers is mostly indirect. The Sudarshan Kriya made breathwork a familiar part of global wellness culture, and the clinical trials of it are now part of a wider scientific literature on slow and cyclic breathing. Within this part, he and Sadhguru are the two living teachers who most clearly turned a single practice into a mass course.
