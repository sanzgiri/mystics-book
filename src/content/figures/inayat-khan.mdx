<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> His followers call him Pir-o-Murshid Hazrat Inayat Khan. <i>Murshid</i> means a spiritual guide, <i>pir</i> an elder or saint, and <i>hazrat</i> an honorific. To them he is simply Murshid.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Hyderabad, in 1907 or 1908. A young singer in his mid-twenties sits near his teacher. In his own later account, the teacher has foretold the day of his death, settled his affairs and called the disciples around him. The group is reciting <i>zikr</i>, the Sufi practice of repeating the names of God, and it is during this remembrance that the old man dies.</p>
<p>Before that, according to the same account, he laid his hands on the disciple's head and gave him a charge. The words as Inayat Khan recalled them were: "Spread the wisdom of Sufism abroad, for to this end art thou gifted by Allah, the most merciful and compassionate." The disciple was a professional musician with a gold-medal reputation in southern India, and his teacher was sending him to a continent he had never seen.</p>
<p>This scene comes from Inayat Khan's own autobiographical essay, so it is the story as he told it. Other sources agree on the charge and differ on the year of the death.</p>

## Life

**A family of musicians.** Inayat Khan was born in Baroda on 5 July 1882. His mother, Khatija Bi, was the daughter of Maulabakhsh, a famous singer and a pioneer of musical notation in India, who in 1886 founded the Gayanshala, an academy of music, under the patronage of the ruler of Baroda, Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad. His father, Rahmat Khan, came from a Punjabi family of landowners, poets and musicians, and also taught. Maulabakhsh died in 1896, when the boy was fourteen, and the grandson grew up in the house his grandfather had made a school.

The movement's own biographical notes say that at nine he sang a Sanskrit hymn at a court ceremony, that at fourteen he published a music textbook, *Balasan Gitmala*, and that he became a professor at the Gayanshala before he turned twenty. He completed the academy's five-year course under his grandfather, and an uncle taught him Western music. In 1903 he published a songbook, *Inayat Geet-Ratnavali*, which he dedicated to the Maharaja. He was trained in both Hindustani and Karnatak music and learned early how to perform before rulers.

Devotees add an inner story. They say he left home at twelve for a period of wandering and that his grandfather's talk about the signs of God in the world was a first initiation. This is the movement's narrative of a boyhood, told after the fact, and it cannot be checked.

**Hyderabad and the Murshid.** In about 1903 he went to Hyderabad, then the capital of the Nizam's state. He wrote *Minqar-i Musiqar* there, a book in Urdu that presented his grandfather's system of music to Urdu readers. The Nizam, Mahbub Ali Khan, is said to have named him "Tansen of the age," after the legendary court singer of Akbar. He toured the south, with pilgrimages to Sufi shrines and stays in Madras, Mysore, Ceylon and Burma.

In Hyderabad he met a Sufi teacher, Sayyid Muhammad Abu Hashim Madani, described by the movement as a descendant of the Prophet from a family of Medina and the head of the Chishti-Nizami-Kalimi line. Inayat Khan says he first saw the man's face in a meditative vision and recognised him when he arrived. In his words, the teacher "granted the request, initiating me into Sufism there and then." The Ruhaniat organisation dates the meeting to 1904 and says he stayed with his murshid for four years. Sources disagree on when Madani died: 1907 in one biographical summary, 1908 in the Inayati Order's account.

<aside class="note"><strong>The four orders.</strong> A Sufi order, or <i>silsila</i> ("chain"), traces a line of teachers back to the Prophet. The Chishti order, founded around the Afghan town of Chisht and rooted in India since the thirteenth century, is the best known in South Asia. The Suhrawardi, Qadiri and Naqshbandi are three other major orders, each with its own emphasis in practice.</aside>

**The four orders.** The Chishti line is the one every account gives first. Wikipedia traces it through Shah Kalim Allah Jahanabadi, and the Inayati Order calls it the Chishti-Nizami-Kalimi. Inayat Khan himself wrote that "I went through a course of training in the four schools: the Chishtia, Naqshibandi, Qadiri, and Suhrwardi." The claim to all four rests on his own account and on the later writing of his movement. The sources consulted for this chapter do not describe a separate ceremony of initiation for each order or identify other teachers who gave them, and an independent record of the training has not been found. What is documented is the Chishti initiation and the four years in Hyderabad.

**The journey west.** After his father died in 1910, Inayat Khan left Baroda on 13 September 1910 with his brother Maheboob Khan and his cousin Mohammed Ali Khan. His brother Musharaff followed a year later. They sailed for the United States and performed as the Royal Hindustani Musicians. According to his own biography, a Brahmin had told him that he must go to the Western world, and he took the loss of his collected medals as a sign that music had done its work in his life. He was carrying the charge to "harmonize the East and the West with the harmony of thy music."

In America the plan was to lecture as well as play. In San Francisco, in 1910 or 1911, he met Ada Martin, who became his first American disciple under the name Rabia Martin. In 1910 he also met Ora Ray Baker, an American who began music lessons with him. They married in London on 20 March 1913. She took the name Ameena Begum, and the couple had four children: Noor (born 1 January 1914 in Moscow), Vilayat (born 19 June 1916 in London), Hidayat (born 6 August 1917) and Khair-un-Nisa, called Claire (born 3 June 1919).

**The Sufi Order and the Sufi Movement.** After Russia, Paris and Geneva, the family settled in London in 1914. The date of the Sufi Order is hard to fix. The Wikipedia biography gives 1914, a chronology on the wahiduddin.net archive says 1918, and a scholarly paper says that he chartered the order in 1915. The surviving letters give a clue. Inayat Khan was writing "International Sufi Order" on letterheads by early 1915, and a printed letterhead reading "Sufi Order: Brotherhood of Purity" appears on a letter of 20 October 1915. The first issue of *The Sufi*, the order's magazine, came out in February 1915. A khankah, a Sufi lodge, was opened at 86 Ladbroke Road in London. Several women held senior posts from the start. Sophia Saintsbury-Green, Lucy Goodenough (Sharifa) and Jessie Dowland (Nargis) were all given the rank of khalifa or murshida, and he put the London order in Nargis's charge when he left England.

The family moved to the Paris suburbs in 1920 and, in the early 1920s, to Fazal Manzil, a house at Suresnes bought by a wealthy supporter. On 11 October 1923, by the Sufi Movement's own chronology, the Sufi Movement was founded, with its international headquarters in Geneva. Summer schools ran at Wissous, Katwijk in Holland and Suresnes between 1921 and 1926. On 13 September 1926 he laid the cornerstone of a planned temple of Universal Worship at Suresnes, which was never built there.

**Universal Worship.** The Sufi Movement's chronology records that Universal Worship was introduced in London on 7 May at 35 Tregunter Road and held for the first time in the Netherlands on 3 September; the page gives no years, and the early 1920s is the likely range. Sophia Saintsbury-Green, a murshida, is described as the first person to help him found the "Church of All" in England. The service uses candles lit for different teachers and religions, and the altar holds scriptures of the Hindus, Hebrews, Zoroastrians, Buddhists, Christians and Muslims. His followers say it is meant as the continuation of one old religion and not as a new one.

**Return and death.** He sailed for India in 1926 and landed at Karachi in October. His journey took him through Lahore, Delhi, Amroha, Lucknow, Benares, Agra, Ajmer, Jaipur and Baroda. In Delhi he lectured at the university on Sufism and on the Hindu-Muslim divide, and he stayed with Khwaja Hasan Nizami. He fell ill with a cold after a visit to Ajmer and died of pneumonia on 5 February 1927 at Tilak Lodge in Delhi, on the bank of the Yamuna. He was buried near the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya, the great Chishti saint of fourteenth-century Delhi. His followers observe the day as his *urs*, the death anniversary of a Sufi, which they call Visalat Day.

## The teaching

**One God, one message.** Inayat Khan started from the oneness of God, *tawhid* in Arabic, and drew wide conclusions from it. If God is one, he argued, truth is one, and the religions are variations of a single message. His text on Universal Worship puts it this way: "There never has been any other religion than one, for God is one, truth is one, and so religion is one." The same message, he said, had come through Moses, Jesus, Muhammad, Buddha and Krishna.

He summarised the position in the Ten Sufi Thoughts, a creed he wrote for his followers. The first says "There is one God, the Eternal, the Only Being; none else exists save God." The fourth defines religion as "the unswerving progress in the right direction towards the ideal." The last gives the path: "the annihilation of the false ego in the real." The language of the last thought comes from the Sufi tradition, in which the loss of the self before God is called *fana*.

**Music and sound.** Music was his first trade and remained his main way of teaching. "My music is my religion," the Inayati Order's biography quotes him as saying. His posthumous lectures include a volume on the mysticism of sound. Asked by the Nizam why his music moved listeners, he is reported to have said that his music was his thought and his thought was his emotion. The idea that sound and vibration lie behind all things, and that the trained musician can use it to open the heart, is one the Chishti tradition already knew from its sessions of devotional music, called *sama*.

**The Sufi Message.** He called his teaching a message rather than a doctrine. It had a moral side, with ideals of harmony, service and love, and a mystical side that trained students in what he called the five grades of Sufism, which he listed as physical, intellectual, mental, moral and spiritual. He insisted that the highest knowledge came through devotion to a teacher. The path, he wrote, opened "the ways of light" through devotion to his murshid, which could not be gained "through discussion or argument, reading, writing, nor mystical exercises."

**No requirement to convert.** The Sufi Order in the West did not require students to become Muslims. A disciple could remain a Christian, a Jew or a Hindu, or belong to no religion, and still be initiated. He appointed women to leadership posts, which was a departure from the usual practice of Sufi lodges.

I find the practices he taught less new than the way he presented them. A Chishti shaykh would recognise the teacher-student bond, the remembrance, the music and the aim of losing the self. What a shaykh of the thirteenth century would not recognise is a creed that names no religion, a service with scriptures from six or more religions on one altar, or a lodge in Kensington with a printed magazine. The scholars who study the order disagree about how far these changes separate it from the tradition it came from, and I take that argument up under Critiques and questions.

## Lineage

Inayat Khan stands at the point where the Chishti line, which runs from the great shrines of Delhi and Ajmer, meets the Western spiritual movements of the early twentieth century. His teacher in Hyderabad gave him the Chishti link, and his grandfather Maulabakhsh gave him the music.

The edge from the Sufi orders to Inayat Khan is documented in the sense that his Chishti initiation is recorded in his own writing and in the accounts of his movement. The wider claim of training in the Suhrawardi, Qadiri and Naqshbandi lines is his own and has not been independently confirmed. The edge from Maulabakhsh is a family and musical one. The edges to Rabia Martin and to his successors begin in the West and are well documented. Some of them are contested, as the succession section below shows.

## The practice

The Inayati Order and the Sufi Movement keep their detailed practices for initiates, and the lines below describe only what he and his movement have said in public.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Find a teacher.</strong> The central bond is between murshid and <i>mureed</i>, the student. Inayat Khan's own teacher described it in the words "a friendship that never ends."</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Follow the five grades.</strong> He described training that moves from the physical to the intellectual, mental, moral and spiritual levels.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Work through the Inner School.</strong> The movement describes four stages of practice, called concentration, contemplation, meditation and realisation.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Remember God.</strong> Zikr, the repetition of divine names, is a Chishti practice, and Inayat Khan's account shows his own teacher's circle at it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Attend Universal Worship.</strong> This is a public service in which candles are lit for different religions and scriptures are read.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Is it still Sufism, and is it still Islam?** Inayat Khan was born a Muslim and was initiated into a Chishti line, and the Inayati Order keeps Chishti elements. A 2013 article by Celia Genn in the *Journal for the Academic Study of Religion* says that separating Sufism from the legal tradition of Islam "was part of Inayat Khan's vision," and that tensions over Islam remain in the movement. A 2020 University of Virginia master's thesis by Keenan Field argues the opposite emphasis. It holds that academic writers stressed the order's universalism and eclectic rituals and neglected its Sufi and Islamic roots. The Wikipedia article on Western Sufism notes that the phrase "Universal Sufism," often attached to his work, was coined by a later follower and that he never used it.

**Orthodox Muslim criticism.** A 2016 article in *Ulum Islamiyyah* by Mohamed Mihlar Abdul Muthaliff of the Universiti Sains Islam Malaysia examines Inayat Khan's thought from an orthodox Islamic position. It says that "Inayat Khan's mystical thoughts and teachings are completely against Islamic principles." The author argues that his idea of God does not respect the category of tawhid, that his account of the divinity of the human soul is rejected by Islam, and that his unity of religious ideals removes what distinguishes Islam from other faiths. Another 2016 paper in the university's repository, titled "Inayatian Mystical Movement As A Deviant Sect In Contemporary Muslim World," says in its abstract that the message "creates contradictions and confusions" in the basics of Islam. The page does not name its authors. Both are the judgments of individual scholars. Celia Genn's article notes that the Inayati groups keep significant strands of Indian Chishti Sufism and take varied stances toward Islam and toward the word Sufi.

**Succession and splits.** After 1927 the movement divided. Rabia Martin, who had been his first American disciple, broke away when Maheboob Khan took over, and Samuel Lewis went with her at first. She later affiliated with Meher Baba, and her work gave rise to Sufism Reoriented. Lewis later founded the line that became Sufi Ruhaniat International. Inayat Khan's son Vilayat left the leadership of the Sufi Movement to others, then in the 1950s and 1960s built a separate organisation on his father's original London constitution. His nephew Mahmood Khan has said, according to a 2018 paper by Kuehn and Pokorny, that the order's categorisation of esoteric teachings is an outdated vestige that should be revised. None of these disputes involved any court proceeding in the sources consulted.

## Descendants

The Sufi Movement, with its headquarters in Geneva, passed to his brother Maheboob Khan, whom a Sufi Movement council is recorded as appointing as esoteric leader in June of the year of Inayat's death, and who led until his own death in 1948. His cousin Mohammed Ali Khan was elected next. Vilayat Inayat Khan, born in London in 1916, deferred to the elders and took up his father's mantle in 1956 or 1957 (sources differ). In 1968 the name Sufi Order was revived for his own group, later the Sufi Order International and since 2016 the Inayati Order, renamed Inayatiyya in 2020. His son Zia Inayat Khan was invested in 2000 and succeeded him on his death on 17 June 2004. Fazal Inayat Khan left the Sufi Movement in 1988 to found The Sufi Way. In India the shrine in Delhi draws visitors on the *urs* every 5 February. Noor Inayat Khan, his eldest daughter, served as a wireless operator for Britain's Special Operations Executive in occupied France, was arrested in 1943, and was shot at Dachau on 13 September 1944. She was awarded the George Cross after the war.
