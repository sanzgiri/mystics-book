## Life

S. N. Goenka was born on 30 January 1924 in Mandalay, Burma, into a family of Indian origin that Wikipedia describes as Marwari and conservatively Hindu. He worked in business, and Wikipedia describes him as a successful businessman. Wikipedia places the start of severe migraines in 1955. Finding no medical relief, he met U Ba Khin, a Vipassana teacher in Rangoon, on a friend's suggestion. The Vipassana organisation's account says he took a course under U Ba Khin in 1955 and studied with him for fourteen years.

U Ba Khin (6 March 1899 to 19 January 1971) was the first Accountant General of independent Burma and taught meditation in his office and at the International Meditation Centre he founded in Rangoon in 1952. In 1969 he authorised Goenka to teach. The organisation's account says that U Ba Khin could not leave Burma himself and encouraged Goenka to take the Dhamma back to India. Both the organisation and Wikipedia date Goenka's move to India and his first courses there to 1969. U Ba Khin died in Rangoon in 1971. He taught alone until 1982, when he began training assistant teachers. He opened the centre Dhamma Giri at Igatpuri, near Nashik, in 1976 and founded the Vipassana Research Institute there in 1985. The foundation of the Global Vipassana Pagoda in Mumbai was laid in 2000, and the building opened in 2009. He received the Padma Bhushan in 2012, and he died at home in Mumbai on 29 September 2013, aged 89. Wikipedia counts about 393 locations in more than 94 countries where courses are held.

## Why this figure appears here

This part asks whether the Buddha can come home. Goenka's answer is a method. He presented Vipassana as open to people of any faith or none and rooted in the Buddha's teaching, and his organisation calls the approach "totally non-sectarian". The sources read do not call it secular. Courses are free, and food and lodging are paid for by donations from former students.

The line he inherited is a Burmese one. The organisation traces the practice to the Buddha and says it vanished from India five centuries later and survived in Burma. Its modern links are Ledi Sayadaw (1846 to 1923), a scholar-monk who is credited with making Vipassana available to laypeople as well as monks, and Saya Thetgyi (1873 to 1945), a layman who studied under Ledi for seven years and taught in his village. U Ba Khin studied under Thetgyi. Wikipedia dates his first full course at Thetgyi's centre to 1937, and the organisation says he studied under him from 1941, so the date remains open. Wikipedia adds that the arahant Webu Sayadaw urged U Ba Khin to teach in 1941. This chain is the tradition's own account. The edges on the tree are marked documented where a source read names the teacher and student.

What Goenka added was mostly the format. The ten-day course is residential and held under a code of discipline. For its length students keep five precepts: no killing, stealing, sexual activity, false speech or intoxicants. They observe noble silence, in which they do not speak to other students, and they do not read, write or follow other religious practices. The first days train attention on the natural breath (anapana). By the fourth day students begin Vipassana, observing bodily sensations and learning to meet them with equanimity. On the last full day they learn metta, loving-kindness, and share what they have developed with all beings. Public instruction during the course is given through Goenka's recorded audio and video talks, with assistant teachers on site for questions.

The practical teaching centres on the body. The sources read for this sidebar describe the form of the course but give no direct account of how Goenka explained impermanence or the aim of the practice, so the sidebar leaves that open.

On the tree Goenka sits in the line from Ledi Sayadaw. The Burmese tradition reached India by more than one route, and Mahasi Sayadaw's method came through other teachers, as the Dipa Ma chapter shows. Goenka carried a Burmese lay course to Indian students and from there to centres abroad.
