<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Followers call him Soamiji Maharaj, "the revered Lord." The word <i>Radhasoami</i> joins <i>Radha</i>, which the movement reads as the soul, with <i>Soami</i>, "lord" or "master." He himself mostly spoke of the highest reality as <i>Sat Naam</i>, the True Name, and <i>Anami</i>, the Nameless. The name Radhasoami came into use through his disciple Salig Ram.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>The surviving accounts say little about the day itself. It was Basant Panchami, the spring festival of the fifth day of the bright half of Magh, which in 1861 fell on 15 February. In a house in Panni Gali, a lane in the old city of Agra, a man of forty-two who had spent most of his adult life in private meditation agreed to let anyone who wished come and listen to him.</p>
<p>Until then his meetings had been for family and a small circle. The request to open them came from Salig Ram, a postal official ten years younger, who had met him three years earlier and regarded him as more than a teacher. The meetings went on in that house for the next seventeen years. Every branch of the movement now called Radhasoami dates its beginning to that festival.</p>
</section>

## Life

Shiv Dayal Singh was born in Agra on 25 August 1818, in Panni Gali, the lane where he would also die. His family were Punjabi Khatris who had moved to Agra. The historian Mark Juergensmeyer notes that the British kept a large military base there that relied on Sikh recruits, and the family's move is linked to it. His father, Seth Dilwali Singh, was a *sahajdhari* Khatri, a Sikh by heritage who did not keep the full outward discipline of the baptised Khalsa. His mother was Mata Maha Maya. He had two younger brothers, Partap Singh, later known to followers as Chachaji Saheb, and Rai Bindraban. The household's religion mixed Vaishnava Hindu devotion with reverence for the Sikh scriptures.

The most important religious presence in the house was a teacher from the small town of Hathras, about fifty kilometres north of Agra. Tulsi Sahib was a poet and mystic in the Sant line, the tradition of Kabir, Nanak and others who taught that God is found inside the body, through the name and an inner sound, and needs no temple or caste. Shiv Dayal Singh's father, mother, mother-in-law and sister were all Tulsi Sahib's disciples, and Tulsi Sahib stayed in the family's house in Panni Gali and held meetings there. The boy grew up among his talks. The Radhasoami writer James Bean also cites an early source saying that he attended the meetings of a local Sant called Girdhari Das for a long period.

Whether Tulsi Sahib was his guru is one of the first disputed questions in the movement's history. Juergensmeyer, following the movement's early biographies, says he never took initiation from Tulsi Sahib and had no human guru. Other accounts, including statements attributed to Shiv Dayal Singh himself in a collection kept by his disciple Garib Das, credit the inner mercy of Tulsi Sahib and of Girdhari Das. The Agra branches have generally held that Soamiji was a self-realised master who needed no teacher. Writers outside those branches have tended to stress his debt to Hathras.

He was married young to Naraini Devi, daughter of Lala Izzat Rai of Faridabad. Followers later called her Radhaji. They had no children. He learned several languages and, according to the Peepal Mandi branch's biography, wrote a book in Persian. He worked for a time as a Persian translator for a government officer, then as a teacher of Persian, and then joined his father's moneylending business. None of these lasted long.

Tulsi Sahib died in 1843. For the next seventeen years, by the account given in the Beas edition of *Sar Bachan*, Shiv Dayal Singh practised what he later taught, mostly in seclusion. The Peepal Mandi biography says he had begun regular spiritual practice at the age of six. The movement's later histories speak of long periods when he barely left his room, though the earliest sources say only that he withdrew from work and gave his time to religious practice.

In 1858 Salig Ram, then a young official in the postal department, met him in Agra. Salig Ram was born in Peepal Mandi in Agra on 14 March 1829 and would rise to become, in 1881, Postmaster-General of the North-Western Provinces, the first Indian to hold that post. He became the most devoted and the most articulate of the disciples. It was Salig Ram who asked that the meetings be opened to the public, and on Basant Panchami in 1861 they were.

For the rest of his life Shiv Dayal Singh taught from the house in Panni Gali. He gave talks, sang and recited his verses, and initiated disciples into the practice of inner sound. Among those initiated was a young soldier from the Punjab. Jaimal Singh, born in July 1839 in a village near Batala, had joined the British Indian Army at seventeen. In 1856, according to the Beas branch, he came to Agra and received initiation into the practice of the five sounds. He served for many more years, rising to havildar, a sergeant, before retiring and settling outside the town of Beas.

The followers were a mix of Agra merchants, officials like Salig Ram, Punjabi soldiers and ascetics. Around 1866, by the Peepal Mandi account, the gathering began to be called the Radhasoami Satsang. Salig Ram had come to see his guru as the first human appearance of the Supreme Being, and he named that Supreme Being Radhasoami. The name spread through Salig Ram's writings and through the greeting the followers used with one another.

Shiv Dayal Singh died at Panni Gali on Saturday, 15 June 1878, at fifty-nine. The Peepal Mandi biography says his health failed because he was so absorbed in spiritual work that he neglected food and rest. A Dayalbagh translation of his last words, quoted by Bean, has him saying, "The Faith I had given out was that of Sat Naam and Anami." His remains were later enshrined in a garden outside the city, which became Soami Bagh. Work on the present marble shrine there began in 1904 or 1908, depending on the source, and has continued for more than a century.

His teaching survives in two books, both called *Sar Bachan*, "essential utterances." One is in verse, the *Sar Bachan Chhand Band*, a large collection of devotional and instructional poems that Salig Ram had printed in 1884. The other, in prose, combines an introduction by Salig Ram with notes of talks that Shiv Dayal Singh gave up to 1878. Neither was published in his lifetime.

## The teaching

Shiv Dayal Singh taught that the human soul is a drop of spirit that has fallen from its source, far above the physical world, and is now caught in the body and the senses. He called the attention, or the soul's capacity to attend, *surat*. He called the divine current that flows down from the highest region and back up to it *shabd*, the Word or Sound. The practice he taught, *surat shabd yoga*, joins the two. The soul withdraws its attention from the body and the world, gathers it at a centre behind the eyes, and there makes contact with the inner sound. The sound is both a sign of the way and the way itself. Followed with attention, it draws the soul back up through a series of inner regions to its home.

<aside class="note"><strong>In the original.</strong> <span class="script" lang="hi">सुरत शब्द योग</span> (<i>surat shabd yog</i>): "the yoga of joining the attention to the Sound."</aside>

### The regions

The map of those regions is the most distinctive part of the teaching, and he did not draw it from nothing. Tulsi Sahib's long poem *Ghat Ramayan*, "the Ramayana within the body," already set out a ladder of inner stations. In its later printed form, with a preface by his disciple Baba Devi Sahab, the list rises from the six bodily *chakras* of yoga through the thousand-petalled lotus (*sahasdal kanwal*), to Trikuti, a region of three peaks, to Sunn, the void, and the Great Void (Maha Sunn), to Bhanwar Gufa, the whirling cave, and on to Sat Lok, the True Region, and the higher regions of Alakh, the Invisible, Agam, the Inaccessible, and Anami, the Nameless.

Shiv Dayal Singh taught the same ascent, with each region marked by its own sound and its own light. In the verses of *Sar Bachan* the soul hears, at the different stages, sounds that the poems compare to a bell, a conch, thunder, a lute, a flute and a vina. The yogic *chakras* below the eyes, which many hatha yoga schools work to open one by one from the base of the spine, he treated as regions below the starting point. His practice began at the eye centre and went up. The Peepal Mandi site puts it plainly: "His surat-shabd-yoga is easier than pranayam and hath-yoga."

Salig Ram and later writers in the Agra lines added a further region above Anami and named it Radhasoami, the absolute source. This was one of the main ways the later movement went beyond its founder's own vocabulary. Shiv Dayal Singh's own poems speak most often of Sat Naam and Anami as the highest.

### The living master

The second pillar of the teaching is the *sant satguru*, the true teacher who has himself made the ascent. The inner sound, Shiv Dayal Singh taught, cannot be reached by reading, ritual or pilgrimage. It has to be connected by a living master at initiation and kept open by devotion to him. The Peepal Mandi biography sums this up: "the only pre-requisite to His yoga is true and sincere devotion to the Santsatguru."

This idea was old among the Sants, who had always honoured the guru. What was new was its weight. In Radhasoami teaching the inner form of the master accompanies the disciple through the regions, and the outer master is the visible presence of the supreme. Salig Ram took the step of calling his own guru the first incarnation of the Supreme Being. That claim made the succession question, who the living master is now, the most important question in the movement, and it has remained so.

### Ethics and community

The rest followed from these two pillars. Followers are expected to keep a strict lacto-vegetarian diet and to avoid alcohol, drugs and sex outside marriage. Celibacy is not required, and most later masters have been married householders. Caste plays no part in initiation. Scholars of the movement describe six elements that hold its communities together: the living guru, *bhajan* (the inner practice), *satsang* (the gathering), *seva* (service), the centre or shrine, and the *bhandara*, a large festival meal. No scripture is installed in the shrine, and the Guru Granth Sahib, though quoted constantly, is not kept and worshipped as in Sikh gurdwaras.

### Where he stood

In the setting of nineteenth-century north India, the teaching sat between several worlds. It drew on the Sikh scriptures for its language of Naam and Shabd and on Hindu yoga for its map of the body, and it rejected the ritual of both. It addressed householders who wanted a discipline that could be practised daily, at home, alongside an office job or a business. It also offered something more ambitious than most devotional movements of its time, a precise inner geography and a technique for moving through it.

The more I read of the early Radhasoami material, the harder it is to separate Shiv Dayal Singh from the people around him. Salig Ram gave the faith its name, much of its theology and its first books. Jaimal Singh carried the practice to the Punjab, where it took a plainer, more Sikh-flavoured form. The founder himself is mostly visible through his verses, which are long, repetitive and full of the joy of a man describing places he has been. I have tried to keep his own vocabulary of Sat Naam and Anami apart from what later branches made of it.

## Lineage

Shiv Dayal Singh's teaching came out of the Sant tradition of north India, through Tulsi Sahib of Hathras, and he used the Sikh scriptures constantly. After his death the tree divides more than any other in this book.

The succession is disputed, and each branch tells it differently. What the sources agree on is that several people began to hold their own meetings after 1878. They included his widow, Radhaji, his brother Partap Singh, Sanmukh Das, Garib Das in Delhi, Jaimal Singh in the Punjab and Salig Ram in Agra. Some scholars hold that he passed the leadership to Salig Ram. Other branches dispute this. The scholar David Lane has charted many separate lines of guru succession that descend from the Agra founder, some of them now extinct.

**The Agra lines.** Salig Ram, known to his followers as Huzur Maharaj, led the largest Agra group from 1878 until his death on 6 December 1898. The leadership after him split again. In Peepal Mandi, his home in Agra, his son Ajodhya Prasad (Lalaji Maharaj) and then later descendants carried on a family line. The branch names Agam Prasad Mathur (Dadaji Maharaj), a great-great-grandson of Salig Ram and a former vice-chancellor of Agra University, as its guru since 1959. Another disciple, Brahm Shankar Misra, known as Maharaj Sahab, led from 1898 to 1907 and set up a Central Administrative Council in 1902 to manage the property and the branches. After his death, most of the branches, 111 of 116 by one count, accepted Kamta Prasad Sinha (Sarkar Sahab) of Ghazipur, whose line later moved to Agra and built the colony of Dayalbagh under Anand Swarup. Five branches did not accept him, and they formed the Soami Bagh satsang, which keeps the founder's samadh and calls itself the parent body of the faith.

**Beas.** Jaimal Singh, the soldier initiated in 1856, settled after retirement on open land outside Beas in the Punjab, where his followers count his leadership from 1889 and the settlement from 1891. His disciple Sawan Singh, an engineer in the Military Engineering Service, led from 1903 to 1948 and made the Dera at Beas a large centre. The Beas line then ran to Jagat Singh (1948–1951), Charan Singh (1951–1990) and Gurinder Singh Dhillon, head since 1990. In September 2024 the organisation named Jasdeep Singh Gill as its patron and as Dhillon's eventual successor, while Dhillon continues as spiritual head. Radha Soami Satsang Beas is now the largest of the branches; estimates put its initiates at about two million.

**Later lines.** Kirpal Singh, a disciple of Sawan Singh, founded Ruhani Satsang in Delhi after 1948 and has his own profile in this part. Dera Sacha Sauda was founded in 1948 by Mastana Balochistani, a follower of Sawan Singh. Other lines include Faqir Chand's Manavta Mandir at Hoshiarpur (1962) and the Radha Swami Satsang at Dinod in Haryana.

## The practice

The details of initiation are given privately in every branch, and the branches differ. Taken from published accounts, the general shape of the practice is this.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Take initiation from a living master.</strong> The master, or a representative, explains the practice and gives the names to be repeated. Without this, the movement holds, the practice has no force.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Repeat the names.</strong> Sitting still with eyes closed, the practitioner silently repeats the names given at initiation to gather the attention from the body and the world.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hold the attention behind the eyes.</strong> The attention is drawn to the point between and behind the eyebrows, which the teaching treats as the seat of the soul in the waking state.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Contemplate the master's form.</strong> Holding the image of the teacher steadies the attention and, in the teaching, keeps it under his protection.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Listen.</strong> In a separate period the practitioner listens for the inner sound and lets the attention follow it upward. Different branches give this practice different names and postures.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep the vows.</strong> A vegetarian diet, no alcohol or drugs and an honest life are treated as part of the practice.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

He died in 1878, and no recording of his voice survives. His voice reaches us through the verses of *Sar Bachan*, which followers still sing, and through the prose notes of his talks. The books are published separately by the Agra branches and by Beas, and the translations differ.

## Critiques and questions

**Who succeeded him?** The branches give incompatible answers, and each has a stake in its own. The Agra branches trace the faith through Salig Ram; Peepal Mandi, Soami Bagh and Dayalbagh then differ on who came next. Beas traces its authority through Jaimal Singh, who by the Beas account was initiated by the founder himself in 1856, and holds that the founder's grace passed through him. A long-running point of friction is the samadh at Soami Bagh. The Soami Bagh council allows followers and tourists to visit but does not allow religious processions there by anyone outside its own body. In 2010 the Dayalbagh Sabha held a conference, SPIRICON 2010, for groups who revere the founder, and the Soami Bagh council boycotted it. David Lane's *The Radhasoami Tradition* is the standard critical history of these disputes.

**Did he have a guru?** The Agra branches present him as a self-realised master. Writers who stress his family's long bond with Tulsi Sahib and his attendance at Girdhari Das's meetings see him as Tulsi Sahib's spiritual heir, whatever the formal position. The question matters because the movement's whole structure depends on a living master in an unbroken chain.

**The founder and the theology.** The idea that the founder was the incarnation of the supreme, and the name Radhasoami itself, came from Salig Ram. Scholars such as Juergensmeyer have pointed out that Shiv Dayal Singh's own writings say Sat Naam and Anami. Readers who want to know what he taught, as distinct from what the movement came to believe about him, need to read *Sar Bachan* with that in mind.

**Relations with Sikhism.** The teaching uses Sikh scripture and Punjabi Sant vocabulary heavily, and the Beas branch in particular grew among Sikhs. Its practices, including a living guru and no installed scripture, differ from orthodox Sikh custom, and this has caused friction with Sikh religious bodies over the years.

**Borrowing.** The inner-sound teaching has been taken up, with and without acknowledgement, by Western movements. Paul Twitchell, who founded Eckankar in the 1960s and had been a disciple of Kirpal Singh, wrote in a letter of 1963 that *Sar Bachan* "acts as practically my Bible!" Lane and others have documented how much Eckankar drew on Radhasoami sources.

## Descendants

The family of movements is large. Radha Soami Satsang Beas, with its headquarters at the Dera on the Beas river, is the largest and has centres across the world. Dayalbagh in Agra runs a university and a planned colony. Soami Bagh keeps the founder's shrine. Peepal Mandi keeps Salig Ram's family line. Kirpal Singh's Ruhani Satsang and the groups that came after it carried the practice to Europe and North America. Dera Sacha Sauda in Haryana grew into a large movement of its own. Each claims to keep what Shiv Dayal Singh began in Panni Gali in 1861.
