## Life

Kashinath Govind Upasni was born on 15 May 1870 in Satana, in Nashik district, into a conservative Brahmin family. His father was a court copyist. His grandfather, a Sanskrit scholar, raised him. He studied Ayurveda and Sanskrit grammar in Sangli, practised as an Ayurvedic physician with a dispensary at Amraoti, and edited a Marathi medical monthly. From 1906 a land venture at Gwalior failed and took his money and standing with it.

After a breathing illness and a search for help among holy men, he reached Shirdi in late June 1911. Sai Baba of Shirdi, whom many Hindus and Muslims revered, did not impress him at first. The scholar Antonio Rigopoulos notes that Upasni planned a short visit and was put off by a saint who seemed to him a Muslim. A teacher at Kopargaon sent him back. Sai Baba then told devotees that Upasni would need to stay about four years and that there was no difference between the two of them. He had Upasni sit in the empty Khandoba temple, and Upasni stayed there more than a year with little food, in what he later described as intense altered states. In 1913, at the Guru Purnima festival, Sai Baba told devotees to worship Upasni. Upasni disliked it and obeyed.

In 1915 he met Merwan Irani, the future Meher Baba, at Shirdi. Meher Baba says that over the following seven years Upasni gave him the knowledge that completed his own opening by Hazrat Babajan in 1913. According to Charles Purdom's biography of Meher Baba, *The God-Man*, Upasni addressed him as Avatar at the end of December 1921. Upasni's base for the rest of his life was Sakori, a village a few kilometres south of Shirdi. Sources date his settling there to 1917, 1918 or 1922, and a Meher Baba chronology already has the young Merwan visiting him at Sakori in 1916 or 1917. Sai Baba died in 1918.

In 1922 he shut himself in a bamboo cage for roughly fourteen to fifteen months. *Hinduism Today* reports his explanation that he had "undertaken this bondage to liberate my devotees." Later he started what became the Upasani Kanya Kumari Sthan, a community of unmarried women trained in Sanskrit and Vedic ritual. According to Bhau Kalchuri's *Lord Meher*, he met Meher Baba for the last time at Dahigaon on 17 October 1941. He died at Sakori on 24 December 1941.

## The teaching

The best-known summary is three rules, often given as a recipe for a life worth living. Trouble nobody in the least. Suffer for others and be useful to them. Remain contented in a state of "be as it may." The third is the hard one, since it asks for acceptance of events that cannot be controlled.

His own manner was unlike his rules. Devotees and visitors describe a teacher who used coarse language, threw stones and sometimes struck followers, and in the next hour showed affection. Admirers read this as method, a way of breaking pride, and one description calls him a living embodiment of the *Ashtavakra Gita*. He disclaimed miracles and sainthood. The writer Kevin Shepherd records him saying, "I tell everyone that I am not a saint. I am not God."

His metaphysics stayed inside Advaita. Self-realisation was the goal, and service and ethical conduct prepared for it. Where he broke with convention was on women. Although he was a traditionalist in dress, diet and ritual, he held that women should again perform all Vedic rites in Sanskrit. The kanyas at Sakori take lifelong vows of purity, celibacy and daily worship, and they conduct name-giving, thread, wedding and funeral rites. *Hinduism Today* counted forty-eight of them in 1992.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="upasni-maharaj" />

Upasni's apprenticeship to Shirdi Sai Baba is well documented, and the Khandoba temple year is the core of his own story. His link to Meher Baba is documented as a close association over about seven years, and the Avatar declaration is reported from Meher Baba's biographers. Godavari Mataji (1914 to 1990) arrived at Sakori in 1924 as a girl of ten and, according to *Hinduism Today*, was named his spiritual successor.

## Critiques and questions

The Kanya Kumari Sthan drew strong opposition from conservative Brahmins, which *Hinduism Today* calls "horrendous." Wikipedia, without a cited source for the point, says that after the Bombay Devadasi Protection Act of 1934 some girls offered to the ashram were married to Upasni in a spiritual marriage, described as union with the divine through the guru, and that this caused public outcry. Shepherd's biography lists three court cases against Upasni in 1934 and 1935, one of them relating to the Devadasi Act. An ashram publication cited by Wikipedia says the courts found him innocent in all of them. The court, the charges and the course of each case could not be confirmed from a primary or scholarly source.
