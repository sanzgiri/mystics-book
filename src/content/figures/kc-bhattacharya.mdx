## Life

Krishnachandra Bhattacharyya (the name is also spelled Bhattacharya) was born on 12 May 1875 at Serampore, a town on the Hooghly river north of Calcutta. He went to school there, matriculated in 1891 and studied at Presidency College, Calcutta. He joined the Bengal Education Department as a lecturer in philosophy in 1898 and taught in government colleges until he retired in 1930. From 1933 to 1935 he directed the Indian Institute of Philosophy at Amalner.

His first book, *Studies in Vedantism*, grew out of a doctoral dissertation of 1901 and is dated 1907 or 1909 in different sources. In 1921 the University of Calcutta passed him over for its new King George V chair of philosophy in favour of the younger Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan. He held that chair himself from 1935 to 1937. *The Subject as Freedom* appeared in 1930. In October 1931 he gave the Sir Asutosh Memorial Lecture at Chandernagore, "Svaraj in Ideas," which was not published until 1954, five years after his death, in the *Visva-Bharati Quarterly*. One recent study dates the lecture to 1928.

He also wrote on Kant, in a Bengali work later translated as *Implications of the Philosophy of Kant*, and on the Samkhya system, the Jaina doctrine of many-sidedness and the concept of the absolute. His essay "The Concept of Philosophy" was published in 1936. His son Kalidas Bhattacharyya (1911–1984) became a philosopher, and Gopinath Bhattacharyya edited his collected *Studies in Philosophy*. He died on 11 December 1949, although his publisher's recent edition gives 1947.

## The teaching

Bhattacharyya wrote dense, compressed English that even admirers find hard. His central questions were what the self is, how it can be known at all, and what philosophy is for.

**The subject as freedom.** Anything we can point to is an object: a chair, our body, even a feeling. The subject, the one who knows, is never one of these. Bhattacharyya argued that it can still be known, though not as a thing. We come to know it by progressively freeing ourselves from what we had taken to be ourselves. *The Subject as Freedom* traces this freeing through levels. At the bodily level, we identify with a body that we perceive only in part. At the psychic level, we discover ourselves as the one who has images, feelings and thoughts. At the spiritual level, the subject knows itself apart from any content. Each level is a further degree of freedom, and the self is this freeing activity itself. This is the closest he comes to the question of this part. The spirit's growth is the subject's own work, carried out in reflection.

**What philosophy is.** In "The Concept of Philosophy" he distinguished grades of thought, from thinking about empirical facts to thinking about the subject and finally about truth itself. Philosophy, he argued, deals with what cannot be stated as fact and can only be lived toward. He called it the "self-evident elaboration of the self-evident."

**Alternative absolutes.** In a late essay he proposed that the absolute appears in three forms, as truth for knowing, freedom for willing and value for feeling, and that none can be ranked above the others. The absolute is reached as one or another of these, alternately. This was his way of honouring both the Advaita stress on knowledge and the devotional stress on feeling without reducing one to the other.

**Svaraj in Ideas.** The 1931 lecture is now his most read work. Political subjection, he argued, is visible and can be resisted; cultural subjection is usually unconscious, and "implies slavery from the very start." Educated Indians had absorbed Western ideas without comparing them seriously with their own, so that traditional ideas survived as habit while new ones remained borrowed. Thought of this kind, he said, was hybrid and sterile. He did not reject Western philosophy, which he knew well. He asked Indian thinkers to weigh the two traditions on equal terms and to think creatively from their own inheritance, which he called svaraj, or self-rule, in ideas.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="kc-bhattacharya" />

Bhattacharyya had no guru and founded no movement. His tree shows intellectual influences, all documented in his own essays. Advaita Vedanta gave him the problem of the self, and he wrote on the Samkhya and Jaina systems as live philosophical options. His son Kalidas Bhattacharyya carried his method into the next generation, and the "Svaraj in Ideas" lecture has become a reference point for later Indian debates about decolonising thought.
