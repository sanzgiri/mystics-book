## Life

Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan was born on 5 September 1888 at Tiruttani, a temple town north-west of Madras, into a Telugu-speaking Brahmin family from Sarvepalli village in Nellore district. His father, Sarvepalli Veeraswami, was a minor revenue official. Almost all of his education took place in Christian mission schools: four years at a Lutheran mission school in Tirupati from 1896, then Voorhees College in Vellore, run by the American Arcot Mission, and from 1904 Madras Christian College. He married Sivakamu in 1904; they had five daughters and a son.

At Madras Christian College he studied European philosophy under A. G. Hogg, a Scottish Presbyterian missionary. His MA thesis, *The Ethics of the Vedanta and Its Metaphysical Presuppositions*, was published in 1908. It answered missionary critics who said that Vedanta, by calling the world illusion, left no ground for ethics. He later wrote that "the challenge of Christian critics impelled me to make a study of Hinduism." Like many educated Hindus of his generation, he also credited Vivekananda with rousing his pride in his own tradition.

He taught at Presidency College, Madras, from 1909, then at Anantapur and Rajahmundry, and in 1918 became professor of philosophy at the University of Mysore. In 1921 he took the King George V chair at Calcutta. Lectures at Manchester College, Oxford, in 1926 became *The Hindu View of Life*, and his Hibbert Lectures of 1929 became *An Idealist View of Life*. From 1936 to 1952 he was Spalding Professor of Eastern Religions and Ethics at Oxford, and a fellow of All Souls College. He was vice-chancellor of Andhra University from 1931 and of Banaras Hindu University from 1939 to 1948.

His later public career, as ambassador to Moscow, Vice-President from 1952 and President of India from 1962 to 1967, lies outside this book's subject. His birthday is kept as Teachers' Day in India. In 1975 he received the Templeton Prize for religion. He died in Madras on 17 April 1975.

## The teaching

Radhakrishnan's central claim is that religion is first of all an experience and only secondarily a set of beliefs. He called religion "a kind of life or experience." Doctrines, rituals and creeds are interpretations of that experience, much as scientific theories interpret observations. "The creeds of religion correspond to theories of science," he wrote in *An Idealist View of Life*.

**Intuition.** The faculty that grasps this experience he called intuition, or integral experience. Reason and sense perception give knowledge of parts; intuition gives a direct, whole and self-certifying awareness of reality. He found it in the poet, the scientist at the moment of discovery and the moral reformer, and above all in the mystics of every religion. Because it is a capacity of the human mind as such, it is open in principle to anyone, which made religion something closer to an empirical inquiry than a matter of authority.

**Maya reread.** He accepted Shankara's Advaita as the deepest statement of this experience, but softened its most criticised doctrine. *Maya* (the doctrine that the world as we see it is appearance) did not, in his reading, mean that the world is unreal. It meant that the world is dependent and that we err when we take it as ultimate. This gave room for history, ethics and social progress, which his missionary teachers had said Vedanta could not support.

**One religion, many religions.** From this followed his answer to the question of this part. If all religions express one experience, their differences are differences of interpretation. "The Vedanta is not a religion, but religion itself in its most universal and deepest significance," he wrote in *The Hindu View of Life*. He presented Hinduism as tolerant and experimental, willing to accept every sincere approach to God, and he spent decades writing for Western readers in this vein, setting Indian thought beside Plato, Plotinus and the Christian mystics.

The same openness had limits that critics have pointed out. The *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* notes that he ranked religions by how close they came to Advaita, so that tolerance meant placing every faith on a ladder whose top was Vedanta. The German Indologist Paul Hacker called this kind of position inclusivism, and Robert Rinehart has described Radhakrishnan's tolerance as a theological scheme for absorbing religious difference into Vedantic truth. Others have noted that the appeal to experience is circular, since the Upanishads validate the experience and the experience validates the Upanishads, and that his contrast of a spiritual East with a rational West repeated older European stereotypes. *The Hindu View of Life* also offered a qualified defence of caste as a social arrangement, which reformers in the same period rejected.

His *Indian Philosophy* drew a public accusation in 1929. Jadunath Sinha, a younger scholar, charged in *The Modern Review* that passages of the second volume (1927) followed his doctoral thesis on Indian theories of perception. Radhakrishnan denied it, saying he had lectured from the same material for years and that both men drew on the same classical sources and translations. Each sued the other; the suits were settled out of court and withdrawn in 1933, with the terms undisclosed.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="radhakrishnan" />

Radhakrishnan had no guru and took no initiation. His lines of descent are intellectual. Advaita Vedanta, read through Vivekananda, gave him his conclusions; Hogg and the missionary critics of Hinduism gave him his questions and his method, and the tree shows that edge as a reaction. He founded no order and left no disciples in the monastic sense, but his textbooks and translations of the Upanishads, the Gita and the Brahma Sutra shaped how Hinduism was taught in Indian and Western universities through the middle of the twentieth century.
