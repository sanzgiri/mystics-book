## Life

Paul Brunton was born Hyman Abraham Isaacs in East London on 21 October 1898, the only child of a master bootmaker, according to the *New Dawn* profile. Wikipedia reports that his mother died when he was thirteen and that his stepmother introduced Christian Science to the house. He served in a tank unit in the First World War, then worked as a bookseller and journalist, writing under pseudonyms. He was a partner in the Atlantis Bookshop in Bloomsbury and wrote for the *Occult Review*. A profile in *New Dawn* says he joined the Theosophical Society in 1920 and was mentored by Allan Bennett, a former member of the Golden Dawn who had become a Buddhist monk and returned to London as Ananda Metteya. The profile says Brunton worked for a time as his secretary, and encyclopedia.com says Bennett initiated him into Buddhist meditation. He married in 1921, had a son in 1923 and was divorced in 1926.

He sailed for India in 1930. The same profile says the first trip was aimed at meeting Meher Baba, about whom his book later gave a negative verdict. He also met the Paramacharya of Kanchipuram, in January 1931 at Chingleput, as the Paramacharya chapter records, and the Paramacharya sent him to Ramana Maharshi at Tiruvannamalai. His first visit to Sri Ramanasramam was in 1931. He asked how to reach God-realisation and was answered with the inquiry "Who am I?" *A Search in Secret India* appeared in 1934 under the name Paul Brunton, which he adopted for good, and it was translated into more than twenty languages.

A second stay in India ran from 1935 to 1937, according to the *New Dawn* profile. During the Second World War he was in India and was a guest of the Maharaja of Mysore. Further books followed quickly: *The Secret Path* (1935), *A Search in Secret Egypt* (1936), *The Quest of the Overself* (1937), *The Hidden Teaching Beyond Yoga* (1941) and *The Wisdom of the Overself* (1943). His last book published in his lifetime was *The Spiritual Crisis of Man* (1952). He settled in Switzerland (in 1956, according to encyclopedia.com) and spent his later years writing notes and essays, about 20,000 pages by Wikipedia's count, some 7,000 pages of notebooks by encyclopedia.com's, and over 30,000 notes by the *New Dawn* profile's. He died at Vevey on 27 July 1981. Larson Publications issued the notes as *The Notebooks of Paul Brunton* in sixteen volumes between 1984 and 1988. The Paul Brunton Philosophic Foundation, formed with the help of his son Kenneth Hurst, publishes his work, and his archive is now at Cornell University Library.

## Why this figure appears here

This part asks whether the Self can be known without method. Brunton is the person through whom a large English-reading public first heard Ramana's answer. Wikipedia credits him with introducing Ramana to the West through *A Search in Secret India* and *The Secret Path*. The Chingleput meeting recorded in the Paramacharya chapter is where the hand-off began, since the Paramacharya declined to take Brunton as a disciple and pointed him to Arunachala.

Brunton came to the meeting from occult societies, Theosophy and Buddhist meditation. From Ramana he took the inquiry into who is having the experience, which the *New Dawn* profile treats as the model practice of his system. He did not stay inside Ramana's frame. His books set out a graded path from religion through mysticism to philosophy, with the Sage as the goal. He worked with the idea of the Overself, which in his terms is identical at the highest level with the World Mind, and with Mentalism, the doctrine that matter has no independent existence and is a form taken by mental processes. The same profile reports that he rejected the role of guru and called himself a student, and that he left Theosophy behind and disagreed with the view that existence is essentially suffering. The advaita.org.uk summary adds that he held meditation and inner inquiry to be suitable for people in active Western lives, and not only for monks and hermits.

On the tree he is a branch from Ramana with a documented stay in 1931, and an edge from the Paramacharya for the referral. The Meher Baba edge records a reaction against him after the meeting of 1930, and Allan Bennett is recorded as an early influence on his Buddhist meditation. The chain that matters runs from Ramana to the English-speaking world, since many Western readers who reached Tiruvannamalai in the following decades were prepared by his books.
