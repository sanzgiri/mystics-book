<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> She was born Sudhamani, which her followers translate as "ambrosial jewel." By the organisation's account, her first resident disciples gave her the name Mata Amritanandamayi, "Mother of Immortal Bliss," in 1979. Nearly everyone calls her Amma, the Malayalam for mother, and she uses the name for herself, often speaking of "Amma" in the third person. This chapter calls her Amma, as most writing about her does.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Visitors to her programmes describe much the same evening, whether it is held in a hall in Kerala, a London exhibition centre or a convention centre in Manhattan. Each person takes a numbered token on arrival, and the numbers are called in batches, so a wait of several hours is common. Volunteers serve food. Amma, a small woman in a white sari, leads a guided meditation in Malayalam while lay monastics translate and screens show the words in other languages. Then the singing begins and the line starts to move.</p>
<p>Near the front, people go forward on their knees. Volunteers ask what language each person speaks, take away glasses and bags, and tell them not to put their weight on her. She pulls each person to her shoulder, holds them for a few seconds, sometimes half a minute, and murmurs into one ear. A journalist from the <i>New Humanist</i>, kneeling in the line at Alexandra Palace in 2008, could only make out a soft repeated syllable. Before letting go she presses something into the palm: a rose petal, a pinch of sacred ash, a sweet. At a New York programme in August 2024, the sweet was a Hershey's kiss folded inside the petal.</p>
<p>On the nights called Devi Bhava, the mood of the Goddess, she appears in the rich dress of a goddess, and the darshan can last through the night. The organisation says she has sat for more than twenty-two hours without a break, and that she has embraced over forty million people.</p>
</section>

## Life

Sudhamani Idamannel was born on 27 September 1953 in Parayakadavu, a fishing hamlet on the narrow strip of land at Alappad between the Arabian Sea and the backwaters, in what is now Kollam district of Kerala. She was the third of seven children of Sugunanandan, who sold fish, and his wife Damayanti. The movement's accounts say she was born smiling and that she was composing songs to Krishna by the age of five. These are devotional stories.

Her schooling ended at about nine, when her mother fell ill and the household work passed to her. The movement's accounts describe a hard childhood: long days of housework, and going from house to house collecting food scraps for the family's animals. On those rounds, the story goes, she met hunger, illness and old people left alone, and began giving away food and clothes from her own home and embracing people in grief. Her family scolded and punished her for it. She told the *Christian Science Monitor* in 2006 that she had been beaten at home, and that as a girl she spent hours in meditation. She refused the marriages her parents arranged.

In 1975, at about twenty-two, she began to give what her devotees call bhava darshan. The movement's version runs as follows. Walking past a neighbour's house where the *Bhagavata Purana* was being recited, she ran toward the singing, and her face and gestures took on those of Krishna. Accounts date this to March or to September 1975. She later described hearing an inner voice: <q>Thousands and thousands of people in the world are steeped in misery. I have much for you to do, you who are one with Me.</q> Villagers began to come to her for blessings on the nights of Krishna Bhava. A vision of the Goddess followed, by her account, and she added a Devi Bhava, the mood of the Divine Mother. The Devi Bhava is the form described in recent accounts of her programmes. A local rationalist group opposed the gatherings from the start as fraud, and the movement's histories describe harassment and threats in 1975 and 1976.

The first young men came to live with her in the late 1970s. Among them was a young man of twenty-two who met her in 1979 and became Swami Amritaswarupananda Puri when she gave him sannyasa in 1989; he is now the Math's vice-chairman and president of its university. An American, Neal Rosner, arrived in 1979 and became Swami Paramatmananda. A young Australian woman, Gail Tredwell, came at about the same time and served as her personal attendant for nearly twenty years. On 6 May 1981 the Mata Amritanandamayi Math and Mission Trust was registered at Kollam under the Travancore-Cochin societies registration law of 1955, with Amma as its chairperson. The first buildings were thatched huts beside her family's house. That site, now a large campus of tall residential blocks, is Amritapuri.

In May 1987 she flew to San Francisco for her first world tour, which she has repeated almost every year since. An ashram opened at San Ramon, California, in 1989 and became the American headquarters. In 1993 she spoke at the centenary Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago. She spoke at the United Nations in New York in 1995 at the UN's fiftieth anniversary celebrations, and on 29 August 2000 she addressed the Millennium World Peace Summit of Religious and Spiritual Leaders in the General Assembly hall, in Malayalam. On 7 October 2002, at the Palais des Nations in Geneva, Jane Goodall presented her with the Gandhi-King Award for Non-Violence, and she gave the keynote at a meeting of women religious leaders under the title *The Awakening of Universal Motherhood*. In 2014 she signed the joint declaration of religious leaders against modern slavery at the Vatican.

Alongside the tours, the Math built a network of institutions. The Amrita Institute of Medical Sciences opened in Kochi in 1998. Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, the university she founded and of which she is chancellor, grew to several campuses and was named an Institute of Eminence by the Government of India in 2019. The organisation groups its charitable work under the name Embracing the World and reports housing schemes, pensions for widows, women's self-help groups and relief after disasters from the 2004 tsunami onward. She also consecrated a series of temples of her own design, the Brahmasthanam temples, sixteen of them in India and abroad by 2003, each with a single image bearing four faces. In temples she founded she ordained women as priests as well as men, which drew objections from traditionalists.

In 2020 she stopped giving darshan in person because of the pandemic and met followers by video. In July 2024 she returned to the United States for her first tour since then, opening in Seattle on 4 July.

<aside class="note"><strong>Bhava.</strong> Here a bhava is a mood or state in which a person manifests a deity. Amma's Krishna Bhava and Devi Bhava were ritual occasions in which she wore the deity's dress and received devotees as that deity. The scholar Maya Warrier calls them ritualised states in which she deliberately puts on the garb of a goddess.</aside>

## The teaching

Amma has written no treatise. Her teaching comes through talks, answers to questions at programmes, the recorded conversations published as *Awaken, Children!*, and her public addresses. It is simple in its terms and repeated often.

**Love as the substance of God.** The centre of her teaching is that love and compassion are the nature of the divine and of the human self, and that the spiritual life consists of uncovering them. She told the *Christian Science Monitor* in 2006 that the motherhood she speaks of <q>is Divine Love – and that is God.</q> At Stanford in 2014 she called love the greatest power and said that <q>Compassion is the first step.</q> She describes herself in the same language. <q>A continuous stream of love flows from me to all of creation,</q> runs a statement her organisation publishes. The ideal she sets for others is what she calls universal motherhood: a maternal care for every living being, which she says men and women alike can develop. In Geneva in 2002 she said that <q>No authentic religion belittles or denigrates women</q> and that <q>Men and women have to support one another.</q>

**Ego as the obstacle.** The thing that stands between a person and this love is the ego, the sense of being a separate doer who must protect and enlarge itself. She told the *Monitor*: <q>We human beings are just instruments of God so we should not be egoistic.</q> The practices she recommends are means of wearing the ego down. Prayer, chanting, meditation and work done for others all serve, in her account, to purify the mind until its true nature shows.

**Service as worship.** She gives great weight to *seva*, work done for others without thought of reward, and treats it as a spiritual practice equal to meditation. This is the teaching behind the hospitals and housing schemes, and she often states it in practical terms. Her organisation quotes her: <q>The duty of a doctor is to treat patients. In the same way, my duty is to console those who are suffering.</q> Her disciples present her own service as the model and her institutions as its extension.

**One goal, many paths.** The philosophy underneath is Advaita Vedanta, the teaching that the individual self and the absolute are one. Her disciple Swami Ramakrishnananda presents her path as the path of the Vedas and the *Bhagavad Gita*. She treats action, knowledge and devotion as necessary together, and she speaks of liberation as something to be reached in this life. She regards every religion's practices as ways of purifying the mind toward one end. When she initiates someone with a personal mantra, the person may choose one addressed to a Hindu deity, to the Goddess, to Christ, to the Buddha, or to no named deity at all.

**The embrace.** Her darshan is the most visible part of the teaching and the part she explains least in abstract terms. In the movement's account it began in her teens, when people came to her with their troubles and she wiped their tears and held them. Her organisation describes the embrace as <q>a process of purification and inner healing</q> and says that in it Amma transmits <q>a part of Her pure, vital energy</q> to the person. She offers it to everyone, regardless of caste, religion or sex. For a young woman of a fishing family in 1970s Kerala, holding strangers of every caste in public, men among them, went against customary rules of ritual purity and decorum, and in the movement's own account her family punished her for it. Her movement has also admitted menstruating women to darshan, which traditionalists objected to.

**The Goddess.** Devotees regard her as a satguru, a fully realised teacher, and many regard her as a form of the Divine Mother herself. The Devi Bhava makes this belief visible: on those nights she sits in the dress of the Goddess and is worshipped with the offerings a temple image would receive. Amanda Lucia's study of American devotees found that whether they see her as the Goddess in person or as a model of women's spiritual power depends largely on whether they inherited Hindu practice or adopted it.

I find it hard to separate Amma's teaching from what she does with her arms. Read on the page, her talks repeat what many teachers in this book have said about love, ego and service, and her metaphysics is the Advaita that frames most modern Hindu teaching. What is new is the physical form. Darshan in a temple is looking at an image; darshan with most gurus is sitting at a distance. She closed that distance, held strangers one by one for decades, and asked people to treat that act as the core of the spiritual life. For someone of her background, in Kerala of the 1970s, that was a break with the rules of who could touch whom, and it is the part of her work I would call original.

## Lineage

Amma stands outside any formal guru line. She received no initiation from a teacher, and her authority rests on her own experience as the movement narrates it and on the devotion of those who came to her.

<MiniTree id="amma" />

The edges into her are broad influences. Her teaching on the self draws on **Advaita Vedanta**, and her disciples describe it in those terms; the edge is documented by the content of her talks and the writings of her monks. Her worship of the Goddess, the Devi Bhava and the daily *Lalita Sahasranama* at Amritapuri place her within **Shakta** devotion; this too is documented by practice. The outward edge to **Swami Amritaswarupananda Puri** is documented: she gave him sannyasa in 1989, and he is the senior monk of her order and the Math's vice-chairman. She has given sannyasa to other monks and nuns, who carry the name Puri.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Darshan.</strong> The central act is to go to her and be embraced. Darshan is free; a token sets the order of the line. At Amritapuri she generally gives darshan four days a week, and on tour every day of a programme.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Chanting.</strong> The day at Amritapuri begins at about five in the morning with the <i>Lalita Sahasranama</i>, the thousand names of the Goddess, chanted together as an <i>archana</i>, a recitation of divine names.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Meditation.</strong> Residents set aside the morning and early evening for meditation. The organisation also teaches the Integrated Amrita Meditation Technique, a programme of about thirty minutes a day combining stretches, breathing and concentration, taught free by trained volunteers and offered as open to people of any faith.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Seva.</strong> Most of the day goes to unpaid work for the ashram and its projects, from cooking and cleaning to disaster relief. All work at Amritapuri is done by volunteers.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Bhajans.</strong> Every evening she or her monks lead devotional singing. She has recorded more than a thousand songs in many languages.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Mantra.</strong> A devotee may ask her for a personal mantra and repeat it daily as <i>japa</i>, choosing the form of the divine it addresses.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Gail Tredwell and *Holy Hell*, 2013 and 2014.** Gail Tredwell, born in Queensland in 1958, was known in the ashram as Gayatri. She joined Amma at about the start of the 1980s (1980 or January 1981 in different accounts) and left at the end of 1999, having been her personal attendant for most of those years. In 2012 she posted her reasons for leaving in an online group of former followers. In October 2013 she published a memoir, *Holy Hell: A Memoir of Faith, Devotion, and Pure Madness*. In it she alleges that Amma hit, kicked and verbally abused her in private; that a senior male disciple she calls Balu, described by *India Today* as the Math's chief priest, repeatedly raped her in the mid-1980s; and that donations were diverted to Amma's relatives. The book became a public matter in Kerala in February 2014, after the journalist John Brittas interviewed her on Kairali TV. Tredwell told *India Today* that she stood behind everything in the book and did not intend to file a legal complaint, saying that a case would not necessarily bring justice. No criminal case has been filed on her allegations, and no court has examined them.

The ashram's spokesman, Sudeep Kumar, called the claims <q>a figment of her depressed mind</q> and said she had been taking medication for depression, according to *DNA*. He said then that the ashram would not take legal action against her, although the Math told *India Today* in February 2014 that it planned a defamation suit in New York; no such suit has been reported. Speaking at Palakkad on 22 February 2014, Amma rejected the allegations. <q>Nothing wrong has occurred in the ashramam as alleged by some people,</q> she said, according to *India Today*, and she described the Math as an open book whose accounts are submitted to the authorities every year. She attributed the charges to people whose wishes had not been met and to forces trying to stir up religious feeling. Supporters published detailed rebuttals pointing to what they called inconsistencies in Tredwell's account.

The Kerala government declined to act. Chief Minister Oommen Chandy said the organisation would not be investigated on the basis of allegations alone, and Home Minister Ramesh Chennithala said no action could be initiated on the strength of the book. The CPI(M) leader Pinarayi Vijayan called the charges serious and asked for an inquiry. Devotees then went to the police and the courts against critics. Police registered cases against Facebook users on a complaint by five devotees that they had shared defamatory material, and a few people were arrested. A magistrate in Kochi ordered an inquiry on a private complaint against Tredwell and some media outlets. A devotee's criminal defamation complaint against the executives of Reporter TV over a programme on the book ran for ten years. In 2024 Justice P.V. Kunhikrishnan of the Kerala High Court quashed it, holding that the media may discuss a book in the public domain and that <q>a defamation case cannot be against 'pick and choose persons' alone</q> when the author and publisher had not been prosecuted.

**Finances.** Tredwell's book alleges that money given for charity went to build houses for Amma's siblings and into her father's fishing business, and, as *The Pioneer* summarised it, that the ashram held large sums in Swiss bank accounts. Critics in Kerala have long questioned the organisation's finances. The Math says darshan, mantras and membership are free, that its charitable work is paid for from donations and the sale of devotional goods, and that it files its accounts every year. No court has made a finding of financial wrongdoing against Amma or the Math.

**The rationalist critique.** The sharpest early criticism came from Kerala's rationalist movement, which has opposed her since 1975. In 1985 Sreeni Pattathanam, a rationalist leader, published *Matha Amritanandamayi: Divya Kathakalum Yatharthyavum* ("Sacred Stories and Realities"), which disputes the miracles told of her and asks for police inquiries into deaths near the ashram. In 2002 the CPI(M) paper *Deshabhimani* published a report on such deaths and, according to later accounts, withdrew it with an apology the following month. In 2004, on the complaint of T.K. Ajan, a resident of the Math, and after a direction from the Kerala High Court, the state government sanctioned his prosecution, with his publisher and printer, under laws against insulting religious beliefs. Civil liberties groups, including the People's Union for Civil Liberties, protested. The outcome of that case has not been traced in this research.

**The death of Satnam Singh Mann, 2012.** On 1 August 2012 Satnam Singh Mann, a twenty-three-year-old law student from Bihar, ran toward the dais during darshan at Amritapuri. Ashram security staff restrained him and handed him to the police, and on a court's direction he was admitted to a government mental health centre, where he died three days later. His family alleged that he had also been beaten at the ashram. According to the *Deccan Herald*, he was beaten at the centre by attendants engaged to look after patients; the police filed a charge sheet, which his father called unsatisfactory. His father asked for a CBI investigation, and in April 2014 the Kerala High Court asked the police for a statement after the prosecution conceded omissions in the inquiry. The final outcome of the case, and any statement by the ashram, have not been found in this research.

**Devotion and discipline.** The scholars David Bromley and Stephanie Edelman summarise other criticism: that the movement is a personality cult, that the claims made for her miracles do not hold up, and that critics object to her directions on the separation of couples, celibacy and short hours of sleep. They also record the counter-claim of followers that opponents have tried to harm her.

## Descendants

Her work continues through the Math's monastic order, the senior monks and nuns she has initiated, who run its programmes, temples and institutions; Swami Amritaswarupananda Puri, its vice-chairman, is the most senior. Local groups in many countries, run mostly by volunteers, hold chanting and service projects between her visits. The wider legacy is the embrace itself. Darshan as a hug, offered to anyone who joins the line, has become what she is known for worldwide, and the phrase "the hugging saint" has carried her name far beyond Hindu circles.
