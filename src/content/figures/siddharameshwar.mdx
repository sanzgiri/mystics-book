## Life

Siddharameshwar Maharaj was born in 1888 at Pathri, near Solapur, in the Marathi-speaking Deccan. Little is recorded of his early life. In 1906, aged about eighteen, he was initiated by Bhausaheb Maharaj, the teacher who had founded the Inchegiri line in a village of northern Karnataka. Bhausaheb taught meditation on the divine name, repeated for hours a day over many years. His followers called this the *pipilika marg*, the ant's way: slow, steady, sure to arrive.

Siddharameshwar followed it faithfully while Bhausaheb was alive. After Bhausaheb died in 1914 he began to doubt that long meditation was necessary. Around 1920 he started teaching what he called the *vihangam marg*, the bird's way. A bird reaches the fruit at the top of the tree directly, where the ant has to climb the trunk. Several of his fellow disciples opposed him for departing from their guru's method, and he went on alone.

He taught in Marathi, mostly in Karnataka and Maharashtra, travelling to Bombay to give talks. His usual text was the *Dasbodh* of Samarth Ramdas, the seventeenth-century Marathi saint, along with the *Yoga Vasishtha* and Eknath's Marathi commentary on the *Bhagavata*. He explained these books line by line and used them to lead his listeners, step by step, from the body to the Self.

He died of an infection on 9 November 1936, at forty-eight. Nisargadatta Maharaj, who had been with him for only three years, collected and published his talks in Marathi in 1961–1962.

## The teaching

The bird's way rests on discrimination. Siddharameshwar asked his listeners to examine what they took themselves to be, layer by layer: the gross body, the subtle body of mind and thought, the causal body of deep sleep and ignorance, and the "great causal body" of pure knowing. Each layer turns out to be something observed, and so something other than the observer. What remains when all four are set aside is the Self, which he described as beyond even the knowledge "I am."

He did not reject the old practices. He kept the mantra of his line and gave it at initiation. He simply treated meditation as an aid to understanding, where Bhausaheb had treated it as the path itself. Understanding, for Siddharameshwar, was the decisive act, and it could happen quickly in a mature student.

His best-known instruction to Nisargadatta, to hold attention on the sense "I am," came from this approach. It points the student at the last thing that remains before the Self, the bare fact of being.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="siddharameshwar" />

The Inchegiri line runs from Nimbargi Maharaj (1789–1875) to Bhausaheb Maharaj (1843–1914) and then to Siddharameshwar. It calls itself a branch of the Navnath tradition, though the links before Nimbargi are hard to document. Siddharameshwar's disciples included **Nisargadatta Maharaj**, **Ranjit Maharaj** (1913–2000), who joined him as a boy of twelve, **Ganapatrao Maharaj** and **Muppin Kaadsiddheshwar**. The meditative branch of Bhausaheb's teaching continued separately through the philosopher **R.D. Ranade**.
