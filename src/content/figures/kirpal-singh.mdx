## Life

Kirpal Singh was born on 6 February 1894 in the village of Sayyad Kasran in the Punjab, in what is now Pakistan. He trained as an accountant and spent his working life in the Military Accounts Department, rising to a senior post. His movement's life sketch says that from about 1917 he saw a luminous form in meditation and went looking for the teacher who matched it. The English Wikipedia article says he took the form to be Guru Nanak. In 1924 he visited the Radhasoami colony at Beas, met Sawan Singh, and, by the account of his followers, recognised him as the figure he had seen. He was initiated the next day.

Over the next two decades he remained a householder disciple and later wrote a short life of his teacher. Sawan Singh died on 2 April 1948, and this is where the record divides.

Kirpal Singh's followers say that Sawan Singh told him on 12 October 1947 that he would carry on the work, and that a transfer of spiritual power took place the day before the master died. The English Wikipedia article on Radha Soami Satsang Beas puts the verbal commission in December 1947 and says the Beas records do not contain it. Beas says Sawan Singh named Jagat Singh, a retired chemistry professor, in a will dated 20 March 1948. Jagat Singh was installed on 13 April 1948, and Beas holds that he alone was authorised to give initiation. Kirpal Singh founded his own work in Delhi under the name Ruhani Satsang, "spiritual gathering," in place of the Radhasoami name. Sources give 1948 or 1951 for the founding, and his Sawan Ashram in Shakti Nagar, Delhi, was built in 1951. Jagat Singh died in October 1951 and was succeeded at Beas by Charan Singh. Beas and Ruhani Satsang have been separate bodies since.

From the 1950s his work became public in a way Beas never was. He was the first president of the World Fellowship of Religions, from 1957 to 1971. In 1955 he made the first of three Western tours, the others following in 1963 and 1972, and the Wikipedia article calls the first the earliest such visit by an Indian spiritual master. In September 1962 he received the Order of St John of Jerusalem in New Delhi. He died on 21 August 1974.

## The teaching

Kirpal Singh taught what Sawan Singh had taught, that the soul can leave the body consciously and travel inward along a current of sound and light. He used the Punjabi and Hindi word *Naam*, "Name," and the Sant Mat word *Shabd*, "Word," for this current, and he said it appears under different names in the scriptures of many religions. The path is called surat shabd yoga, the union of the attention with the Sound.

Three points mark his version. First, the living master is required. In a passage from *Man! Know Thyself*, as reproduced on his movement's site, he writes, "It is only through a living Master that we can contact the Almighty God within us." A dead saint's writings can inspire, he said, but initiation has to come from someone who is alive and has made the inner journey. Second, the ethical base is part of the path. Wikipedia reports that he asked initiates to follow a lacto-vegetarian diet, and his collected writings include chapters on the vegetarian diet and on keeping a spiritual diary. Third, the teaching was presented as a science of the soul open to any religion. His World Fellowship of Religions and the later Human Unity Conferences carried that claim to a public audience.

The practice itself has three parts: silent repetition of charged names (*simran*), concentration on the inner light (*dhyan*), and listening to the inner sound (*bhajan*). Sawan Singh had asked for a minimum of about four hours a day. English Wikipedia says Kirpal Singh asked for about six.

## Lineage

On the tree Kirpal Singh sits one step below Sawan Singh, the same position as Charan Singh, who led Beas. The line through Jaimal Singh goes back to Shiv Dayal Singh, the founder of the Radhasoami faith.

<MiniTree id="kirpal-singh" />

The initiation by Sawan Singh in 1924 is documented from both sides. The claim to have been named his successor is Kirpal Singh's own and his movement's, and the Beas will points to Jagat Singh. After 1974 the movement divided. Darshan Singh, a civil servant who had served Kirpal Singh from 1948, is the most widely recognised successor and led what became the Sawan Kirpal Ruhani Mission. Ajaib Singh, who taught in Rajasthan and the United States, was backed by supporters at Sant Bani Ashram. Thakar Singh founded the Kirpal Light Satsang. Others did not recognise any successor, and a group in Italy followed Pier Franco Marcenaro. The movement at one time reported about 200,000 followers, a figure that Wikipedia attributes to a report from 2002 and that cannot be checked independently.
