## Life

Chinmoy Kumar Ghose was born on 27 August 1931 in Shakpura, a village in the Chittagong district of East Bengal, the youngest of seven children. His father died of illness in 1943 and his mother a few months later. In 1944, aged twelve, he joined older brothers and sisters who were already living at the Sri Aurobindo Ashram in Pondicherry, the community that had gathered around Sri Aurobindo and the Mother.

He stayed about twenty years. He meditated, read Bengali and English literature, wrote poetry, worked in the ashram's cottage industries, and took part in its athletics programme. By his own account he also served for about eight years as personal secretary to Nolini Kanta Gupta, the ashram's general secretary.

He said later that an inner message told him to go west. He landed at John F. Kennedy Airport on 13 April 1964, met by two American sponsors, Sam Spanier and Eric Hughes. He had little formal schooling, and in June he took a job as a junior clerk at the Indian Consulate in Manhattan, which he kept until 13 June 1967. In the evenings he gave talks on Hindu spirituality. The first two centres for his students were registered in 1966, in Puerto Rico in July and in New York in August, and from 1970 he led twice-weekly peace meditations for diplomats and staff at the United Nations.

From then on the range of his activity grew wide. He began painting in 1974 and called the work *Jharna-Kala*, Bengali for "fountain-art." In 1991 he began drawing birds, which he called Soul-Birds, by the thousand. He composed short devotional songs in Bengali and English and gave free peace concerts from 1984, at venues that included the Royal Albert Hall and Carnegie Hall. He ran marathons and ultramarathons, and when a knee injury stopped his running in 1985, at fifty-four, he took up weightlifting. In April 1987 he started the Peace Run, a torch relay for peace that is usually held every two years. His students organise races in Queens that include the Self-Transcendence 3100 Mile Race, which *The New York Times* called the Mount Everest of ultramarathons. Musicians including Carlos Santana were his disciples for a time.

The Sri Chinmoy Centre gives the totals: 120,000 published poems, about 13,000 songs in Bengali and 7,000 in English, over 140,000 artworks and close to 1,600 published works. He died of a heart attack at his home in Jamaica, Queens, on 11 October 2007, aged seventy-six. A spokesperson said then that there would be no successor, and a committee of senior students took over the running of the Centres.

## The teaching

Sri Chinmoy taught a path of love, devotion and surrender to God, with the guru as guide. Its method is meditation on the spiritual heart, which he said brings the light of the soul forward and quiets the restless mind. He told beginners to meditate early, before seven in the morning. Students were asked to be vegetarian, avoid alcohol and drugs, and practise celibacy whether married or single. He did not ask them to leave the world. The Centre describes the aim as <q>a harmonious lifestyle that blends the inner discipline of prayer and meditation with the dynamism of modern life</q>, and says he took no fee because <q>meditation is everyone's birthright</q>.

His most distinctive idea is self-transcendence. Every achievement, physical or inner, is a step to be gone beyond, and the only rival is one's own previous self. Asked about competition, he answered that <q>we compete with ourselves, with our own achievements, not with others</q>. That is why the races are so long, the poems so many and the weights so heavy. He described his own progress in lifting as growth from small beginnings, like a seed becoming a banyan, and credited it to God's grace.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="sri-chinmoy" />

Sri Chinmoy grew up in the Sri Aurobindo Ashram while the Mother ran it, and that is his documented edge. The sources consulted record no formal initiation by Aurobindo and no claim to succeed him. His service to Nolini Kanta Gupta rests on his own account. His work continues through the Sri Chinmoy Centres, which have no successor guru.

## Critiques and questions

**Control and departure.** Former students have described the community as a cult, pointing to rules on celibacy, dating and friendships outside the group. Jayanti Tamm, who was born into the community, gives this account in her memoir *Cartwheels in a Sari* (2009); she was sent away at twenty-five and notes that others may have experienced it differently.

**Allegations of sexual misconduct.** From 2004 several former female students alleged in the press that Sri Chinmoy had summoned them for sex, reports carried by the *New York Post*, *San Diego CityBeat* and *Salon*. In 2016 a former student, Anne Carlton, repeated her account on PIX11 in New York. He was never charged with a crime or sued. His lawyer denied the allegations at the time, and in 2016 the Sri Chinmoy Centre said that suggestions of improper conduct were <q>categorically false and completely baseless</q> and that some individuals were engaged in defaming him.

**The lifting claims.** Terry Todd, a former powerlifter and kinesiology professor at the University of Texas, examined the weightlifting feats and concluded that the type and weight of some lifts had been misrepresented.
