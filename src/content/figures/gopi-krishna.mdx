## Life

Gopi Krishna was born in 1903, on 30 May according to his biographers, in a village about twenty miles from Srinagar in the Kashmir Valley, into a Kashmiri Brahmin family. The family moved to Lahore in 1914, and he finished school there. At about seventeen he failed a college examination. By his own account the failure "created a revolution in my young mind," and he resolved to master his mind through meditation while living an ordinary family life. He returned to Kashmir, took a post in the state's Public Works Department, married and raised a family. Every morning for some seventeen years he sat in meditation before work.

On a morning in the Christmas season of 1937, when he was thirty-four, he was meditating in a house on the outskirts of Jammu, where the state government moved in winter. He had been concentrating, as usual, on an imagined lotus at the crown of his head. He felt a sensation at the base of his spine, and then, in his words, "with a roar like that of a waterfall, I felt a stream of liquid light entering my brain through the spinal cord."

What followed was not bliss. For weeks and then years he suffered sleeplessness, loss of appetite, burning heat, fear and swings of mood, and at times thought he was going mad. By his own account, he concluded that the energy had risen through the *pingala*, the channel on the right side of the subtle body associated with heat, instead of the central channel, and that he recovered his balance by concentrating on the *ida*, the cooling channel on the left. He describes a process of about twelve years before his condition settled into a lasting, luminous state of awareness.

He took early retirement from government service. In 1946 he founded a social organisation in Kashmir that campaigned against the dowry system and for the remarriage of widows. In the 1960s he wrote down his experience. *Kundalini: The Evolutionary Energy in Man* was published in New Delhi in 1967 and in London in 1970, with an introduction by Frederic Spiegelberg of Stanford and a psychological commentary by James Hillman. It was later reissued as *Living with Kundalini*. Sixteen more books followed, among them *The Biological Basis of Religion and Genius*, with an introduction by the physicist Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker, and a translation with commentary of the *Panchastavi*, a set of Kashmiri hymns to the goddess (1976 or 1978 in different listings). He died on 31 July 1984.

## The teaching

Gopi Krishna's account is unusual in the literature of yoga for its candour about suffering and its bodily detail. He had no guru and belonged to no lineage, and he insisted that his conclusions came from what happened to him.

**Kundalini as biology.** Classical tantra describes *kundalini* as a coiled power asleep at the base of the spine, which rises through channels in the subtle body to the crown. Gopi Krishna took this map and gave it a physical reading. Kundalini, he argued, is a real biological mechanism, linked to the reproductive system and the nervous system, which can redirect a refined vital energy (he called it *prana*) to the brain. When it is active, the brain itself is reorganised.

**Evolution of consciousness.** From this he built a theory of human evolution. The human brain, he believed, is still evolving, and kundalini is the mechanism through which it does so. Mystics, prophets and men of genius are individuals in whom the process has run ahead of the species; their visions and inspirations are a foretaste of a consciousness that humanity as a whole will reach. Religion, on this account, has a biological basis, and its insights are evidence about where evolution is going. He warned that if humanity ignored this process, or forced it through unbalanced practice, the results could be mental illness in individuals and disorder in societies.

**A call for science.** Because he held that the mechanism was physical, he asked scientists to test it. He promoted international research projects to look for measurable signs of kundalini activity, and he presented his own experience in universal, scientific terms. Marleen Thaler, writing for Harvard's Center for the Study of World Religions, describes his work as an example of the "therapeutization and psychologization of twentieth-century religion."

His claims remain claims. The physiological mechanism he proposed has not been demonstrated, and readers have disagreed about whether his twelve-year ordeal was a spiritual transformation, a psychological crisis or both. Hillman's commentary in the 1970 edition already read the account in psychological terms. Later clinicians, such as the psychiatrist Lee Sannella, drew on it when describing difficult kundalini episodes in their patients.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="gopi-krishna" />

Gopi Krishna had no teacher, and his tree is thin by design. The tantric kundalini tradition gave him his map, and Kashmiri Shaiva and Shakta texts gave him a local vocabulary he later studied and translated. These edges rest on his own testimony and writings. His influence ran mainly westward, through his English books, the Kundalini Research Foundation in New York, which published several of them, and the later literature on spiritual emergencies.
