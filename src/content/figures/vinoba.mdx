## Life

Vinayak Bhave was born on 11 September 1895 in Gagode, in the Kolaba district of Maharashtra, into a Brahmin family. His mother, Rukmini Devi, was devout, and her influence on him was strong. Wikipedia lists him as the eldest of five children and says his father, Narahar Shambhu Rao, was a trained weaver of rationalist outlook, while his grandfather Shamburao Bhave had a large share in raising him. By that account he read the *Gita* very young and took a vow of celibacy in adolescence. His younger brother Balkoba also chose renunciation and service. Vinoba read the Maharashtra saints and had a taste for mathematics, and the Gandhi Book Centre's life sketch says the two years he spent in college were marked by "internal uneasiness and agitation." In early 1916, on the way to examinations in Bombay, he is said to have burned his school and college certificates. He went to Kashi to study the Sanskrit texts, in search of Brahma, as the life sketch puts it, and on 7 June 1916 he met Gandhi at Kochrab Ashram. Vinoba later said, "Providence took me to Gandhi." He joined the ashram soon after. On 8 April 1921 he went to Wardha to take charge of the ashram there, and in 1923 he began a Marathi monthly, *Maharashtra Dharma*.

Vinoba spent years in Gandhi's ashrams, studying, spinning and keeping silence. He served several prison terms, including six months at Dhulia in 1932. There he gave eighteen Sunday talks on the Gita's eighteen chapters to fellow prisoners. Sane Guruji took them down by hand, and they appeared as a book, *Talks on the Gita*, in 1940. The publisher says the book has since been translated into 24 languages and sold about 2.5 million copies. Vinoba later divided the talks into 108 sub-sections and 432 paragraphs and wrote 540 Sanskrit aphorisms, collected as *Samyasutra-vritti*, to help readers reflect on them. He also read proofs of *Gitai*, his Marathi verse rendering, there. He wrote *Ishavasyavritti* and *Sthitaprajna Darshan* in jail. In 1938, after illness, he moved to Paramdham Ashram at Paunar near Wardha, which remained his base. In 1940 Gandhi chose him as the first individual satyagrahi of the campaign against Indian participation in the war. In 1959 he founded the Brahma Vidya Mandir there, a community of women living a contemplative life of work and service. Wikipedia says its members recited the Isha Upanishad at dawn, the Vishnu Sahasranama in mid-morning and the *Gita* in the evening.

After Gandhi's death in 1948 he became the leading figure of the Sarvodaya movement, the "welfare of all." The Sarvodaya idea took shape at a meeting of Gandhi's followers at Sevagram in March 1948. On 18 April 1951, in Pochampally in Telangana, villagers said they needed eighty acres of land. A landlord, Ram Chandra Reddy, offered a hundred, and Vinoba began to walk from village to village asking for land for the landless. He left Paunar on 12 September 1951 and returned on 10 April 1964, a walk of thirteen years. The life sketch gives his target as five crore, or fifty million, acres, and Wikipedia says landowners gave him one-sixth of their land. In May 1952 he received a whole village, Mangrath in Uttar Pradesh, as a gift, the first *gramdan*. He later received more than a thousand villages. Walking through Bihar from September 1952 to December 1954, he received about 23 lakh acres. On 7 June 1966 he said he wished to move from outward action to inward spiritual work. He returned to Paunar in November 1969, announced in October 1970 that he would stay in one place, and kept silence for the year from 25 December 1974. He died at Paunar on 15 November 1982, after refusing food and medicine for a few days; press accounts describe this as a death he chose. He received the first Magsaysay Award in 1958 for community leadership and the Bharat Ratna after his death, in 1983.

## The teaching

Vinoba's religious writing centres on the *Bhagavad Gita*. He called his Marathi Gita *Gitai*, "Mother Gita," and by Wikipedia's report he said the Gita is his life's breath. In the preface to *Talks on the Gita* he wrote, "The place of the Gita in my life is unique," and called the book "the story of my life, and it is also my message." He read the Gita as a teaching for people who must act. The publisher's note gives his firm conviction that "the Gita is meant to spiritualize human life." In the first talk he argues that Arjuna's despair was not a turn to non-violence but "attachment to his kith and kin," which hid his own duty, and that "the purpose of the Gita is to remove the delusion that stands between us and our swadharma." His slogan *Jai Jagat*, "victory to the world," gave the same idea a social form. The ideal figure in his reading is the *sthitaprajna*, the person of steady wisdom whom the second chapter describes, and he wrote a book on that figure.

In the 1950s he carried this into the land campaign. In Satish Kumar's account of Vinoba's view, land "belongs to all or none," and the gift was to come from a change of heart, not from confrontation. From 1954 he asked whole villages to give their land together. The publisher of *Talks on the Gita* says Vinoba saw the book itself as a vehicle for spreading *Bhoodan*. The village gift, or *gramdan*, extended the idea from a field to a community.

## Critiques and questions

**Results.** Gifts and distribution diverged. The Gandhi Book Centre's life sketch gives about 4.19 million acres obtained, of which about 1.29 million had been distributed by 1975 and about 1.86 million had been found unfit for distribution.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Read the Gita one chapter at a time.</strong> The Dhulia talks were given once a week, one for each of the eighteen chapters.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Recite on a daily rhythm.</strong> At Brahma Vidya Mandir the Isha Upanishad, the Vishnu Sahasranama and the Gita were each recited at a fixed time of day.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Ask for a gift.</strong> The land campaign was a request, made on foot and in person, and the donor decided how much to give.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Extend the gift to a village.</strong> The gift of a whole village, as at Mangrath in 1952, carried the same act to a community.</div></li>
</ol>
