## Life

Satyananda was born on 25 December 1923 at Almora, in the hills of what is now Uttarakhand. Wikipedia describes the family as farmers of the kshatriya caste. His name at birth is not given in the sources consulted for this entry. Wikipedia says he was classically educated and studied Sanskrit, the Vedas and the Upanishads. By his own account, reported there, from about the age of six his awareness would leave his body, and as a young man he met saints, among them Anandamayi Ma and a tantric bhairavi, Sukhman Giri, who gave him *shaktipat*, a transmission of spiritual energy, and urged him to find a guru.

In 1943 he left home in search of a guru, at eighteen by Wikipedia's account, nineteen by Hinduism Today's and twenty by the Bihar School's, and went to Swami Sivananda's ashram at Rishikesh. He stayed for more than twelve years, serving in several departments, editing the ashram's Hindi journal and writing articles and poems in Hindi and Sanskrit. Sivananda initiated him into sannyasa, the vow of the renunciant monk, on 12 September 1947, on the bank of the Ganges, and named him Satyananda Saraswati. *Saraswati* is the name of one of the ten orders of monks traced to the philosopher Shankara.

In 1956 Sivananda sent him out to spread the teaching. The Bihar School of Yoga's biography says Sivananda taught him kriya yoga and charged him to carry yoga "from door to door and shore to shore." He travelled as a wandering monk, for some eight years by Hinduism Today's account, through India, Afghanistan, Nepal, Burma and Ceylon, and settled on Munger, a town on the Ganges in Bihar. The Bihar School's own history says he founded the International Yoga Fellowship Movement in 1956 and the Bihar School of Yoga at Munger in 1963. Hinduism Today gives 1963 for the Fellowship and 1964 for the school, and Wikipedia gives 1962, at Rajnandgaon, for the Fellowship and 1964 for the school.

The school worked as a publishing house as well as an ashram. Its manual *Asana Pranayama Mudra Bandha* appeared in 1969 and became one of the most widely used handbooks of hatha yoga in English. *Yoga Nidra* followed in 1976. The Munger books presented each technique with numbered steps, cautions and the benefits claimed for it, and teachers trained there carried the system to Europe and Australia.

In 1988 he handed the running of the school to his successor, Swami Niranjanananda Saraswati, and left Munger. In September 1989 he moved to Rikhia, near Deoghar in what is now Jharkhand, arriving on 23 September by the school's account, and founded the ashram called Rikhiapeeth, where he lived in seclusion as a *paramahamsa*, a monk of the highest order. There he took up Vedic austerities, among them *panchagni*, in which the practitioner sits outdoors among four fires beneath the sun. He said he received a mandate in 1991 to serve the tribal villages around Rikhia, and the ashram's Sivananda Math now gives free medical care to the area. In 1995 he began a twelve-year Rajasooya Yajna, a Vedic rite, during which, Wikipedia says, he passed his spiritual and sannyasa responsibilities to Niranjanananda. He died on 5 December 2009. His followers describe his death as *mahasamadhi*, a yogi's conscious leaving of the body. Hinduism Today reported that a sixteen-day puja, the *ashodashi*, would be observed for him at Rikhiapeeth, ending on 22 December 2009.

## The teaching

Satyananda taught yoga as a graded training of body, breath and mind that anyone could take up, householder or monk. From Sivananda he kept the idea that hatha, raja, karma, bhakti and jnana yoga belong together. The Satyananda system, as Wikipedia lays it out, groups hatha, raja and kriya as the external yogas of body, mind and senses and karma, bhakti and jnana as the internal yogas of attitude. At Rikhia he gave Sivananda's three instructions, serve, love and give, as the basis of the ashram's work, and in 1994 he predicted that devotion would become the key practice of the twenty-first century. To this he added a large body of technique he drew from tantra: mudras, bandhas (internal locks of the throat, abdomen and pelvic floor), kriyas and the awakening of kundalini, the energy that the tantric texts place at the base of the spine.

His best-known contribution is yoga nidra, "yogic sleep." The student lies on the back and is guided by a spoken script. The practice opens with a *sankalpa*, a short personal resolve, and then leads attention around the parts of the body in a fixed order, then to the breath, to paired sensations such as heavy and light, and to a series of images, before the resolve is repeated. The aim is to stay aware at the edge of sleep. In the introduction to *Yoga Nidra* he calls the method a systematic rotation of consciousness in the body that came from tantric *nyasa*, in which mantras are placed or felt at different parts of the body. He says he first called it "yogic sleep" and later thought the name yoga nidra enough, and he traces its beginning to an episode with boys chanting Sanskrit, which he calls the birth of yoga nidra.

Two of his instructions carry the practice. The *sankalpa* is made when the mind is calm and quiet, once at the beginning and once at the end. The rotation of consciousness follows a fixed order that, he writes, should not be altered once established. The guiding instruction, in his words, is "Do not sleep", and he adds that in yoga nidra "it is not necessary to concentrate."

Yoga nidra is now taught in clinics and studios far from his movement, and several research groups have studied its use for stress and sleep. His books make large health claims for many practices, and these are the school's own. One of them is that a single hour of yoga nidra rests the body as much as four hours of ordinary sleep.

## Lineage

Satyananda is one of the main branches of the Sivananda line, alongside Swami Vishnudevananda in the West and Chinmayananda in Vedanta teaching.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Lie on the back.</strong> The student lies still and follows a spoken script, as in the guided practice described above.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Make the resolve.</strong> A short sankalpa is stated mentally, in a calm mind, with feeling.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Rotate the awareness.</strong> Attention passes through the parts of the body in a fixed order, which Satyananda wrote should not be altered once it is established.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Follow the breath.</strong> Attention rests on the breath, one of the practices the book uses to bring on withdrawal of the senses.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Feel the opposites.</strong> Heaviness comes first, so that the body seems to sink into the floor, and lightness follows.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Let images arise.</strong> Visualisation is meant to be effortless, with the image arising within and not forced.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Repeat the resolve.</strong> A second sankalpa closes the session, as the first opened it.</div></li>
</ol>
