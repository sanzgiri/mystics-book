## Life

Lekhraj Khubchand Kripalani was born in Hyderabad, in Sindh, into the Bhaiband community of Hindu traders. The Brahma Kumaris give his birth year as 1876; a timeline compiled for the World Religions and Spirituality Project gives 1884, which fits better with its statement that he died at eighty-five. He made his money in jewellery, and the movement calls him a diamond merchant. He was raised a Vaishnava Hindu.

In his fifties he left the business. The movement's histories say that in 1936 he had a series of visions that turned his life around; other accounts date the first visions to 1931 in Benares and the founding of his circle to 1932. From about 1934 he held a *satsang* at his house in Hyderabad, reading the Gita and leading the chanting of *Om*, and outsiders started calling the group Om Mandali, the circle that chants Om. Women and girls were prominent among them. A relative later reported that the Supreme Soul, Shiva, entered his body and spoke through him.

In October 1937 he formed a trust to run the community. The Brahma Kumaris say it was a committee of eight young women headed by Om Radhe, who became the community's first administrative head and whom members call Mamma. He made his property over to it. The response in Hyderabad was hostile. Wives took vows of celibacy and girls refused the marriages arranged for them, and many Bhaiband families saw a threat to their households. An Anti-Om Mandali Committee formed in 1938 and picketed the group. In August 1938 Lekhraj was served notice to show why he should not be bound over to keep the peace, and a complaint of kidnapping against him was inquired into without any action, according to a history of the dispute in *The Friday Times*. In 1939 a Karachi court ordered two girls returned to their mothers.

Opponents demanded a ban under the Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1908. Some later studies, following Reginald Coupland's 1944 account, say the Sind government declared the group an unlawful association; one timeline records the premier telling the assembly in March 1939 that there was no legal basis for a ban. By then the community had moved to Karachi, where it stayed until 1950.

After Partition the community left Pakistan. In 1950 it settled at Mount Abu, a hill station in Rajasthan, which is still its headquarters. Om Radhe died on 24 June 1965. Lekhraj died at Mount Abu on 18 January 1969. His followers do not speak of a death; they say he became *avyakt*, unmanifest, and continues to guide them.

## The teaching

The Brahma Kumaris teach that God is the Supreme Soul, Shiva, whom they call Shiv Baba. He has no body. He is described as a point of radiant light, beyond birth and death. Every human being is also a soul of the same kind, a tiny point of light seated in the body, and the root of suffering is forgetting this. The movement's own explanation is plain: <q>Soul consciousness is the awareness that we are souls</q>. Its opposite, body-consciousness, is identification with the body and what it owns, and the movement traces lust, anger, greed and ego to it.

Members believe that from the 1930s Shiva spoke through Lekhraj's body, and that Lekhraj, renamed Brahma, was the medium for a new revelation. His talks were written down as *murlis*, from the word for Krishna's flute. The movement describes God's <q>daily teachings (Murli), spoken through Brahma</q>, and the same talks are read aloud each morning in its centres around the world. After 1969, members say, messages from the subtle form of Brahma Baba came through Dadi Hriday Mohini, known as Dadi Gulzar, and these are read as *avyakt murlis*.

The practice the movement calls raja yoga borrows the old name and changes the content. It uses no posture, breath control or mantra. The meditator pictures himself or herself as a point of light and turns the mind to Shiva, the Supreme Soul, as the same kind of light.

Time, in this teaching, runs in a cycle of exactly five thousand years, divided into Golden, Silver, Copper and Iron Ages, and repeats identically, so that each soul plays the same part in every cycle. The present is the Confluence Age, the short meeting point between the end of the Iron Age and a new Golden Age, when God comes to teach. A life of celibacy, a vegetarian diet without onions or garlic, early rising and white clothing are presented as the way to become fit for that age.

The last distinctive feature is institutional and was there from the start. Lekhraj put the movement in the hands of young women, and women have run it ever since. Om Radhe was followed by Dadi Prakashmani, who had joined as a girl of about fourteen in 1936 and shared administration with Didi Manmohini after 1969; she led until her death on 25 August 2007. Dadi Janki followed until 2020, then Dadi Hriday Mohini until March 2021, then Dadi Ratan Mohini until her death on 8 April 2025 at 101. The movement names BK Mohini Didi as its present administrative head. It describes itself as <q>A worldwide spiritual movement led by women</q> and says it has more than 5,750 branches in over 130 countries.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="dada-lekhraj" />

Lekhraj had no human guru, and the tree marks the movement's central claim, that Shiva spoke through him, as a devotional edge. The documented edges are his reading of the Gita and the line of women who have led the movement since 1937. In the late 1970s a breakaway group rejected Dadi Gulzar as the medium and named another man as the new vehicle; it calls itself the Adhyatmik Ishwariya Vishwa Vidyalaya, and the main movement does not recognise it.

## Critiques and questions

**The 1930s opposition.** Bhaiband opponents accused Lekhraj of sorcery and of breaking up homes; the legal actions are described above. None of the sources consulted records a conviction, and Om Radhe published a defence titled *Is This Justice?*

**Dates for the end.** Early movement documents named 1976 as the year of the destruction that would end the Iron Age, according to the World Religions and Spirituality Project timeline. The movement's current public materials present its work as personal transformation and world renewal through meditation and do not give a date.

**Former members and families.** INFORM, the London research centre on new religions, records complaints from relatives and former members: strain on marriages from celibacy, tension over members eating only food cooked by Brahma Kumaris, and a few disputes over wills that left property to the movement. The Brahma Kumaris told INFORM that their visits to bereaved families are only to comfort them and that child-protection policies have been in place since 2001. INFORM reports no allegations of widespread or systematic child abuse.
