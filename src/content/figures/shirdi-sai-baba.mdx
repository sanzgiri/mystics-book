<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> <i>Sai</i> is a Persian-derived word used for a Muslim holy man or mendicant; <i>Baba</i> means father. He seems never to have used any other name, and nobody recorded the one he was born with.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>The story is told in the fifth chapter of the <i>Shri Sai Satcharita</i>, the Marathi verse life written by one of his devotees. A well-to-do Muslim named Chand Patil, from the village of Dhupkheda near Aurangabad, has lost his mare and spent two months looking for it. On his way home he passes a young fakir in a long robe sitting under a mango tree. The fakir calls him over to share a smoke, and when Chand Patil looks for fire for the clay pipe, the fakir pushes his tongs into the ground and draws out a burning coal. Then he tells him where to find the mare, and it is there.</p>
<p>Some time later Chand Patil takes a wedding party to Shirdi, a village in Ahmednagar district, and the fakir goes with it. The carts stop in a field near the temple of Khandoba, a local form of Shiva. The temple priest, Mhalsapati, a goldsmith by caste, sees the young man step down and greets him with the words <q>Ya Sai</q>, "Welcome, Sai." The name stuck. When the wedding party went home, the fakir stayed. He lived in Shirdi for the rest of his life, about sixty years.</p>
</section>

## Life

Almost nothing about Sai Baba's life before Shirdi can be checked. The *Satcharita* says it plainly: "Nobody knew the parents, birth or birth-place of Sai Baba." He did not help. When devotees asked, he gave answers that were, in the words of one modern account, vague and contradictory. To Mhalsapati he is reported to have said that he was born to Brahmin parents at Pathri, a town in the Nizam's territory to the east, and handed over as a child to a fakir. On another occasion he spoke of being left with a Hindu guru, Venkusa of Selu, and of staying with him for twelve years. Both accounts come to us through devotees, written down after his death.

The *Satcharita* says he first appeared in Shirdi as a lad of about sixteen, sitting under a neem tree in hard penance, careless of heat and cold. He stayed some three years, vanished for about a year, and came back with Chand Patil's wedding party. Devotees date that return to around 1858, the year after the great rebellion against British rule. Later writers have filled the missing year with stories, among them a claim, recorded by his devotee B.V. Narasimhaswami, that he had fought in the army of the Rani of Jhansi. There is no independent evidence for it.

In Shirdi he first lived under the neem tree and in the scrub around the village. After losing a wrestling match to a local man named Mohiddin Tamboli, the story goes, he gave up wrestling and took to wearing a long knee-length robe, the *kafni*, and a cloth tied round his head. This was the ordinary dress of a Muslim fakir, and many of the Hindu villagers treated him with suspicion as a Muslim. At some point, probably in his early years, he moved into a small, half-ruined mosque at the edge of the village. He called it Dwarkamai, after Dwarka, the city of Krishna, joined to *mai*, mother. He lived and slept there, or in a small building nearby called the Chavadi, for the rest of his life.

<aside class="note"><strong>The dhuni and the udi.</strong> In the mosque he kept a fire, the <i>dhuni</i>, burning day and night. He gave its ash, <i>udi</i>, to visitors as they left, and devotees used it as medicine and protection. The fire is still kept alight in Dwarkamai.</aside>

For decades he was a local figure. He begged for his food from a few houses each day, and worked as a village healer, a *hakim*, giving herbs and udi to the sick. He tended a small garden and watered it himself. The *Satcharita* tells how the oil sellers of the village once refused him oil for the mosque's lamps at Diwali, and how he filled the lamps with water and they burned all night. It reports this, and a great many similar stories, as miracles. This chapter reports them as what his devotees believed.

From about 1910 his reputation spread far beyond the district. The main cause was Das Ganu, a former police constable turned singer of devotional *kirtan*, who sang Sai Baba's praises across Maharashtra. Pleaders, officials and businessmen began coming from Bombay by train and cart. The lawyer G.S. Khaparde kept a diary of his visits. H.S. Dixit, a Bombay solicitor, built a wada, a guest house, in the village. A rich Nagpur devotee, Gopalrao Buti, began building a large stone house intended as a temple to Krishna. Hindu visitors began to worship Sai Baba himself with the full Hindu ritual of *arati*, lamps and songs, inside the mosque. He let them. He also allowed Muslim devotees to recite the Fatiha and hold their own observances there, and he liked to listen to *qawwali*, the devotional songs of Sufi shrines.

One of the people who arrived in these years was Govind Raghunath Dabholkar, a magistrate from Bombay whom Sai Baba nicknamed Hemadpant. He came to Shirdi intermittently from 1910 to 1916. He asked Sai Baba for permission to write his life and stories, and by his own account received it. The actual writing seems to have been done mostly after Sai Baba's death. The result, the *Shri Sai Satcharita*, runs to fifty-three chapters and more than nine thousand verses in the Marathi *ovi* metre used by the poet-saints of Maharashtra. Dabholkar died in 1929; one source gives his birth year as 1856, another as 1859. The book was published in 1930, and one account gives the date as 26 November. An earlier copy of an online encyclopedia gives 1922, which appears to confuse the date the writing began with the date of publication. Nagesh Vasudev Gunaji's English adaptation, published in 1944, is the version most English readers know.

In August 1918 Sai Baba told those around him that he would soon leave his body. He died on 15 October 1918, the day of the festival of Vijayadashami, in the mosque, resting against a devotee. He was buried, as he had asked, in Buti's unfinished stone house. It became the Samadhi Mandir, and the shrine trust that runs it was formed a few years after his death.

## The teaching

Sai Baba wrote nothing and gave no systematic teaching. What survives are sayings, parables and exchanges, remembered by devotees and written down by Dabholkar, Khaparde, Narasimhaswami and others. Read together, they show a teacher who worked through relationship. He asked people to trust him and wait.

**Two coins.** He often asked visitors for *dakshina*, an offering of money, and gave away what he received the same day. The two "coins" he asked for most famously were inner ones, *shraddha* and *saburi*, faith and patience. Faith meant reliance on the guru; patience meant enduring what came without forcing the result. Most of the advice recorded in the *Satcharita* comes back to these two words.

**Surrender to the guru.** He spoke of himself as the devotee's guru and protector. "Look to me, and I shall look to you" is one of the sayings attributed to him. He gave no mantra, no formal initiation and no technique of meditation. Devotees describe him teaching through ordinary events, a scolding, a request for money, a story about a past life, and leaving the listener to work out what it meant.

**Allah Malik.** The phrase the *Satcharita* says he "always uttered" is <q>Allah Malik</q>, "God is the master" or "God is the sole owner." It is an ordinary Muslim expression of reliance on God, and it comes up again and again in the text. He sent Muslim visitors back to the Qur'an and Hindu visitors to the *Ramayana*, the *Bhagavad Gita* and the *Yoga Vasishtha*. He condemned quarrelling over religion and caste.

<aside class="note"><strong>Sabka Malik Ek.</strong> The slogan most associated with him today, "Sabka Malik Ek," "everyone's Master is one," is painted on temples, souvenirs and film posters. I have not found it in the <i>Satcharita</i>, and the reference works I consulted do not trace it to an early source. It reads as a later Hindi summary of what he taught, built on the Allah Malik he is recorded as saying.</aside>

**Hindu or Muslim.** Chapter seven of the *Satcharita* lays out the puzzle with some relish. If you thought he was a Hindu, he favoured circumcision. If you thought he was a Muslim, his ears were pierced, as Hindu boys' are. He lived in a mosque, kept a sacred fire in it like a Hindu ascetic, celebrated the birthday of Rama and allowed the Muslim *sandal* procession of the Urus festival in the same season. The book does not resolve the puzzle and does not try to.

**Karma and the soul.** Much of what he said to Hindu devotees uses the familiar vocabulary of Hindu devotion. People are souls, distinct from their bodies; what happens to them follows from past actions; the guru can lighten that load if they hold on to him. He also told long allegorical stories about past lives, his own and his devotees', which Dabholkar records in detail.

### What the record cannot settle

Where did his religion come from? Three positions are on offer, and each has real evidence behind it.

The **Hindu reading** rests on his own reported statements about Brahmin birth at Pathri and the guru Venkusa, on the Hindu worship he accepted, and on the way the *Satcharita* frames him as a saint of the Marathi bhakti world. Many Hindu devotees came to see him as an incarnation of Dattatreya, the deity revered by the Nath yogis and Marathi ascetics, or of Shiva. In the 1970s a field study claimed to establish Pathri as his birthplace and to identify his birth family; some accounts give his birth name as Haribhau Bhusari. A temple was built on the claimed site and opened in 1999.

The **Sufi reading** rests on his dress, his dwelling, his constant Allah Malik, his use of the Fatiha and qawwali, and the testimony of Muslim devotees who regarded him as a *wali*, a friend of God. Its fullest scholarly statement is Marianne Warren's *Unravelling the Enigma: Shirdi Sai Baba in the Light of Sufism*. Warren worked from the notebook of Abdul Baba, a Muslim attendant who lived with Sai Baba for some twenty-nine years, tended the mosque's lamps and wrote down his master's sayings. Her translation shows Sai Baba explaining Islamic history and theology to Abdul in detail. Her argument is that his Islam has been played down in later Hindu devotional writing.

The **reading of the scholars in between** is that the question was the wrong one. Antonio Rigopoulos, whose 1993 study remains the standard academic life, describes a man who deliberately refused to identify with either community and moved between their idioms, as many Deccan holy men had done for centuries. He also notes how much of the record comes from Hindu devotees writing for a Hindu audience.

The evidence is lopsided in a way readers should know about. The largest early source, the *Satcharita*, was written by a Brahmin in Marathi for Hindu readers. The main Muslim source, Abdul's notebook, is small and reached print only through Warren. The Pathri claim surfaced decades after his death and rests partly on a passage added to a later edition of a Sai biography. None of this proves any side wrong. It means the picture any reader holds depends heavily on which book they read first.

I find Sai Baba the most difficult figure in this book to see clearly, because the man is almost entirely covered by what came after him. His temples now outnumber those of many gods. What I can see underneath is a poor holy man in a ruined mosque who said "God is the master," asked for faith and patience, and refused to tell anyone what he was.

## Lineage

He gave no formal initiation and named no successor. The tree therefore shows influences above him and, below, people who lived with him and people who later claimed him.

<MiniTree id="shirdi-sai-baba" />

Above him stand the **Sufi orders** of the Deccan, whose dress and idiom he used, and **Venkusa** of Selu, the Hindu guru he is reported to have named. The Venkusa link appears as a claim, because it comes through devotees' accounts written after his death.

Below him are the people who **lived with him**. **Mhalsapati**, the Khandoba priest who named him, slept beside him in the mosque on alternate nights for years. **Abdul Baba** kept his notebook and, after his death, kept the tomb. **Upasni Maharaj**, a Brahmin scholar sent to him in 1911, spent about three years under his instruction, much of it in the Khandoba temple, and went on to found an ashram at Sakori. Through Upasni, and through one meeting in December 1915, the line reaches **Meher Baba**, who placed Sai Baba among the five "Perfect Masters" of his age.

Below those are the **claimants**. In May 1940 a fourteen-year-old boy in Puttaparthi, in what is now Andhra Pradesh, announced that he was Sai Baba of Shirdi reborn. As **Sathya Sai Baba** he built one of the largest religious movements of modern India, and in 1963 he added that a third Sai, Prema Sai, would follow him. The tree shows this as a claim, made by Sathya Sai Baba and accepted by his followers. B.V. **Narasimhaswami**, who never met Sai Baba, became his most energetic promoter after his death and carried his worship into south India.

## The practice

Sai Baba taught no method. Devotional practice grew up around him, and the *Satcharita* itself became part of it.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Remember God's name.</strong> He urged constant remembrance, which Hindus called <i>japa</i> and Muslims <i>dhikr</i>, without prescribing a formula.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Give.</strong> Food and money to those who ask. "If anybody wants any money from you and you are not inclined to give, do not give, but do not bark at him like a dog."</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Hold on to faith and patience.</strong> Trust the guru and wait.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Read.</strong> Devotees read the <i>Satcharita</i> through in a week, a practice called <i>parayan</i>, often timed to festivals.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Take udi.</strong> The ash from the fire is still given out at Shirdi and in Sai temples elsewhere.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

No recording of his voice exists. A small number of photographs survive from his last years. The nearest thing to his voice is the written record of devotees, above all the *Satcharita*, which is freely available online in Gunaji's English version, and the interviews with surviving devotees that Antonio Rigopoulos recorded in 1985 and published open access in 2020.

## Critiques and questions

**The record.** Nearly everything known about Sai Baba comes from devotees, and most of it was written after 1918. The miracle stories cannot be checked. Even the plain facts of his life before Shirdi rest on his own contradictory remarks as remembered by others.

**Whose saint.** Since the 1970s the question of his origins has become a public quarrel. In January 2020 Maharashtra's chief minister, Uddhav Thackeray, announced ₹100 crore to develop Pathri as Sai Baba's birthplace. Shirdi held a day-long shutdown in protest, and after a meeting with Shirdi representatives the state kept the grant but dropped the word "birthplace" from its name. Pathri's representatives said their claim still stood.

**Can Hindus worship him.** Some Hindu religious leaders have objected to Sai Baba's worship in Hindu temples on the grounds that he was a Muslim fakir with no place in Hindu scripture. Swami Swaroopanand Saraswati, Shankaracharya of Dwarka, made such statements in 2014 and was answered by protests from Sai devotees. In October 2024 a group calling itself the Sanatan Rakshak Dal removed Sai Baba images from several temples in Varanasi on the same grounds. Sai devotees and his defenders reply that he never asked to be classed as anything and that devotion to him has been part of Hindu practice for over a century.

**The money.** Sai Baba asked for money and gave it away the same day. The shrine at Shirdi is now one of the richest in India and draws, by the trust's own figures as reported, around twenty-five thousand pilgrims a day. Critics note the distance between the two; devotees point to the free meals and hospitals the trust funds.

## Descendants

His descendants fall into two groups. The first are those who knew him or sat with someone who did: **Upasni Maharaj** at Sakori, the nuns of the **Kanya Kumari Sthan** that Upasni founded, and **Meher Baba**, who made Sai Baba part of his own account of the spiritual hierarchy. The second group claimed him from outside: **Sathya Sai Baba**, who said he was Sai Baba reborn, and the countless independent Sai temples, many in south India and the diaspora, that grew from Narasimhaswami's work. The Shri Saibaba Sansthan Trust at Shirdi has no authority over any of them.
