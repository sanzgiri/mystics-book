<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Sivananda means the bliss (ananda) of Shiva. Saraswati is the name of the branch of Shankara's monastic order into which he was initiated. Followers call him Gurudev, and the township that grew round his ashram is called Sivanandanagar. The name is also spelled Shivananda.</aside>

<section class="scene">

<p>Rishikesh, 8 January 1950. The ashram on the west bank of the Ganga is holding its night satsang, the evening gathering of the swami and his disciples. A man breaks in on the gathering and goes for Sivananda. The swami is sixty-two, a former doctor who has lived on this stretch of the river for more than twenty-five years.</p>

<p>The account here is his own, from a short essay called "How God Came into My Life," which later opened his autobiography. He gives no detail of the attack itself. He writes that "the Lord came to me in the form of a half-demented assailant, who disturbed the night Satsang at the Ashram," and that he "bowed to him, worshipped him and sent him home." In the same essay he lists the other forms in which God had come to him: first the image his father worshipped at home, which to his "child-mind" was God, and then, during his years as a doctor abroad, the patients. "Then in Malaya, God came to me in the form of the sick."</p>

<p>The essay is two pages long, and it holds most of what he taught. He believed God is met in whoever is in front of you and that the right response is service, and he thought the rest of yoga followed from that.</p>

</section>

## Life

Kuppuswami was born on 8 September 1887 at Pattamadai, a village on the Tamraparani river in the far south of the Madras Presidency. His father, P. S. Vengu Iyer, was a revenue officer and a devotee of Shiva. His mother was Parvati Ammal. He was the third and last son of an orthodox Brahmin family that, according to the Divine Life Society, counted the sixteenth-century Advaita scholar Appayya Dikshita among its ancestors. As a boy he helped his father with the daily worship, carrying flowers for the image.

He was a good student. He went to the Rajah's High School at Ettayapuram and then to S.P.G. College in Tiruchirappalli. In his autobiography he recalls, "I learnt fencing from a teacher who belonged to a low caste," and adds that he was told it was unbecoming for a caste Brahmin to be the pupil of an untouchable. He then studied medicine at the medical school in Tanjore. One of the society's biographies says he earned the title M.B., C.M.; another speaks only of two years of study, and the qualification has not been independently confirmed. He practised in Tiruchi and, with a hundred rupees from his mother, started a small medical journal called *Ambrosia*.

In 1913 he sailed for Singapore on the S.S. *Tara*. He records that he was frightened of the meat served on board because he came from an orthodox family. In Malaya he went to Seremban, where a Dr Harold Parsons introduced him to A. G. Robins, the manager of a rubber estate that had its own hospital, and Robins took him on as an assistant at the estate hospital. He stayed in Malaya about ten years. The society's accounts describe him treating poor patients free of charge. He later wrote that he could look back on the whole Malayan period "as a single event."

He does not explain clearly why he left. The introduction to his autobiography notes the gap. He says only that an aspiration grew in him and that it "drew me from Malaya to the Himalaya." In 1923 he gave up his post and returned to India. He travelled north as a wandering ascetic, stopping in Madras and at Benares, and stayed for a time at Dhalaj on the Chandrabhaga river, where, by the society's account, a postmaster suggested he go to Rishikesh. The Divine Life Society's biographies date his arrival in Rishikesh to 8 May 1924. Swami Krishnananda, his long-serving general secretary, said in a 1972 talk that he arrived in 1922, which does not fit the society's own date of 1923 for his leaving Malaya. The year is therefore given as 1922 or 1924 in different sources.

On 1 June 1924 he took sannyasa, the vows of a renunciant monk, from Swami Vishvananda Saraswati, a monk of the Saraswati branch of the Dashanami order, who gave him the name Swami Sivananda Saraswati. Krishnananda says the two met for only a few minutes. The formal fire ritual of renunciation, the viraja homa, was performed by Swami Vishnudevananda, the head of the Kailas Ashram in Rishikesh (an older monk, distinct from the later disciple of the same name). Vishvananda then went on his way, and Sivananda had no further training from him that the sources record.

<aside class="note"><strong>Viraja homa.</strong> The fire offering at the heart of the sannyasa rite, in which the candidate symbolically offers his past life, his ties and his desires into the fire before taking the ochre robe.</aside>

For the next several years he lived at Swarg Ashram, on the east bank of the Ganga, in a small, dilapidated hut that the society's biography says was "infested with scorpions." By Krishnananda's account he ate dry bread with Ganga water, spent long hours on a sandbank across the river from Lakshman Jhula, and walked to the almshouse for food at fixed times. He described tapas, ascetic discipline, as "burning like fire by sense-control." He also kept doctoring. He visited the huts of sick sadhus with medicines, massaged their legs, begged food for them and fed them by hand, and sat up with them at night. A friend, Hari Ganesh Ambekar, sent him a rupee a month. Sivananda used it to buy medicine and curd for a neighbour with dysentery. In 1927, with the money from an insurance policy that had matured, he opened a small charitable dispensary at Lakshman Jhula, the Satya Sevashram.

He also travelled. Between periods of seclusion he walked on pilgrimage across India, went to Kailas and Lake Manasarovar in Tibet, and visited both the Ramana ashram at Tiruvannamalai and the Sri Aurobindo Ashram at Pondicherry. From the early 1930s he began to give public talks and lead group chanting on tour. And he began to write. An early book, *Practice of Yoga*, volume one, was published by Ganesh & Co. of Madras in 1929.

In 1932 he moved across the river to the west bank near Muni ki Reti, into an old, disused hut that, in the society's words, "looked like an abandoned cowshed." Disciples began to arrive and more sheds were cleaned out for them. In 1936 he founded the Divine Life Society and registered it as a trust. From then on the ashram grew into an institution. The monthly journal *The Divine Life* began in September 1938. An unbroken chanting of the Hare Rama mahamantra, the Akhanda Mahamantra Kirtan, began on 3 December 1943. An Ayurvedic pharmacy followed in 1945, and in 1948 the Yoga-Vedanta Forest Academy (at first called a Forest University), which gave resident students a structured course in yoga and Vedanta. He set up federations of religions and of sadhus, convened a World Parliament of Religions at the ashram in 1953, and opened an eye hospital in 1957.

His method of spreading the teaching was print. He wrote quickly and in plain English, on every subject from Upanishadic philosophy to diet, and the society printed the books and leaflets and sent them out free or cheaply to anyone who wrote. The number of his books is given as more than two hundred in some sources and more than three hundred by the society. The energy he put into it reportedly earned him the nickname "Swami Propagandananda." In 1950 he made a fast tour of India and Ceylon, founding branches of the society as he went. The night attack on 8 January came that same winter.

After he became a monk he never travelled to the West. The people he trained did that travelling. He died in his hut on the Ganga on 14 July 1963. His disciple Swami Chidananda was elected president of the society the following month.

## The teaching

Sivananda's philosophy was the Advaita Vedanta of his order. There is one reality, Brahman, pure consciousness, and the individual self is that reality. The world of separate things is real only in a dependent and passing way. Liberation is the direct knowledge of this identity. Krishnananda summed up his guru's priority in the phrase "God-realisation first, everything else afterwards." On metaphysics he followed Shankara closely.

His interest was in the route, and here he was a practical man and a doctor. He thought that people are made of several capacities and that a spiritual practice which trains only one of them leaves the person lopsided.

**The Yoga of Synthesis.** He called his method the Yoga of Synthesis. Its basic image is a vehicle. "He is like a tricycle or a three-wheeled chariot," he wrote of the human being, and action, emotion and intelligence are the horses. Karma yoga, selfless work, trains the hands. Bhakti yoga, devotion, trains the heart. Jnana yoga, discriminating enquiry into the self, trains the head. Raja yoga, the meditative discipline of Patanjali, steadies the mind that has to do all three. "One-sided development is not commendable," he wrote, and "The Yoga of Synthesis alone will develop the head, heart and hand, and lead one to perfection." He did not ask anyone to practise all the paths equally. "Everyone should have one Yoga as the basic Yoga and combine other Yogas." A temperamentally devotional person would make chanting the centre of the day and add some study and some service. A scholar would do the reverse. To this he added hatha yoga, the postures and breathing exercises, for health: "A little practice of Hatha Yoga (Asanas and Pranayamas) will give you good health." In the autobiography he says the synthesis came out of his own life, each phase growing into the next. "One coalesced into the next, and the Yoga of Synthesis was the fruit."

He reduced the whole scheme to six words, which the Divine Life Society still prints at the head of its pages. Serve, Love, Give, Purify, Meditate, Realise. Another of his short formulas, "Be good, do good," appears in many versions. The anthropologist Sarah Strauss, who did fieldwork on the yoga that came out of his ashram, connects the four main verbs to the four yogas that Vivekananda had set out half a century earlier.

**Discipline in small units.** Sivananda's books are less about theory than about a daily routine. His best-known text is a single page, the Twenty Important Spiritual Instructions. It begins, "Get up at 4 a.m. daily," because the hour before dawn, Brahmamuhurta, is favourable for practice. It goes on to asana and breathing, repetition of a mantra (any mantra the reader likes, "from 108 times to 21,600 times daily"), simple vegetarian food, a fixed place for meditation, daily study of scripture, regular giving, a fast on the eleventh day of the lunar fortnight, silence "for a couple of hours daily," truthful and sparing speech, self-reliance without servants, and constant remembrance of God. Instruction eighteen asks the reader to "Keep a daily spiritual diary and self-correction register as Benjamin Franklin did," reviewing the day each night and making resolutions for the next. The list ends, "This is the essence of all spiritual Sadhana. It will lead you to liberation."

**The ego as the main obstacle.** Under the routine lies a moral teaching about the ego. He wrote that "The aim of Sadhana is the curbing of the ego which veils the Truth," and that the most testing practice is how one bears other people. "Bear insult: bear injury: this is the highest Sadhana." In community, he said, "you have constantly to adapt, adjust and accommodate." He also wrote a rhyming Song of Eighteen Ities, a list of virtues ending in "-ity," with the advice to pick one virtue at a time, develop it fully and root out its opposite.

**Yoga for everyone.** Sivananda wrote for householders as much as for monks, and for Indians and foreigners alike. He wanted the practices out of the hands of specialists. Asana, pranayama, mantra, prayer and Vedanta all appeared in cheap pamphlets, in English, with instructions a clerk could follow before going to the office. His Belgian follower André Van Lysebeth, in a 1981 article as it is usually cited, said this was what his critics disliked: his modern ways of spreading the teaching, the sheer scale on which he offered yoga to the general public, and a practice that mixed a few asanas with a little breathing, meditation and devotion, a little of everything. Van Lysebeth wrote as a follower, not as a critic. Elliott Goldberg, who gives Sivananda a chapter in *The Path of Modern Yoga* (2016), traces how yoga among middle-class Indians moved toward health and fitness in these decades, before the same mix went abroad.

**Medicine and the body.** He kept writing about health throughout his life, including books on Ayurveda, nature cure and diet. He mixed his clinical training with traditional yogic physiology. In *Practice of Brahmacharya* he wrote that "The preservation of semen is the secret of health and longevity," and that through yoga the seminal energy is drawn upward and stored as *ojas*, a subtle vitality. He insisted on a strict vegetarian diet on both moral and medical grounds.

**All religions.** Like most of the reformers in this book, he taught that the great religions lead to the same goal. He founded an All-world Religions Federation in 1945 and convened a World Parliament of Religions at his ashram in 1953. His own devotional practice was Hindu, built round the names of Rama, Krishna and Shiva.

I find him hard to place because his originality is spread so thin across so many pages. Each piece of his teaching can be traced to an older source. What I think is new is the combination of a doctor's practicality with a monk's renunciation, and his conviction that the whole of yoga could be printed in cheap pamphlets and done by a clerk before breakfast. That conviction, more than any idea, is what his disciples took to Montreal, Munger, Virginia and British Columbia. Much of what Westerners call hatha yoga, and much of what middle-class Indians call Vedanta, came down to them through his students.

## Lineage

Sivananda's monastic lineage is formal and short: one initiation from a wandering monk he barely knew. His real place in the tree is as a trunk from which many branches grew, through disciples who founded organisations of their own.

<MiniTree id="sivananda" />

The initiation by Swami Vishvananda Saraswati on 1 June 1924 is documented in the Divine Life Society's own records and is the only guru link he had. It places him, and through him a large share of modern Indian monks, in the Saraswati branch of Shankara's order. His influence on later teachers is documented in their own accounts and in the dates of their sannyasa. Swami Krishnananda came in 1944 and took sannyasa on 14 January 1946. Swami Satyananda came in 1943, took sannyasa on 12 September 1947, and later founded the Bihar School of Yoga. Swami Vishnudevananda, then in the army, came to the ashram in 1947 after reading one of Sivananda's leaflets, which opened "An ounce of practice is worth tons of theory," and became the first teacher of hatha yoga at the Forest Academy. Swami Chinmayananda took sannyasa on 25 February 1949, and Swami Chidananda and Swami Satchidananda in the same year, Chidananda on 10 July. Sylvia Hellman, born in Berlin, took sannyasa in 1956 as Swami Sivananda Radha. The link to Vivekananda is an influence of ideas and is a scholar's reading. Sivananda did not meet him.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Rise before dawn.</strong> Get up at four, in Brahmamuhurta, wash, and sit at once for prayer, japa and meditation in a fixed place kept only for that purpose.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Choose a base yoga.</strong> Decide which of the paths suits your temperament (service, devotion, enquiry or meditation) and make it the centre of the day. Add a little of each of the others.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep the body fit.</strong> Do a short set of postures and breathing exercises daily. Eat simple vegetarian food in moderation, and fast or take only milk and fruit on Ekadashi.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Repeat the name.</strong> Take a mantra of your choice and repeat it a fixed number of times every day, with a rosary kept at hand. Join group chanting where you can.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Study and silence.</strong> Read some scripture daily, such as the Gita. Keep silence for an hour or two each day, and speak truthfully, sweetly and little.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Serve and give.</strong> Do some unpaid service and give a fixed share of income away. Bear insult and injury without retaliation, and adapt, adjust and accommodate.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep the diary.</strong> Each night, review the day and fill in the spiritual diary: hours of sleep, japa, study and meditation, and every lapse. Make a resolution for tomorrow.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

A good deal survives. Most of his books are kept in print by the Divine Life Society, and many are free online, including the *Autobiography*, which opens with his essay "How God Came into My Life." Recordings of his voice leading kirtan circulate in followers' collections, described as historical recordings. Short pieces of film from the ashram, showing him sitting by the river and welcoming visitors, have been put together with his recorded voice by the organisations of his disciples. The dates and makers of the original recordings are not given.

## Critiques and questions

**Spread thin.** The objection his own followers recorded was to his methods. Van Lysebeth reported that critics disliked both his modern methods of spreading the teaching and the scale on which he offered yoga to the general public. The nickname "Swami Propagandananda" belongs to the same complaint. Even the size of the output is uncertain: above two hundred books in some sources, above three hundred by the society's own figure.

**The doctor and the yogi.** Sivananda's medical training lent authority to his health writing. He also taught as fact traditional doctrines that modern physiology does not recognise, such as the claim that preserved semen is converted into ojas and is "the secret of health and longevity," and he wrote that meat-eating is "highly deleterious to health."

**Ashram and establishment.** The anthropologist Lise McKean studied the Divine Life Society in *Divine Enterprise: Gurus and the Hindu Nationalist Movement* (1996), with chapters on Sivananda, life in the ashram, and the 1987 centenary of his birth. Her publisher's summary says she examines how "participation by upper-caste ruling class groups in the Divine Life Society" and other Hindu organisations "further legitimates their own authority," within a wider study of gurus and the Hindu nationalist movement. No published response from the society to her book was found in research for this chapter.

**What the lineage inherited.** Several of the organisations founded by his disciples later faced serious allegations of sexual misconduct by their leaders. In 1991 protesters accused Swami Satchidananda of sexually abusing students, and several former disciples alleged that he had used his authority to draw them into sexual relationships. He denied the allegations; none of the women filed criminal charges. In December 2019 Julie Salter, his former personal assistant, publicly accused Swami Vishnudevananda, who died in 1993, of sexual abuse, and other women followed with similar accusations. He was never charged; the allegations were made after his death. The board of the International Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centres said it was dismayed, apologised for not believing Salter's earlier complaint of 2007, and promised an independent investigation, which it then suspended in 2020. Project SATYA, an investigation commissioned by former members, reported in November 2020 that it found the allegations against him credible. Satyananda's Australian ashram was the subject of a Royal Commission case study in 2014; the details are in his chapter. None of these allegations concerns Sivananda himself. All concern conduct alleged to have taken place after his death in 1963, by disciples who were then leading their own organisations.

## Descendants

The Divine Life Society continues at Rishikesh under elected presidents, with Swami Chidananda (president from 1963; died 2008) and Swami Krishnananda (general secretary from 1961 until his death in 2001, after serving from 1957 as the society's secretary for finance) as its best-known heads. The society runs branches across India and abroad. The larger legacy lies in the organisations his disciples founded. Swami Vishnudevananda's Sivananda Yoga Vedanta Centres, begun in Montreal in 1959, two years after he left for the West, spread worldwide a fixed sequence of twelve basic postures and a summary of practice as five points (exercise, breathing, relaxation, diet with positive thinking, and meditation); his *Complete Illustrated Book of Yoga* (1960) was a long-running bestseller. Swami Satyananda's Bihar School of Yoga at Munger (1964) developed yoga nidra and trained teachers internationally. Swami Satchidananda's Integral Yoga reached the American counterculture, and he gave the opening address at Woodstock in August 1969. Swami Chinmayananda's Chinmaya Mission (1953) took Vedanta study to English-speaking city audiences, and through his student Swami Dayananda of Arsha Vidya to a generation of teachers. Swami Sivananda Radha founded Yasodhara Ashram in British Columbia, at Kootenay Bay from 1963. Other disciples, such as Swami Venkatesananda in South Africa and Australia and Swami Jyotirmayananda in Miami, founded their own centres.
