## Life

Jotirao Phule was born in Poona in 1827, into a family of the Mali, or gardener, caste, a community of cultivators and flower sellers then shut out of most education. He went to a Marathi school from 1834 to 1838 and, after a break, to a Christian mission school from 1841 to 1847. In 1847 he read Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man*, and he later named Paine as an influence. He married Savitribai in 1840, when both were children.

In 1848, by his own later account, he was insulted and turned away while walking in a Brahmin friend's wedding procession. That year he and Savitribai opened a school for girls of the lowest castes in Poona, and two more girls' schools followed in 1851. In 1849 the couple had to leave his father's house because of this work. In 1863 they opened a home where widows could give birth in safety, to prevent infanticide, and in 1868 Phule let untouchables draw water from the well on his property.

In June 1873 he published *Gulamgiri* (Slavery), a dialogue in Marathi dedicated to the good people of the United States for their work against Black slavery. On 24 September 1873 he founded the Satyashodhak Samaj, with himself as first president. Its members pledged to worship one Creator, whom Phule called *Nirmik*, without the help of any mediator, and to treat all people as equal children of God. In October 1873 the Marathi paper *Satyadipika* reported that some seven hundred families of cultivating and artisan castes in Poona had joined, and the Samaj began holding weddings without Brahmin priests.

He sat on the Poona municipal council from 1876 to 1882 and wrote on the condition of peasants in *Shetkaryacha Asud* (The Cultivator's Whipcord, 1883). On 11 May 1888 a public meeting gave him the title Mahatma. He died in Poona on 28 November 1890. His last book, *Sarvajanik Satya Dharma Pustak* (A Book of True Religion for All), appeared in 1891.

## Why this figure appears here

The men in this part asked how the Vedas could be reread by reason. Phule asked who had written them and whom they served. In *Gulamgiri* he told Indian history as a series of invasions by Aryan Brahmins who conquered the original inhabitants and wrote scriptures to keep them subject. The avatars of Vishnu become, in his telling, stages of that conquest. The story of the dwarf Vamana tricking King Bali becomes the overthrow of a just non-Aryan king, and Bali becomes the ideal ruler whose return Marathi farmers still invoke in a common saying. The verses that describe the Brahmin born from the mouth of the cosmic person, Phule argued, were invented to make an arrangement of power look divine.

Gail Omvedt points out that he inverted the colonial theory of Aryan conquest, keeping its outline and reversing its values. Later historians question his history, but his method was a rereading of scripture by reason of a different kind. He read the texts as human documents with authors and interests, and he rejected miracle and myth as evidence. Unlike the Brahmos and Dayananda Saraswati, he wanted nothing saved from them. What remained was a single Creator, open to everyone directly, and a religion of truth that measured belief by whether it treated people as equals.

The two currents met once. In September 1875, when Dayananda Saraswati's supporters held a farewell procession for him in Poona and orthodox opponents threatened to break it up, Phule and members of the Satyashodhak Samaj walked with it to protect it. B. R. Ambedkar, who counted Phule among his teachers, dedicated *Who Were the Shudras?* (1946) to his memory.
