## Life

Jotirao Phule was born in Poona in 1827, into a family of the Mali, or gardener, caste, a community of cultivators and flower sellers then shut out of most education. His father, Govindrao, supplied flowers to the households of the Peshwas, which gave the family the name Phule. His mother, Chimnabai, died when he was about a year old, and a relative named Sagunabai raised him. He went to a Marathi school from 1834 to 1838. According to a university module on Phule at ePG Pathshala, pressure from some Brahmins led his father to take him out of school and put him to work in the garden, and two neighbours, a Muslim teacher and a Christian, persuaded the father to let him return. From 1841 to 1847 he attended the Scottish Mission High School in Poona. In 1847 he read Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man*, and he later named Paine as an influence. Britannica lists the American anti-slavery movement, the Buddha and the poet Kabir among his other influences. He married Savitribai in 1840, when both were children, and began to teach her to read and write the following year.

In 1848, by the account he later gave, he was insulted and turned away while walking in a Brahmin friend's wedding procession. That year he and Savitribai opened a school for girls in Poona, meant for girls of the Shudra and untouchable castes, though some accounts say girls of all castes attended, and two more girls' schools followed in 1851. Savitribai taught in the first school because no other woman teacher was available, and she later headed the women's section of the Samaj. In 1849 the couple had to leave his father's house because of this work. The Board of Education recognised their girls' schools in 1852, and in 1855 he opened a night school and published the play *Tritiya Ratna*, whose title refers to knowledge as a third eye that lets people see through the ideas that keep them down. In 1863 they opened a home where widows could give birth in safety, to prevent infanticide, and in 1868 Phule let untouchables draw water from the well on his property. He supported the remarriage of widows from 1860. In 1869 he published *Brahmananche Kasab*, on the exploitation of laypeople by priests, and a ballad on Shivaji in June of that year.

In June 1873 he published *Gulamgiri* (Slavery), a dialogue in Marathi dedicated to the good people of the United States for their work against Black slavery. On 24 September 1873 he founded the Satyashodhak Samaj, with himself as first president. Its members pledged to worship one Creator, whom Phule called *Nirmik*, without the help of any mediator, and to treat all people as equal children of God. In October 1873 the Marathi paper *Satyadipika* reported that some seven hundred families of cultivating and artisan castes in Poona had joined, and the Samaj began holding weddings without Brahmin priests. The Samaj drew members of many castes and religions, including Brahmins and Muslims, according to Wikipedia. Its newspaper *Deenbandhu* ran from 1877 to 1897, and it used folk dramas, or *tamashas*, to reach peasants.

He sat on the Poona municipal council from 1876 to 1882. On 19 October 1882 he submitted a statement to the Hunter Education Commission arguing that primary schooling for the masses should come before higher education, that village teachers should be trained "as far as possible, out of the cultivating classes" and given a minimum salary, and that their training should include agriculture and sanitation. In it he wrote, "The present number of educated men is very small in relation to the country at large." He wrote on the condition of peasants in *Shetkaryacha Asud* (The Cultivator's Whipcord, 1883). On 11 May 1888 a public meeting gave him the title Mahatma. That year he suffered a stroke that left him paralysed. In 1889 he joined barbers in a campaign against the forced shaving of widows' heads. He died in Poona on 28 November 1890. His last book, *Sarvajanik Satya Dharma Pustak* (A Book of True Religion for All), appeared in 1891.

## Why this figure appears here

The other figures in this part asked how the Vedas could be reread by reason. Phule asked who had written them and whom they served. In *Gulamgiri* he told Indian history as a series of invasions by Aryan Brahmins who conquered the original inhabitants and wrote scriptures to keep them subject. The avatars of Vishnu become, in his telling, stages of that conquest. The story of the dwarf Vamana tricking King Bali becomes the overthrow of a just non-Aryan king, and Bali becomes the ideal ruler whose return Marathi farmers still invoke in a common saying. The verses that describe the Brahmin born from the mouth of the cosmic person, Phule argued, were invented to make an arrangement of power look divine.

Gail Omvedt points out that he inverted the colonial theory of Aryan conquest, keeping its outline and reversing its values. The historian B. R. Sunthankar called his account unhistorical, and Omvedt reads it as an attack on the avatar stories more than a work of history. Even so, his method was a rereading of scripture by reason of a different kind. He read the texts as human documents with authors and interests, and he rejected miracle and myth as evidence. Unlike the Brahmos and Dayananda Saraswati, he wanted nothing saved from them. He kept a single Creator, open to everyone directly, and a religion of truth that measured belief by whether it treated people as equals.

The two currents met once. In September 1875, when Dayananda Saraswati's supporters held a farewell procession for him in Poona and orthodox opponents threatened to break it up, Phule and members of the Satyashodhak Samaj walked with it to protect it. B. R. Ambedkar, who counted Phule among his teachers, dedicated *Who Were the Shudras?* (1946) to his memory.

His sources were mixed. Phule named Paine, and the Scottish mission school gave him an English education. Britannica adds the anti-slavery movements, the Buddha and Kabir. Among his own writings, *Tritiya Ratna* (1855) already treated knowledge as a third eye, a means to see through the ideas that justify subordination, and *Brahmananche Kasab* (1869) turned that eye on the priest's trade. The Samaj put the argument into practice at weddings, where its own simple rites replaced the Brahmin priest's, and through a press and folk theatre addressed to cultivators. The ePG Pathshala module records that Ambedkar regarded Phule as a guru.
