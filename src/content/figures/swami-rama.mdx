## Life

Swami Rama was born in 1925 in the Garhwal Himalaya, in what is now Uttarakhand, to a Brahmin family. His name at birth is given as Brij Kishore Dhasmana or Brij Kishore Kumar. By his own account he was raised from early childhood by a yogi from Bengal who lived in the foothills, and spent his youth in monasteries and caves. Some accounts name this teacher as Madhavananda Bharati. His followers call him Bengali Baba.

The Himalayan Institute Hospital Trust, which he founded, says that at twenty-four he became Shankaracharya of Karvirpitham, a monastic seat in the south, succeeding Dr Kurtkoti, and that he gave up the post in 1952. It also says he studied at Bangalore, Prayag, Varanasi and Oxford and took a medical degree at Darbhanga in 1960. Wikipedia lists further claimed degrees from Hamburg and Utrecht. In 1987 two close followers left his institute after concluding that parts of his official biography had been invented, and a Cult Awareness Network researcher said his academic record could not be verified. The Karvirpitham claim has not been confirmed from that monastery's records for this entry.

He came to the United States in 1969, by his account at his master's request, and taught at YMCAs and in private homes. Dr Elmer Green of the Menninger Foundation in Topeka, Kansas, invited him to take part in research on the voluntary control of states the body normally runs without attention. In 1971 he founded the Himalayan International Institute of Yoga Science and Philosophy in a rented house in Illinois. The Institute gives the place as Barrington and Wikipedia as Glenview. In 1977 the headquarters moved to a large property near Honesdale, Pennsylvania. In 1989 he began a clinic in the hills near Dehradun, which grew into the Himalayan Institute Hospital Trust. He died there on 13 November 1996. The Institute says he consciously left his body.

**The Menninger tests.** The laboratory sessions, around 1970 and 1971, made him famous. According to the published accounts, he produced alpha, theta and delta brain waves at will while remaining aware of what happened in the room, so that he could later report conversations held while his brain showed the pattern of deep sleep. He raised a temperature difference between two spots on one palm. In Elmer Green's own account of a session in March 1970, the two spots ended about 11 degrees Fahrenheit apart, roughly 6 degrees Celsius; other accounts give ten degrees or about five degrees Celsius. He also made his heart race at about 300 beats a minute for some seventeen seconds, a rhythm at which the heart barely pumps blood. The Institute describes this as stopping the heart. The sessions fed the new field of biofeedback.

## The teaching

Swami Rama presented himself as a messenger of the Himalayan sages and said his work was to introduce people to the teacher within. He taught yoga as a practical science that could be tested in the body. The Hospital Trust quotes him: <q>All of the body is in the mind, but not all of the mind is in the body.</q>

Breath was the centre of the method. *Science of Breath*, written with two physicians, calls the breath <q>the link between the body and mind</q> and teaches students to notice their unconscious breathing habits and replace them with better ones. His students were taught breathing from the diaphragm, systematic relaxation of the body and meditation with a mantra. Accounts of the Menninger sessions describe his demonstration of yoga nidra, sleep in which awareness stays alert.

He also built institutions in which doctors and psychologists taught beside monks. The Institute ran courses on holistic health, published a journal and books, and trained teachers. His successor at the head of the Institute, Pandit Rajmani Tigunait, wrote the authorised biography *At the Eleventh Hour* (2001).

## Critiques and questions

**Allegations of sexual misconduct.** Women made allegations of sexual exploitation against Swami Rama from the 1970s onward, concerning Chicago, Minneapolis, New York and India. In 1990 *Yoga Journal* published an investigation by Katharine Webster, "The Case Against Swami Rama of the Himalayas," which reported that numerous former students said they had left the Institute after being sexually exploited.

**The civil verdict.** In 1994 a woman who had come to the Institute at nineteen filed a civil suit in the United States District Court for the Middle District of Pennsylvania, alleging that Swami Rama had sexually abused her there over several months in 1993. Swami Rama was named as a defendant but died about ten months before trial and was dismissed. The case went to trial against the Institute alone. The Institute did not admit or deny the sexual activity, argued that any relationship was consensual, denied knowledge of misconduct and noted Swami Rama's denial. Its lawyer told the press the Institute could not be held responsible. On 4 September 1997 the jury found for the plaintiff and awarded $275,000 in compensatory damages and $1.6 million in punitive damages, about $1.875 million in all. The court later summarised the verdict as finding that Swami Rama had sexual relations with her and abused his position as her guru, and that the Institute was liable both for his acts and for its own negligence. In December 1999 the court denied the Institute's motion for a new trial or a reduced punitive award, ruling that it had not been denied a fair trial and that the punitive award was not excessive. No appellate ruling has been located for this entry. It was a civil judgment. Swami Rama was never charged with a crime.

The Institute's histories of its founder do not discuss these matters, and no public statement from it on the allegations was found for this entry beyond its defence at trial. Its published organisational policies, last updated in September 2025, state that "any form of harassment or abuse is unacceptable, including violations of the student-teacher relationship," without naming him.

## Lineage

Swami Rama stands apart from the large ashram lineages of his time. His guru is known only from his own account, and he called his line simply the Himalayan tradition.
