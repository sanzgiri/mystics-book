## Life

Swami Rama was born in 1925 in the Garhwal Himalaya, in what is now Uttarakhand, to a Brahmin family. His name at birth is given as Brij Kishore Dhasmana or Brij Kishore Kumar. By his own account he was raised from early childhood by a yogi from Bengal who lived in the foothills, and spent his youth in monasteries and caves. Some accounts name this teacher as Madhavananda Bharati. His followers call him Bengali Baba. A 1989 profile in *Hinduism Today* says the teacher initiated him into sannyasa in his early teens, and that villagers in the Himalaya knew Swami Rama as Bhole Baba, "gentle sage." His master's advice, as the Hospital Trust quotes it, was "To obtain freedom from all fear is the first message of the Himalayan sages."

The Himalayan Institute Hospital Trust, which he founded, says that at twenty-four he became Shankaracharya of Karvirpitham, a monastic seat in the south, succeeding Dr Kurtkoti, and that he gave up the post in 1952. It also says he studied at Bangalore, Prayag, Varanasi and Oxford and took a medical degree at Darbhanga in 1960. Wikipedia lists further claimed degrees from Hamburg and Utrecht. Encyclopedia.com gives 1945 for the Darbhanga degree, and Wikipedia says his academic background could not be verified. The Karvirpitham claim has not been confirmed from that monastery's records for this entry. The Trust adds that after leaving the post he spent eleven months in meditation and breathing practice in a sealed cave, then worked as a medical consultant in London, assisted research in parapsychology in Moscow, and founded a clinic and ashram at Rishikesh and a centre in Japan. These are the Trust's statements, and the sources read for this entry give no independent confirmation.

He came to the United States in 1969, by his account at his master's request, and taught hatha yoga at YMCAs and in private homes. Wikipedia says he was troubled that the YMCA presented yoga mainly as fitness and so added philosophy and meditation to his classes. Dr Elmer Green of the Menninger Foundation in Topeka, Kansas, invited him to take part in research on the voluntary control of states the body normally runs without attention. In 1971 he founded the Himalayan International Institute of Yoga Science and Philosophy in a rented house in Illinois. The Institute gives the place as Barrington and Wikipedia as Glenview. In 1977 the headquarters moved to a large property near Honesdale, Pennsylvania. Wikipedia also records an earlier Himalayan Institute of Yoga Science and Philosophy at Kanpur in 1966. In 1989 he began a clinic in the hills near Dehradun, a two-room outpatient clinic according to the Trust, which grew into the Himalayan Institute Hospital Trust. The Trust says it now has 750 beds and a Combined Therapy Program that joins biofeedback, hatha yoga, exercise, nutrition, breathing, relaxation and meditation. He received the Martin Buber Award for Service to Humanity in 1977 (Encyclopedia.com). He died there on 13 November 1996. The Institute says he consciously left his body.

**The Menninger tests.** The laboratory sessions, around 1970 and 1971, made him famous. According to the published accounts, he produced alpha, theta and delta brain waves at will while remaining aware of what happened in the room, so that he could later report conversations held while his brain showed the pattern of deep sleep. He raised a temperature difference between two spots on one palm. In Elmer Green's own account of a session in March 1970, the two spots ended about 11 degrees Fahrenheit apart, roughly 6 degrees Celsius; other accounts give ten degrees or about five degrees Celsius. He also made his heart race at about 300 beats a minute for some seventeen seconds, a rhythm at which the heart barely pumps blood. The Institute describes this as stopping the heart. *Hinduism Today* adds that he slowed his heart from 74 to 52 beats a minute in fifty seconds. The sessions fed the new field of biofeedback.

## The teaching

Swami Rama presented himself as a messenger of the Himalayan sages and said his work was to introduce people to the teacher within. He taught yoga as a practical science that could be tested in the body. The Hospital Trust quotes him: <q>All of the body is in the mind, but not all of the mind is in the body.</q> It also quotes him as saying, "I am a messenger, delivering the wisdom of the Himalayan sages of my tradition." *Hinduism Today* places his teaching in the Advaita Vedanta of the Smarta tradition and says he taught kirtan but not ritual. It quotes him on religion: "Love is the universal religion." He called Hinduism a philosophy and not a religion, a statement the magazine also reports.

Breath was the centre of the method. *Science of Breath*, written with two physicians, calls the breath <q>the link between the body and mind</q> and teaches students to notice their unconscious breathing habits and replace them with better ones. Accounts of the Menninger sessions describe his demonstration of yoga nidra, sleep in which awareness stays alert. Wikipedia says he warned against treating such feats as proof of enlightenment, since they show only the ability to perform them. His own books include *Yoga and Psychotherapy* (1976, with Rudolph Ballentine and Swami Ajaya), *Lectures on Yoga* and *A Practical Guide to Holistic Health* (both 1978).

He also built institutions in which doctors and psychologists taught beside monks. The Institute ran courses on holistic health, published a journal and books, and trained teachers. His successor at the head of the Institute, Pandit Rajmani Tigunait, wrote the authorised biography *At the Eleventh Hour* (2001).

## Lineage

Swami Rama stands apart from the large ashram lineages of his time. His guru is known only from his own account, and he called his line simply the Himalayan tradition.

The edge to Bengali Baba rests on his autobiography and is marked as testimony. The edge to Pandit Rajmani Tigunait, who leads the Institute, is documented.

## The practice

Teachers in the Himalayan tradition in the United States summarise his breath teaching as four emphases, later extended to eight qualities (AHYMSIN). The sequence below follows that account and the Institute's own practice pages. A reader new to breathwork would do well to learn it from a qualified teacher.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Lie down.</strong> Classes usually begin lying in the crocodile pose or the corpse pose, with a guided relaxation that draws attention to the breath.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Breathe from the diaphragm.</strong> The area around the navel gently expands and contracts, and the breath goes in and out through the nose.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Make it silent, smooth, deep and continuous.</strong> These four are the qualities attributed to Swami Rama himself, who asked for no noise, no jerks, no shallowness and no pauses.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Even the two sides.</strong> Later qualities include an even breath and a balance between the nostrils, and the Institute teaches alternate nostril breathing, <i>nadi shodhana</i>, to balance the nervous system.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Carry it into meditation.</strong> The finer qualities are introduced in relation to meditation.</div></li>
</ol>
