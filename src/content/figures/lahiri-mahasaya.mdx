<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> *Mahasaya* is a Bengali honorific, roughly "great-souled sir." His disciples also called him Yogiraj, "king of yogis." In Benares some people called him Kashi Baba, a title he is said to have discouraged.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>This scene comes from one source, the account that Paramahansa Yogananda published in 1946 in <i>Autobiography of a Yogi</i>, told in Lahiri's own voice as his disciples remembered it.</p>
<p>Autumn 1861. A thirty-three-year-old accountant in the Military Engineering Department at Danapur, near Patna, is called in by his superior and told he is being transferred to Ranikhet, in the Kumaon hills, where an army post is being set up. He makes the journey of about five hundred miles by horse and buggy, which takes some thirty days. The office work at Ranikhet is light, and he spends his free hours walking in the hills.</p>
<p>One afternoon, on a mountain called Drongiri, he hears his name called. He climbs toward the voice and finds a young-looking ascetic standing in a clearing near some caves, who greets him by name and says he has been waiting. Over the next days, in Lahiri's account, the stranger restores memories of a past life, initiates him into a method of yoga, and sends him back to his desk. A letter arrives from the head office saying that the transfer to Ranikhet was a mistake. He returns to Danapur.</p>
</section>

## Life

Shyama Charan Lahiri was born on 30 September 1828 in the village of Ghurni, on the Jalangi river near Krishnanagar, in the Nadia district of Bengal. His parents, Gaur Mohan Lahiri and Muktakeshi, were Brahmins. His mother died when he was small, in 1832 or 1833 depending on the source, and some accounts connect her death with a flood that destroyed the family home. His father moved the family to Benares, where the boy grew up and studied Sanskrit, philosophy and English.

In 1846 he married Kashi Moni (also written Kashimoni), in a match arranged by his father. They had two sons, Tinkori and Dukori, and three daughters, Harimoti, Harikamini and Harimohini, according to a biographical note published by Sri Chinmoy's library. In 1851 he took a job as a clerk in the Military Engineering Department of the British government, which handled public works for the army. He stayed in government service for thirty-five years and was promoted several times. His postings included Ghazipur, Mirzapur, Naini Tal, Danapur and Benares. Yogananda calls him an accountant; Chinmoy's note calls him a clerk. The work was the same, keeping the books for construction and supply.

The event on which everything else rests is the meeting near Ranikhet. Sources place it in 1861. The Wikipedia entry gives a precise date, 27 November 1861, which I have not found in a primary source. The account comes to us through Lahiri's disciples and above all through Yogananda, whose book appeared fifty-one years after Lahiri's death. In it, the ascetic, known since as Babaji, tells Lahiri that he had silently arranged the transfer himself. He initiates Lahiri into Kriya Yoga in a golden palace that he has conjured on the mountain for the occasion and dissolves the next morning. Lahiri then spends seven days in *samadhi*, absorbed meditation, in the cave. Babaji tells him that his life belongs "in the crowded marts, serving as an example of the ideal yogi-householder."

Then comes the detail that matters most for the teaching. In this account Babaji first ruled that Kriya should go only to disciples who had vowed complete renunciation. Lahiri pleaded for ordinary people with families and jobs, and Babaji relented: "Give Kriya freely to all who humbly ask for help." The same account describes a later evening at a Bengali household in Moradabad where Lahiri's friends doubted his story, and Babaji appeared in the room to convince them.

Nothing in the documentary record outside the lineage confirms any of this, and it is reported here as the lineage's own account. What can be said with confidence is that a Bengali clerk began, from the early 1860s, to teach a specific set of yogic techniques in Benares, that he said they came from a teacher in the Himalayas, and that he called that teacher Babaji.

He went on working. For the next quarter century he kept office hours, raised his children and taught in the evenings and on holidays. His wife became his disciple. He retired on a pension in 1886 and spent his last nine years at his house in Benares. Yogananda describes him in this period sitting in the lotus posture in a small front parlour, rarely leaving it, while a steady stream of visitors came for *darshan*, the sight and blessing of a holy person. He held regular sessions on the Bhagavad Gita, which his circle called the Gita Assembly.

He initiated people of very different backgrounds. Yogananda names a Muslim disciple, Abdul Gufoor Khan, and says Lahiri initiated Hindus, Muslims and Christians and tried to loosen caste prejudice. His circle included monks and men with families. Some of the names matter for the later history. Panchanan Bhattacharya (1853–1919) came to him as a wandering monk; in the Kriya Yoga literature Lahiri agreed to initiate him only if he gave up his vows and went back to household life, which he did. Around 1885 or 1886 (sources differ) Lahiri allowed Panchanan to open the Arya Mission Institution in Calcutta, which sold cheap editions of the Gita and yogic herbal medicines. Priyanath Karar, later Swami Sri Yukteswar, received initiation in 1884. Bhagabati Charan Ghosh, a railway official, and his wife were initiates too, and their son Mukunda would become Paramahansa Yogananda. The organisation Yogananda founded says that when the mother brought the infant to Lahiri, he told her, "Little mother, thy son will be a yogi."

He discouraged publicity. Yogananda reports that he refused to let disciples spread his name, rejected the title Kashi Baba, and used neither organisations nor the press. "Let the fragrance of the Kriya flower be wafted naturally, without any display," he is quoted as saying. He also disliked being photographed. One photograph of him survives, and the story of how it was taken opens Yogananda's book. That image later appeared among the crowd of faces on the cover of the Beatles' *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* in 1967.

He died in Benares on 26 September 1895, four days short of his sixty-seventh birthday. The Kriya Yoga Institute's biography says it was the day of Mahashtami, during the autumn Durga festival. Yogananda's book reports that several disciples in different places said he appeared to them after his death. His sons and their descendants carried on a family line of Kriya teaching in Benares.

<aside class="note"><strong>Kriya.</strong> The Sanskrit word means action or rite. Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* (2.1) use *kriya-yoga* for a preliminary discipline of austerity, study and devotion to the Lord. Lahiri's Kriya Yoga is a different thing that shares the name, a set of breathing and concentration techniques passed on by initiation.</aside>

## The teaching

Lahiri Mahasaya taught a practice and said little about doctrine. The sayings his disciples kept point the same way. "Solve all your problems through meditation," he told them, according to Yogananda. "Exchange unprofitable religious speculations for actual God-contact." The one he repeated most often, in Hindi, was *banat, banat, ban jai*, which Yogananda renders as "Striving, striving, one day behold! The Divine Goal!" The phrase describes a process. You do the practice, and the result builds up on its own.

The practice itself is breath. In the lineage's description, the practitioner draws the breath and attention up and down the spine, through the six centres (*chakras*) that tantric physiology places along it, from the base of the spine to the point between the eyebrows. The aim is to calm the breath and with it the mind, until breathing becomes very slight or stops of its own accord and attention rests in what Lahiri called the *Kutastha*, the unchanging point between the brows. In his commentaries this stillness is the state the Gita describes. Yogananda later read Gita 4.29, which speaks of offering the inbreath into the outbreath and the outbreath into the inbreath, as a description of exactly this technique.

Three things about the teaching stand out.

**It is graded and transmitted.** Lahiri divided Kriya into four stages, a first Kriya and three higher ones, and gave the higher stages only after a disciple had made progress with the first. Each stage was taught in person, at initiation, and kept confidential. Written descriptions from the lineages agree on some components: *mahamudra*, a seated stretch; *khechari mudra*, in which the tongue is turned back and up into the cavity behind the soft palate; the breathing itself; and *yoni mudra*, closing the senses with the fingers to watch the inner light. The higher Kriyas include a practice called *thokar*. Beyond this, accounts differ, partly because the lineages that came after him do not agree on what he taught at each stage.

**It is meant for people with jobs.** In the Babaji story the whole point of Lahiri's life is that he stayed in the world. He kept his post, his wife and his children. The technique he gave was short enough to fit around a working day. When Panchanan Bhattacharya came to him in the robes of a monk, Lahiri sent him back to family life before teaching him. A practice that had been associated with renunciants in caves was offered to clerks, shopkeepers and their wives.

**It is open across caste and creed.** Lahiri was a Brahmin, and he gave initiation to non-Brahmins and to Muslims and Christians. In the lineage's view Kriya is a technique that works on the body and the breath, so the practitioner's birth or creed is beside the point. The disciples who recorded his Gita commentaries present the scriptures as maps of inner experience, where battles, gods and sacrifices are stages in the practice. His disciples recorded and arranged his commentaries on twenty-six texts, including the Gita and the Upanishads, and the Arya Mission Institution published many of them.

This is where Lahiri belongs in a part of this book that asks whether liberation can be a technology. He comes close to saying yes. A specific procedure, done correctly and regularly, produces a specific change in the breath and the mind, and this change is what the scriptures were describing all along. The guru's role is to transmit the procedure accurately and supervise progress. Belief is secondary. Yet the procedure is still given only by initiation, from a teacher with authority to give it, and the authority traces back to a Himalayan master whom only the lineage has seen. Lahiri's technology came wrapped in a guru tradition, and he never tried to separate the two.

<aside class="note"><strong>The diaries.</strong> Lahiri kept private notebooks in which he recorded his practice and realisations. His grandson Satya Charan Lahiri held them and later passed them to Ashoke Kumar Chatterjee, who drew on them for <i>Purana Purusha</i>, published in French in 1997 and in English in 2000. Twenty-six diaries are mentioned. They have not, as far as I could find, been published in full or examined by independent scholars.</aside>

I find it hard to name a single technique of his that cannot be found in earlier yoga manuals. What I cannot find before him is the combination: a short, ordered sequence of those techniques, handed by a working clerk to other working people as their whole spiritual path, with the claim that it alone was enough. That combination is what Yogananda later carried abroad, and much of what the West came to call meditation descends from that choice of audience. His doctrine stays close to tradition, and what changed was his answer to the question of who should receive it.

## Lineage

Lahiri Mahasaya is the first figure in the Kriya Yoga line who appears in the historical record. Above him stands Babaji, known only through the accounts of Lahiri and his disciples. Below him the line branches several ways, through Yukteswar to Yogananda, through Panchanan Bhattacharya, and through his own family.

<MiniTree id="lahiri-mahasaya" />

The edge from Babaji to Lahiri is drawn as hagiographic, because no evidence outside the lineage confirms the meeting or that Babaji existed. The edges from Lahiri to Yukteswar (initiated 1884) and to Panchanan Bhattacharya are documented in several independent lineage sources and in Yogananda's account, which was written within living memory of both men. The edge to Yogananda is testimony. Lahiri blessed him as an infant and initiated his parents, but died when the boy was not yet three, and Yogananda's training came through Yukteswar. The edge from the hatha and Nath yoga root records that the techniques themselves, *khechari mudra*, *mahamudra*, breath retention and the spinal centres, are named in earlier hatha texts. Trailanga Swami, the long-lived naked ascetic of Benares, lived in the same city at the same time and Yogananda's book reports that he admired Lahiri, but no teaching passed between them that I can document, so the tree draws no edge.

## The practice

The following is the outline that the lineages describe publicly. The techniques themselves are taught only at initiation, and the details vary between lines.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Initiation.</strong> A teacher authorised in the line gives the first Kriya in person. Lahiri insisted that the method be learned from a teacher, from the start.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Preparation.</strong> A seated posture with a straight spine, and <i>mahamudra</i>, a stretch with the breath held, to settle the body.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Kriya pranayama.</strong> Slow breaths with the attention travelling up the spine on the inhalation and down on the exhalation, through the six centres, repeated a set number of times.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Khechari mudra.</strong> The tongue turned back toward the soft palate, practised gradually over months. The lineages treat it as a requirement for the higher Kriyas.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Stillness.</strong> After the breathing, the practitioner sits with attention at the point between the eyebrows and lets the breath grow quiet. <i>Yoni mudra</i>, closing the eyes, ears and nostrils with the fingers, is used to watch the inner light.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Higher Kriyas.</strong> Three further initiations, given when the teacher judges the first has borne fruit, which extend the work on the breath and the spinal centres.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

Lahiri Mahasaya died before sound recording reached India, and no film of him exists. One photograph survives, the one Yogananda's book tells the story of. In that story an amateur photographer, Ganga Dhar Babu, found the master missing from twelve plates in a row, and Lahiri told him, "I am Spirit. Can your camera reflect the omnipresent Invisible?" He sat for the next attempt, which came out clearly, and never again. His words reach us through his disciples: the commentaries they recorded, his letters, the sayings in Yogananda's book and the diaries published in part by Chatterjee. ## Critiques and questions

**Babaji.** Almost everything known about Babaji comes from Lahiri, his disciples and Yogananda. Several of them reported meeting him: Yukteswar at the Allahabad Kumbh Mela of 1894, Pranabananda at Lahiri's house, Keshavananda near Badrinath. Accounts of his age range from about five hundred years, in Pranabananda's report, to claims that he lived in the third century or met Jesus. No source outside the lineage records him. Later groups, such as the Kriya Babaji Sangah founded in 1952 by V. T. Neelakantan and S. A. A. Ramaiah, give him a different biography. A historian can treat Babaji as the lineage's account of where Lahiri's knowledge came from, and as a figure in a long Indian tradition of hidden masters, and can say no more than that.

**Lahiri's self-presentation.** Lahiri wrote no autobiography and published nothing under his own name. The first widely read account of his life, with its miracles, appeared in English in 1946, half a century after his death, written by a man who had known him only as an infant. Yogananda's book attributes to Lahiri a prediction that an account of his life would be written about fifty years after his death because of interest in yoga in the West. Devotees take the book's appearance as the prediction's fulfilment; a sceptical reader may suspect the prediction was shaped by hindsight, since it reaches us only through the book that fulfils it. Bengali biographies by his disciples and descendants are closer to the source, but they too are devotional.

**The diaries.** Chatterjee's books based on the diaries present a Lahiri more critical of ritual and image worship than Yogananda's, and some of their readers draw conclusions about Babaji's nature that differ from Yogananda's. Because the originals have not been published in facsimile or edited by scholars, it is not possible to check the translations or how passages were selected. Scholarship on Lahiri as a historical figure is thin. Most academic work on Kriya Yoga deals with Yogananda. David Gordon White, for one, notes that Yogananda took the name Kriya Yoga for his own synthesis and that Patanjali is largely absent from his writings.

**Which Kriya is the real one.** Lineages descending from Lahiri disagree about what he taught. The order and number of techniques, whether khechari mudra is required, and what Yogananda added or changed (Self-Realization Fellowship teaches preparatory techniques of its own before Kriya) are all disputed. Each line claims faithfulness to Lahiri. With nothing written down by him in public and every stage taught in secret, there is no neutral text to settle the question.

## Descendants

The largest branch runs through **Sri Yukteswar** to **Paramahansa Yogananda**, who took Kriya to the United States in 1920 and founded Self-Realization Fellowship and, in India, the Yogoda Satsanga Society. Through Yogananda's book Lahiri became known worldwide. **Panchanan Bhattacharya** and the Arya Mission Institution carried a separate Bengali line, and **Bhupendranath Sanyal**, **Swami Pranabananda**, **Swami Kebalananda** and **Keshavananda Brahmachari** each passed on Kriya to students of their own. His sons Tinkori and Dukori Lahiri, his grandson Satya Charan Lahiri and his great-grandson Shibendu Lahiri kept a family line of teaching in Benares. Later teachers such as Swami Hariharananda Giri, and organisations including Ananda and the Kriya Yoga Institute, descend from one or more of these branches. Teachers outside the line who say they met Babaji, among them Sri M, also lean on the figure Lahiri introduced.
