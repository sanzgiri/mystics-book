<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> His devotees called him Poonjaji or Papaji, "dear father." He had no monastic name and never took formal vows.</aside>

<p>Lyallpur, in western Punjab, 1944. A former army officer, the father of two children, is at home after years of travelling around India asking holy men the same question: can you show me God? He has spent most of his life repeating the name of Krishna, aiming at one repetition for every breath, which he reckoned at about twenty-four thousand a day, and he has seen Krishna in visions since childhood. What he wants now is to see God at will.</p>
<p>A wandering sadhu comes to the door. Poonja invites him in and asks his question. The sadhu says there is a man in the south, at a place called Tiruvannamalai, who can help. His name is Ramana Maharshi.</p>
<p>When Poonja reaches the ashram and sees Ramana on his couch, he is angry. He is sure this is the same sadhu who visited him in Punjab, and he thinks he has been tricked into a long journey by a showman. He walks up and asks, "Are you the man who came to my house?" Ramana says nothing.</p>

## Life

Hariwansh Lal Poonja was born on 13 October 1910 in Gujranwala, in the western Punjab, now in Pakistan, into a Saraswat Brahmin family. Some later documents give his birth year as 1913. His mother was a sister of Swami Rama Tirtha, the mathematics teacher turned monk who had lectured on Vedanta in America in the early 1900s and drowned in a Himalayan river in 1906, four years before his nephew was born.

As a small boy he slipped into a state he later said was the same peace he found with Ramana. In his own account it overwhelmed him so completely that for some time he could not respond to the people around him. The age he gives varies between six and eight, and some accounts place it in Lahore. His mother, a devout worshipper of Krishna, told him that if he wanted the experience back he should love Krishna. He did, with the intensity he brought to everything, and he began to see Krishna in visions, as a boy he played with.

He grew up, married, had a son and a daughter, and became an officer in the British Indian Army. He resigned to look for God. For years he travelled through India, visiting ashrams and asking teachers whether they could show him God. None could. In 1944 the sadhu came to his door in Lyallpur, and he went south.

The first meeting went badly. Ramana would not confirm that he had visited Punjab. When Poonja asked whether Ramana had seen God and could show God to him, Ramana told him that God could not be seen as an object, since God is the seer, and that he should find out who the seer was. Poonja left disappointed and spent the next days in his room with his visions of Krishna, who came as usual.

He came back. He told Ramana that his practice had stopped working. For twenty-five years he had repeated Krishna's name, and now he could not repeat it at all. Ramana asked how he had come to the ashram. By train, then by bullock cart, Poonja said. Ramana pointed out that when he arrived he had got down from the cart and left it behind. His practices had brought him to where he was, and now they had done their job. Then Ramana looked at him in silence. In Poonja's account, his search ended in that look. "The silent gaze of the Maharshi established me in that primal state that was permanent," he wrote.

For the next three years he worked in Madras and visited Ramana whenever he could. When Partition came in 1947 and his family in western Punjab was in danger, Ramana sent him north to bring them across the new border. He moved them to Lucknow. As he left, Ramana told him, "I am with you wherever you are" (in another version, "wherever you go"). He did not see Ramana again. Ramana died in April 1950.

The next decades were spent earning a living. Through a chance meeting in Bangalore he became administrative manager of mining operations owned by the industrialist Krishna Lal Poddar, in the forests of Karnataka and later Goa. He supervised the workers, paid wages and arranged the transport of ore to the ports, and he did this for thirteen years. Seekers found him there too. In 1953 he met the French Benedictine monk Henri Le Saux, known in India as Swami Abhishiktananda, who had been shaken by his own meeting with Ramana in 1949. Abhishiktananda never became his disciple, but recorded that the meeting had a deep effect on him.

<aside class="note"><strong>Abhishiktananda</strong> (1910–1973) spent his life trying to bring Christian contemplation and Advaita together. He appears on the tree as a branch from both Ramana and Papaji.</aside>

Papaji retired in 1966 and spent long periods in the Himalayan foothills near Haridwar and Rishikesh, sitting by the Ganges and receiving whoever came. In the late 1960s he formed a second family with a Belgian disciple, Ganga Mira, with whom he had a daughter, Mukti. In the 1970s and 1980s a slow trickle of Western visitors found him.

In his last decade the trickle became a flood. From the late 1980s until his death, Westerners came to Lucknow by the hundreds, then the thousands, among them many former followers of Osho. Papaji held *satsang*, open sessions of questions and answers, every morning. Visitors read out letters, described their experiences, wept and laughed, and Papaji answered, joked, told stories and sometimes sent them away with a word. Ram Dass came, as did the Buddhist teacher Christopher Titmuss. The English writer David Godman moved to Lucknow in 1992 or 1993 to record his life and stayed until the end.

He died in Lucknow on 6 September 1997, aged eighty-six or eighty-three depending on which birth year is right.

## The teaching

Papaji taught one thing over and over: freedom is already present, and the effort to reach it is what hides it. "To be bound takes effort," he said. "To be Free takes no effort."

His instruction for realizing this was even shorter than Ramana's. *Keep quiet.* By this he meant something more specific than silence of the tongue. He meant letting the mind come to rest without any thought, intention or effort, for an instant. In that instant, he said, what you are is plain. "Be quiet, don't think, don't make effort." Most of his satsangs circled around these few words, approached from a hundred directions.

He agreed with Ramana on the essentials. The world is an appearance in consciousness, the separate self is a mistaken idea, and inquiry into the source of the "I" thought dissolves it. Where he differed was in emphasis and speed. Ramana had told visitors to keep up the inquiry, sometimes for years, until it became natural. Papaji often told people that a single honest moment of looking was enough, and that further practice only gave the ego something to do. For him, other spiritual practices mainly kept the seeker busy.

He also held that a realized teacher was needed, a point his Western admirers often forget. Asked directly whether one was necessary, he answered, "Absolutely!" He spoke of an intense desire for freedom as the force that brings a seeker to that teacher, and in his own life the decisive event was a look from Ramana.

<aside class="note"><strong>Satsang.</strong> Literally "company of truth." Traditionally any gathering of seekers around a teacher, often with singing. In Lucknow it came to mean a public dialogue with an audience, the form most Western non-duality teachers now use.</aside>

### The devotee who stayed a devotee

The image of Papaji as a pure non-dualist leaves out a great deal. He went on speaking of Krishna with affection all his life, said that he still saw him, and told the story of watching Ramana weep over a picture of Krishna as proof that Ramana too was a lover of God. He had no difficulty holding that nothing ever happens and that Krishna still came to play. When asked about the contradiction, he laughed.

Papaji's importance lies in transmission more than in ideas. Everything he said can be found in Ramana. What he added was a setting in which thousands of Westerners heard it in their own language, from a warm and funny old man who told them they were already free, and then sent many of them home to tell others the same thing. The modern Western satsang circuit, with its retreats, its online sessions and its teachers who speak of awakening as something that can happen in an afternoon, descends largely from his house in Lucknow.

## Lineage

Papaji's lineage is short and clear. His only guru was Ramana, and he took no other initiation. Behind him stand his mother's Krishna devotion and, through her, the Neo-Vedanta of her brother Rama Tirtha.

<MiniTree id="papaji" />

The branches below him are many. **Gangaji**, an American who met him in 1990, became one of the best-known teachers in the West. **Mooji**, born in Jamaica and living in London, met him in 1993 and now runs a large ashram in Portugal. **Eli Jaxon-Bear**, **Isaac Shapiro**, **Catherine Ingram** and others also taught after time with him. On the tree these appear together as Western Neo-Advaita. **Andrew Cohen** met him in 1986 and began teaching soon after, then broke with him; that split is shown separately because it became the sharpest public argument about what Papaji had actually authorised.

## The practice

He disliked the word. When pressed, he described something like this.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Want freedom more than anything else.</strong> He spoke of this desire as what brings a seeker to the teacher in the first place.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>For one moment, do not think.</strong> Make no effort, follow no thought, set no intention. Just stop.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Notice what is present when nothing is being done.</strong> He said this was what you are, and that it had always been here.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>When a thought comes, ask who it comes to,</strong> as Ramana taught, and return to quiet.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Do not make this into a new practice.</strong> He warned that turning "keep quiet" into a technique would bring the seeker straight back.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Andrew Cohen.** Cohen met Papaji in 1986, said that Papaji had declared him his heir, and began teaching. Within a few years he had come to believe that awakening must show itself in flawless behaviour, and that Papaji's conduct fell short of that. Papaji, for his part, said he had never given Cohen permission to teach, that Cohen had spent only a short time with him, and that he regarded him as a messenger at most, never a master or an heir. Cohen later faced serious accusations of abuse from his own students. The quarrel became a public test of a question the satsang movement still has not settled: what, if anything, awakening should change about how a person lives.

**Instant awakening.** Critics argue that Papaji's insistence on a single moment of recognition, together with his readiness to send people out to teach, produced a generation of teachers with very little grounding. The descendants of Lucknow range widely in depth.

**His own life.** In the late 1960s Papaji formed a second family while his first was still living. He taught that realization has nothing to do with outward conduct, a view his defenders call honest and his critics call convenient.

**What he authorised.** Many Western teachers describe themselves as authorised by Papaji. He gave such blessings freely and informally, often in a single conversation, and he left no list. It is usually impossible to check any particular claim.

## Descendants

His descendants are the Western non-duality movement in its most popular form. **Gangaji**, **Mooji**, **Eli Jaxon-Bear**, **Isaac Shapiro** and many others teach in the satsang format he established. **David Godman**, already a historian of Ramana's circle, became his biographer. Through **Abhishiktananda** his influence also reached the Christian contemplative movement in India. Papaji left no organisation and no named successor, and the Avadhuta Foundation keeps his books and recordings in print.
