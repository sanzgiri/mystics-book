## Life

Ayyappan, called Kunjan by his family, was born on 25 August 1853 near Thiruvananthapuram, at Kannammoola according to most sources and at Kollur according to others. His family belonged to the Nair community, which the Brahmin order of the time ranked as Shudra. That ranking mattered for what he later wrote. A Shudra boy could not be admitted to a Brahmin school of Sanskrit, and the commentator Ajay Sekher writes that the young Kunjan learned some of his first Sanskrit by listening to Vedic chanting from outside one.

He went to the village school of Pettayil Raman Pillai Asan, where he was made the class monitor, *chattampi* in Malayalam. The nickname stuck for life, and he is known by it still, with the honorific *Swamikal* added. As a young man he earned a living as a labourer, a document writer, an accountant and a government clerk, and he attended the gatherings of a Thiruvananthapuram circle of scholars where he heard, among others, the Tamil scholar Manonmaniyam Sundaram Pillai and the yogi **Thycaud Ayyavu**. Ayyavu taught him hatha yoga and Shaiva mantras. His devotion to Murugan, the Tamil god also called Subrahmanya or Shanmukha, earned him the name Shanmukhadasa, "servant of Shanmukha."

He spent long years wandering in the Tamil country, studying Tamil literature, music, painting and the medical and yogic lore of the Siddhas. His followers say that during these years he reached realization; I have not been able to confirm the details of the account <em>[to verify]</em>. On one of his journeys, at a physician's house in Perunnelli, he met the young Ezhava teacher Nanu Asan, later **Narayana Guru**. Chattampi introduced him to Ayyavu, and the two became close friends. Kerala Tourism records that they meditated together on Maruthwamala. The friendship lasted until Chattampi's death.

He founded no ashram and no organisation. Disciples gathered around him as he travelled, and he lived among them without a fixed home. He broke caste rules openly, staying and eating in the houses of so-called untouchables, and his friendship with Ezhava physicians in Kochi brought him into the circle around the reformer Sahodaran Ayyappan. He died on 5 May 1924 at Panmana, north of Kollam, where his samadhi shrine stands. Narayana Guru wrote a verse in his memory. India issued a postage stamp in his honour in 2014.

## The teaching

Chattampi Swamikal was a scholar first. He wrote some fifteen books in Malayalam on religion, language and history, and most of them argue one point from different directions: that the exclusive claims of the Brahmin order of Kerala had no basis in scripture or history.

**The Vedas belong to all.** His best-known work, *Vedadhikara Nirupanam*, "An Inquiry into the Right to the Vedas," takes on the rule that only the twice-born castes, and above all Brahmins, may study the Vedas. He argued from the scriptures themselves that this restriction was a later social custom. For a Nair writing in the 1890s and 1900s this was a direct challenge to the religious authority of the Namboodiri Brahmins who ran Kerala's temples and owned much of its land.

**History against legend.** In *Pracheena Malayalam*, "Ancient Malayalam," and related writings on Tamil and Dravidian history, he went after the founding legend of Kerala's Brahmin order, the story that the sage Parashurama created the land and gave it to Brahmins. He held that Kerala's culture and language came from an older Tamil and Dravidian base.

**Non-violence to all beings.** *Jeevakarunya Nirupanam*, "On Compassion to Living Beings," is a sustained argument for vegetarianism and against animal sacrifice, still common in Kerala's village shrines at the time. He is said to have shared his food with dogs.

**Against missionary Christianity.** *Kristumata Chedanam* is a polemic against the Christianity preached by the missionaries of his day, who were making converts among Kerala's lower castes. The book defends Hindu thought against their attacks.

His Vedanta was Advaita, and his ethics followed from it in the same way as his friend's: if all beings are one, cruelty to animals and contempt for other castes have no defence. Unlike Narayana Guru he did not consecrate temples or found institutions. His influence travelled through his books, his disciples and his example.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="chattampi-swamikal" />

Chattampi Swamikal and Narayana Guru learned from the same teacher, Thycaud Ayyavu, and the tree places them side by side. Some of Chattampi's admirers have called him Narayana Guru's guru; Narayana Guru's followers describe them as friends and fellow seekers, and that is how the tree shows them. His own disciples included **Theerthapada Paramahamsa**, who carried his work forward in central Kerala <em>[to verify]</em>. His legacy is claimed today by several groups, including the Nair Service Society, Hindu organisations and Dravidian and anti-caste writers. Ajay Sekher has called it a "mixed legacy," pointing to the same writings that challenge Brahmin authority and, elsewhere, restore the authority of the Vedas.
