## Life

Helena Petrovna Hahn was born in Yekaterinoslav in 1831 and raised partly by her grandparents in Saratov after her mother's death in 1842. She married Nikifor Blavatsky in 1849 and soon left him. Her own account of the years that followed, including claimed study in India and Tibet, is largely uncorroborated, and Britannica presents it as her claim. She arrived in New York on 8 July 1873. In 1874 she met Henry Steel Olcott, a journalist who had served in the Northern army and was reporting on spiritualist phenomena. On 17 November 1875 the two, with William Quan Judge and others, formed the Theosophical Society, which called itself an unsectarian body of seekers after Truth. Blavatsky's *Isis Unveiled* followed in 1877 and presented an ancient wisdom said to underlie all religions.

The founders left New York in December 1878 and reached Bombay on 16 February 1879. The Society had already entered an alliance with Dayananda Saraswati's Arya Samaj on 22 May 1878, and for a time it was known as the Theosophical Society of Aryavarta. Dayananda ended the union with a public attack in March 1882. Blavatsky began the journal *The Theosophist* in October 1879. In 1882 the founders bought the Adyar estate near Madras, which has remained the international headquarters. Blavatsky left India in March 1885 for health reasons and was based in London from 1887, where she wrote *The Secret Doctrine* (1888), which describes cosmic evolution through the Stanzas of Dzyan and seven Root Races. Scholars generally think the Stanzas are her own composition. She died in London on 8 May 1891, and Theosophists remember the day as White Lotus Day. Gandhi, who had visited her in 1889, became an associate member of her lodge in March 1891.

Olcott's story in the east runs through Ceylon. He and Blavatsky landed at Galle on 17 May 1880 and took the Five Precepts on 19 May at the Vijayananda Vihara at Weliwatta, a ceremony often described as the first formal conversion of Westerners to Buddhism. The Buddhist Theosophical Society was founded in Colombo on 17 June 1880. His *Buddhist Catechism*, published in Sinhala and English on 24 July 1881 with the monk Hikkaduwe Sumangala's endorsement, is still used in Sri Lanka. In 1885 he advised the committee that designed the Buddhist flag, and the Society helped found Ananda College (1886), Dharmaraja College (1887), Maliyadeva College (1888) and Mahinda College (1892). At the Adyar conference of January 1891 he put forward fourteen fundamental Buddhist beliefs meant to be accepted by both the northern and southern traditions. In Madras he founded free schools for outcaste children, the first of which, the Olcott Free School, opened in June 1894 and charged no fees. The young Anagarika Dharmapala was his co-worker and, later, his translator. Olcott died at Adyar on 17 February 1907.

## Why this figure appears here

Part 11 asks whether the Buddha can come home. The Buddhist revival in Ceylon owes a good deal to the colonel, and Dharmapala, whose chapter sits in this part, began there. The pair also bear on to Part 2's question of whether all religion is one, because the Society's first object was a brotherhood without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste or colour.

The inheritance was Western. Blavatsky drew on Neoplatonism, Kabbalah, Gnosticism and spiritualism, and added a hidden hierarchy of Masters said to guide human evolution. She also claimed Hindu and Tibetan teachers, which gave the system an Eastern source. What the Society did in India was new. Two Westerners declared themselves Buddhists in public, and Olcott then organised schools, a catechism and a flag around the declaration. The Arya Samaj connection gave the Society its first Indian base, and it broke within four years when the doctrines collided.

The tree runs from the Society to Annie Besant, Krishnamurti and Dharmapala. Olcott's influence on Dharmapala is documented, from the Colombo lecture of 1880 onward. Blavatsky's line to Besant is documented too, from Besant's membership in May 1889. The sources read here do not settle how much of the Buddhist revival the Society started and how much it joined, since the movement already had Sri Lankan leaders such as Migettuwatte Gunananda, whose Panadura debate of 1873 inspired Olcott by the account of one Buddhist Publication Society essay.
