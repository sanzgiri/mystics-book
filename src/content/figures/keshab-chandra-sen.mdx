## Life

Keshab Chandra Sen was born in Calcutta on 19 November 1838 into a wealthy Baidya family. His grandfather, Ramkamal Sen, was a well-known scholar who had sided with the orthodox against Rammohan Roy. His father died when he was about ten, and an uncle raised him. He studied at the Hindu College, read English philosophy and the Bible, and in his teens ran an evening school and a debating society, the Goodwill Fraternity, where he first met Debendranath Tagore.

He joined the Brahmo Samaj in 1857 (some accounts say 1859) and within a year was Debendranath's closest helper. In 1862 Debendranath installed him as *acharya*, the minister who leads worship. Keshab was a gifted English speaker and he pushed the Samaj outward. He wanted Brahmos to give up caste marks, to allow marriage across castes and to let only men without the sacred thread lead services. Debendranath would not go so far. Keshab withdrew in February 1865, and in November 1866 (15 November in *Banglapedia*) he and the younger members founded the Brahmo Samaj of India. By 1872, *Banglapedia* records, it had about a hundred branches.

The same year he gave a lecture in Calcutta, *Jesus Christ: Europe and Asia*, which presented Christ as an Asian prophet whom Europe had misunderstood. Missionaries welcomed it and many Hindus suspected he was about to convert. He did not. In 1870 he spent about six months in England, from March to September, preaching to Unitarian and other congregations and meeting Queen Victoria. Back in India he campaigned for a civil marriage law. Act III of 1872 allowed marriage outside Hindu rites, required monogamy and set minimum ages of fourteen for brides and eighteen for grooms.

In 1875 Ramakrishna came to see him at a garden house in Belgharia, and the two became friends. Keshab's papers made Ramakrishna known to educated Calcutta, and Keshab began to speak of God as Mother.

In 1878 Keshab's daughter Suniti, thirteen or fourteen years old by different accounts and below the age his own law had set, was married to the young Maharaja of Cooch Behar, with Hindu rites performed by Brahmin priests. Keshab defended the match as providential. Many of his followers saw a betrayal of everything he had campaigned for. Led by Sivanath Sastri and Ananda Mohan Bose, they left and founded the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj on 15 May 1878.

With those who stayed, Keshab proclaimed the Nava Vidhan, the New Dispensation, in January 1880; some sources date its formal institution to 1881. In the four years left to him he gave it a calendar of rites. He assigned four colleagues to study Hinduism, Christianity, Islam and Buddhism. He led his followers in devotional exercises he described as pilgrimages to the prophets and saints of many traditions. He adapted the Christian Eucharist using rice and water and a baptism that invoked Varuna, and held Vedic-style fire rites. Britannica describes his missionaries preaching under a flag that combined a crescent, a cross and a trident. He died in Calcutta on 8 January 1884, aged forty-five.

## The teaching

Keshab kept the Brahmo core: one formless God, no images, no priestly mediation. He added two claims. The first was that God speaks directly and continuously to the conscience of the believer, so that inspiration is a living source of religious knowledge. The second was that all religions are true, each holding part of a single revelation, so that the scriptures and prophets of the world are mutually complementary. *Banglapedia* summarises his view in his own repeated phrase that "all religions are true."

That changed the answer to this part's question. For Rammohan and Debendranath, reason or the pure heart sifted the Vedas. For Keshab the Vedas became one scripture among many, read together with the Gospels, the Quran, the teachings of the Buddha and the songs of the bhakti saints, and revelation did not stop with any of them. It continued in the present, through inspired people, and his church was its newest form. Christ stood near the centre of it, as a prophet of Asia whose teaching India could claim as its own. Keshab never became a Christian and did not accept the churches' doctrines about Christ.

He also brought feeling back into Brahmo worship. He drew on Bengali bhakti, and after 1875 on Ramakrishna, whose influence his colleague P. C. Mozoomdar credited for Keshab's teaching of the Motherhood of God. He spoke of yoga and renunciation as Brahmo disciplines and of his own work as a dispensation from God.

Critics in his own lifetime saw a contradiction. Shandip Saha, reviewing John A. Stevens's biography, notes that Keshab's claim to unique access to divine inspiration undercut his message that inspiration was open to all. The Cooch Behar marriage, defended as God's will, made the problem public.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="keshab-chandra-sen" />

The edge from Debendranath Tagore is documented from both sides: Keshab joined his Samaj, served as his minister from 1862 and broke with him in 1865–66. The tie with Ramakrishna, from 1875, rests on the testimony of both circles, and how much each gave the other is still argued over. Keshab advised Dayananda Saraswati in Calcutta in 1873 to lecture in Hindi, and the young Narendranath Datta, later Vivekananda, attended his services around 1880 before joining the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj. Stevens argues that the Ramakrishna movement and Visva-Bharati both played down how much their founders owed him.
