<p>The film was shot in Mysore in 1938 with money from the Maharaja. It is silent and black and white, and the copy that survives runs to about fifty-seven minutes. A man of about fifty, bare-chested, in a short white cloth, moves from one posture to the next on a plain floor. He lifts his body on his hands, folds it, swings his legs through his arms and lands in the next shape without a pause. Then a slim young man, his wife's brother, B.K.S. Iyengar, does the same.</p>
<p>The camera stays close and the pace is slow. Each posture is joined to the one before by a set of small movements that repeat, so that the sequence looks less like a list of poses and more like a single exercise. The Smithsonian, which showed the film in its 2013 exhibition on yoga, describes it as perhaps the earliest footage of linked postures.</p>
<p>The Maharaja wanted the film shown across India. Yoga in the palace was competing with Western gymnastics and bodybuilding for the attention of young men, and this was his teacher's answer.</p>

## Life

Krishnamacharya was born on 18 November 1888 at Muchukundapura, a village in the Chitradurga district of what was then the princely state of Mysore. His family were Telugu-speaking Srivaishnava Brahmins, followers of the god Vishnu in the tradition of the philosopher Ramanuja. His father, Tirumalai Srinivasa Tatacharya, was a teacher of the Vedas and taught him Sanskrit and Vedic chanting. He was the eldest of six children. His father died when he was about ten, and at twelve he moved to Mysore, where his great-grandfather headed the Parakala Matha, a Srivaishnava monastery.

He spent the next twenty years as a student of the classical Sanskrit learning. He went to Banaras around 1906 and studied logic and grammar there. He attended Queen's College in Banaras and later studied at Patna, and he said he took examinations in all six schools of Indian philosophy, along with Ayurveda, the traditional medicine. His family and later biographers list titles he received for this learning, such as *Samkhya-yoga-shikhamani* and *Mimamsa-ratna*. Much of this record rests on his own account and on the biographies written by his son T.K.V. Desikachar and his student A.G. Mohan.

### Ramamohana Brahmachari

The most important claim in his story is the one hardest to check. In *Yoga Makaranda*, published in 1934, Krishnamacharya wrote that the scholar Ganganath Jha advised him to seek out a yogi named Ramamohana Brahmachari, who was said to have mastered thousands of postures. The fuller version, told by his son and by Mohan, goes on from there. He needed the Viceroy's permission to travel, so he first spent six months in Simla teaching yoga to the Viceroy, who had diabetes. The Viceroy then arranged his journey. After about two and a half months on foot he reached his teacher's home, which the accounts place in a cave near Mount Kailash in Tibet, or at Muktinath in Nepal. He stayed seven and a half years. He learned the *Yoga Sutras*, postures, breath control and the use of yoga to treat illness. When he asked what he owed, his teacher told him to go home, marry, raise a family and teach yoga.

No independent record of Ramamohana Brahmachari has been found. The story's details also strain against each other. The Viceroy named in the accounts is Lord Irwin, who held office from 1926 to 1931, while the journey is usually dated to about 1919. Mark Singleton, the historian of modern yoga, notes that Krishnamacharya gave different versions of his life at different times, sometimes dropping stories and sometimes adding new ones.

### The Yoga Korunta

A second claim concerns a book. According to the version told in the Ashtanga yoga community, Ramamohana Brahmachari taught Krishnamacharya a text called the *Yoga Korunta*, attributed to a sage named Vamana, and Krishnamacharya learned it by heart. When they parted, his teacher asked him to find a written copy. He is said to have found one in a library in Calcutta, badly damaged by ants, and it was later lost. The text is said to have described sequences of postures linked by breath, which is the system K. Pattabhi Jois later taught as Ashtanga yoga.

No copy has ever been seen. One writer, Fernando Pagés Ruiz, reported hearing at least five conflicting accounts of how it was found. Singleton points out that Krishnamacharya did not cite the *Yoga Korunta* in *Yoga Makaranda*, his first book, and finds it surprising that no student made a copy. Others in the tradition reply that the text existed in Krishnamacharya's memory and was taught orally, which is how most Indian texts were passed on.

### Mysore

He returned south and married Namagiriamma in 1925. For a time he worked as a manager on a coffee plantation in Hassan district. His break came through the Maharaja of Mysore, Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV, a patron of the arts and physical culture. The sources date their meeting differently. One account has the Maharaja hearing of him in Banaras in 1926. Another places the decisive moment in 1931, when Krishnamacharya lectured on the Upanishads at the Mysore Town Hall and was invited to teach at the Sanskrit College. The Maharaja, who credited yoga with easing his own ailments, asked him to open a school. The yogashala opened on 11 August 1933 in a wing of the Jaganmohan Palace.

The room had been the palace gymnasium. Its ropes were still in place. Next door, by one account, K.V. Iyer ran a bodybuilding gym that many young men preferred. Krishnamacharya's job was to make yoga attractive to boys from the palace and the city, and to show it off. He and his students toured the region giving demonstrations. Accounts from the 1920s and 1930s, collected by Pagés Ruiz, describe him suspending his pulse, stopping cars with his hands and lifting heavy objects with his teeth.

His students in these years included the people who would carry his teaching around the world. **K. Pattabhi Jois** had first seen him demonstrate in Hassan in 1927, at twelve, and followed him to Mysore. **B.K.S. Iyengar**, his wife's younger brother, a sickly boy of fifteen, came to live with the family in 1934. In the late 1930s a European woman who had acted in Indian films under the name **Indra Devi** persuaded him to accept her as a student. She later taught yoga to Hollywood actors.

He was a hard teacher. Iyengar later described beatings and humiliation, and the historian Elliott Goldberg concludes that the treatment damaged him. Iyengar also said that the few days of real teaching he received "determined what I have become today."

The Maharaja died in 1940. His successor gave less support, and after Indian independence the state's new government cut funding. The yogashala closed in 1950.

### Madras

After a short time in Bangalore, Krishnamacharya moved in 1952 to Madras, at the invitation of a lawyer who wanted help recovering from a stroke. He taught at Vivekananda College and saw private students for the next thirty-seven years. His teaching changed. The vigorous jumping sequences of Mysore gave way to slow, careful practice designed for one person at a time, often someone ill or elderly. He taught chanting, breath control, meditation and the *Yoga Sutras* alongside the postures. His son, **T.K.V. Desikachar**, who had trained as an engineer, became his student and founded the Krishnamacharya Yoga Mandiram in Madras in 1976 to continue this work. Krishnamacharya died on 28 February 1989, aged 100.

## The teaching

Krishnamacharya wrote little and was not a philosopher in the usual sense. His teaching was a method, and it changed over his life. Three ideas run through it.

**Breath leads movement.** In Mysore he joined postures into sequences, with each movement made on an inhalation or an exhalation and each transition counted. He called this *vinyasa*, a Sanskrit word meaning "arrangement" or "placing in order." The breath was slow and audible, and the eyes rested on a fixed point. This made posture practice into a moving meditation and gave it a rhythm that could be taught to a whole class.

**Posture serves the rest of yoga.** He was a Sanskrit scholar before he was a gymnast, and he always placed the postures inside Patanjali's scheme of eight limbs: ethical restraints, observances, posture, breath control, withdrawal of the senses, concentration, meditation and absorption. For him the body work prepared the ground for breath control, and breath control prepared the mind. He was also a devout Vaishnava. Students in Madras recall him teaching that the end of yoga was devotion to God, and he chanted Vedic hymns daily.

**Fit the practice to the person.** In Madras he taught each student a different practice. A.G. Mohan records his principle as "Teach what is appropriate for an individual." Desikachar put it this way in 1995: "What makes my father's yoga teachings unique is his insistence on attending to each individual and to his or her uniqueness." The same man who had drilled Mysore schoolboys in fixed sequences spent his last decades designing breathing exercises for heart patients. He told students that yoga had always been adapted to time and place, and he adapted it himself.

<aside class="note"><strong>Vinyasa.</strong> In Krishnamacharya's usage, a counted sequence of movements linked to breath. In today's studios it often means any flowing class.</aside>

I find him the most surprising figure in this part. He was a traditional Brahmin scholar who never left India, wrote in Kannada and Sanskrit, and spent most of his life teaching a handful of students at a time. Through four or five of those students, his classroom in a palace gymnasium became the template for yoga studios in every city on earth. The question of this part, whether liberation can be a technology, would barely make sense without him, because he is the one who turned a set of postures into something you could teach like a drill and then, late in life, turned it back into something made for one person.

## Lineage

Krishnamacharya's own lineage rests on his account of Ramamohana Brahmachari, which no one has been able to confirm. What can be documented is the learning he acquired in Banaras and Patna, the court tradition of Mysore and the hatha yoga texts he cited.

His students spread in three directions. **K. Pattabhi Jois** kept the Mysore sequences and fixed them into Ashtanga vinyasa yoga. **B.K.S. Iyengar** slowed the postures down, held them longer and made precision and props his method. **T.K.V. Desikachar**, his son, carried on the Madras teaching of individual adaptation, which came to be called Viniyoga. **Indra Devi** took yoga to Shanghai, Hollywood and Argentina. Later students in Madras, including **Srivatsa Ramaswami** and **A.G. Mohan**, teach their own versions of his later method.

## The practice

The Madras teaching resists a single recipe, since every student received a different one. The principles his son and students describe can be put in steps.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Begin with the breath.</strong> Breathe slowly through the nose, with a slight narrowing of the throat so the breath can be heard. Let it set the pace.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Move on the breath.</strong> Enter and leave each posture in time with an inhalation or an exhalation, never holding the breath to force a position.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Fit the posture to the body.</strong> Change the form to suit age, strength and health. A posture that harms is the wrong posture.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Move from posture to breath control.</strong> After the postures, sit and practise regulated breathing, often with longer exhalations.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>End in stillness.</strong> Close with chanting, meditation or prayer, according to the student's faith and aim.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**The biography.** Most of what is known about his early life, including the Banaras degrees, the Viceroy and the years in the Himalaya, comes from his own statements and from books by his son and students. Some details conflict, and the chronology of the Viceroy story does not fit Lord Irwin's term of office. Admirers treat the account as the memory of an old teacher. Historians treat it as a story that gave a new practice an old pedigree.

**Sjoman and the palace.** In *The Yoga Tradition of the Mysore Palace* (1996), the Indologist Norman Sjoman, a longtime student of Iyengar, translated the yoga section of the *Sritattvanidhi*, a nineteenth-century illustrated compendium made for an earlier Maharaja, which shows 122 postures. He also drew attention to the *Vyayama Dipika*, a gymnastics manual used in the palace, and noted that Krishnamacharya taught in the old gymnasium. He argued that exercises from gymnastics shaped Krishnamacharya's syllabus and so the vinyasa style passed to Iyengar and Jois, and that the rope postures may come from Indian wrestling and pole gymnastics. Sjoman treated this as part of a living tradition of adaptation at the Mysore court.

**Singleton and physical culture.** Mark Singleton's *Yoga Body* (Oxford University Press, 2010) placed Krishnamacharya in the wider physical-culture movement of colonial India, alongside Swami Kuvalayananda and Shri Yogendra. He noted that some of the Mysore drills resemble sequences in the Danish gymnast Niels Bukh's widely translated *Primary Gymnastics*, and that the name "Ashtanga" in the Jois system may echo the eight-point prostration of Hindu worship. Singleton has been explicit about what he does not claim. In a later preface he wrote that he did not argue that postures were recently invented, or that Krishnamacharya plagiarised, and he preferred the words adaptation and reinterpretation. Some practitioners nevertheless read the book as an attack, and Singleton received hostile personal criticism. The reviewer Bernie Gourley called the thesis bold but unproven, arguing that it rests heavily on a few sources.

**The teacher.** His early students describe severity and sometimes violence. Iyengar's later accounts of being beaten and humiliated as a boy are the most detailed. These accounts come from people who also revered him, and they did not lead to any formal complaint.

## Descendants

Most posture-based yoga taught today descends from his Mysore students. Iyengar yoga and Ashtanga vinyasa yoga each claim millions of practitioners, and "vinyasa flow," the most common studio class, derives from the Ashtanga sequences. His son's Krishnamacharya Yoga Mandiram in Chennai and the Viniyoga schools in Europe and America carry the later, therapeutic teaching. Yoga therapy as a profession owes much to the Madras years.
