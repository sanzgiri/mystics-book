<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Anandamayi means "permeated with bliss." A devotee, Jyotish Chandra Roy, known as Bhaiji, gave her the name in Dhaka in the mid-1920s. She almost never said "I." She referred to herself as "this body," and her followers still call her simply Ma, "mother."</aside>

## Life

Nirmala Sundari was born on 30 April 1896 at Kheora, a village in the Tripura district of East Bengal, now in Bangladesh. Her parents, Bipin Bihari Bhattacharya and Mokshada Sundari Devi, were poor and pious Brahmins. Her mother, many years later, took the vows of a renunciant herself and, as Muktananda Giri, became one of her daughter's closest companions. Nirmala had only a little primary schooling.

The ashram's chronology records stories from her infancy and childhood, including a fit of ecstasy while listening to devotional singing at the age of two. Like all such stories, these come from the movement's own accounts, written down after she had become famous. What is clear from every source is that the child was often absent-minded and withdrawn, and that her family worried about her.

On 7 February 1909 (some sources say 1908), when she was not yet thirteen, she was married to Ramani Mohan Chakravarti. As was common, she did not live with him at first. She spent several years with his family at Sripur and then with her own parents, and joined him in 1914 (1915 in the ashram's chronology). They moved where his work took them, first to Ashtagram and then, in 1918, to Bajitpur.

Her biographers say the marriage was never consummated and that her husband accepted this. She did the housework, and by the chronology's account, around 1915 people in Ashtagram were already calling her Kushir Ma.

### The six years at Bajitpur

Between 1918 and 1924, at Bajitpur, she went through what her followers call her *lila* of *sadhana*, her play of spiritual practice. According to her own later accounts and her biographers, the practices of one tradition after another arose in her spontaneously, often at night after the household work: ritual worship, the repetition of divine names, yogic postures and long periods of trance. She said that no one taught her these things, and that each stopped of itself when it was complete.

The self-initiation of August 1922 was the turning point of this period. In December of that year, when a cousin of her husband asked who she was, she answered with a phrase from the Vaishnava and Vedantic vocabulary, *Purna Brahma Narayan*, roughly "the complete Absolute, God." Her husband asked for initiation. The ashram's chronology dates it to December 1922. Lisa Lassell Hallstrom, the scholar who has studied her most closely, dates it to 3 January 1923. She gave him a new name, Bholanath, a name of Shiva, and he kept it for the rest of his life.

From January 1923, according to Hallstrom, she kept silence for about three years. Visitors were already beginning to come.

### Dhaka

In 1924 Bholanath took a job on the Shahbagh garden estate of the Nawab of Dhaka, and they moved to Dhaka. (The ashram's own chronology places the move in April 1922, which does not fit with the events it places at Bajitpur later that year.) At Shahbagh she was often in deep trance, sometimes for hours, and Sanskrit hymns are said to have come from her in those states. Educated people from the city began to visit, lawyers, doctors and professors among them, and were struck by the short, plain answers she gave to the philosophical questions they brought.

Among them was Jyotish Chandra Roy, who became her second disciple after Bholanath. Ma's circle called him Bhaiji, "brother." He gave her the name Anandamayi, in 1925 by the ashram's chronology, and it became the name she was known by. Her devotees also found the site of an abandoned Kali temple at Siddheshwari, near Dhaka, which she said she had seen in a vision at Bajitpur, and they built a small ashram there. A larger one followed in 1929 at Ramna, inside the precinct of the Ramna Kali temple.

Two details from the Dhaka years became part of how she was remembered. She took part in the worship of Kali, the goddess at the centre of Bengali Shakta religion; at a Kali puja held at Shahbagh in 1925, the ashram's chronology records, a goat was sacrificed as custom required, and devotees reported as a wonder that no blood flowed. From 1929 she gave up non-vegetarian food. And at the shrine of a Muslim saint in Dhaka, her biographers report, she recited verses from the Qur'an, which she had never studied, to the surprise of the Muslims present.

From about 1926 her hands stopped serving her well at meals, and from then until her death she was fed by others, by Bholanath and later by a few women companions. Her followers understood this as part of her detachment from the body. Hallstrom records it as a fact of her daily life for more than fifty years.

### North India

In 1932 she left Bengal with Bholanath and Bhaiji and arrived in Dehradun, where she stayed in an old Shiva temple at Raipur. From the early 1930s until her death she travelled almost constantly across India, from ashram to ashram and from one devotee's house to the next.

In Dehradun in 1933 she met Kamala Nehru, the wife of Jawaharlal Nehru, who became one of her most devoted followers. Nehru himself, a professed unbeliever, met Ma on several occasions. Bhaiji died at Almora in 1937, after a journey with her to Mount Kailash. Bholanath died at the Kishenpur ashram in Dehradun on 6 May 1938. She nursed him through his last illness as a wife.

In the decades that followed, her circle grew to include much of the Indian establishment. The ashram's chronology records a stay with Mahatma Gandhi at Wardha in February 1942, with Subhas Chandra Bose in 1938, with S. Radhakrishnan, and with presidents and governors. Kamala Nehru's daughter, Indira Gandhi, came to her for comfort and for blessings. According to the historian Sunil Khilnani, when Indira Gandhi was first sworn in as prime minister in 1966, she wore a rudraksha necklace that Ma had given her. The chronology records a visit by Ma to the prime minister's house at Indira Gandhi's request in April 1966, and in December 1968 Indira Gandhi inaugurated the hospital named after Ma at Varanasi in her presence. Pierre Trudeau, the Canadian prime minister, came to see her at Varanasi in January 1971.

Seekers from abroad came too, though she never travelled outside India. Paramahansa Yogananda met her during his visit to India in the mid-1930s and gave her a chapter in *Autobiography of a Yogi* (1946), "The Bengali Joy-Permeated Mother." The French writer and filmmaker Arnaud Desjardins became a disciple, as did the German writer Melita Maschmann.

By the early 1950s her devotees had formed the Shree Shree Anandamayee Sangha to manage her ashrams. By her death there were around twenty-five to thirty of them, at Varanasi, Kankhal, Vrindavan, Dehradun and elsewhere, along with schools, a hospital and, at Varanasi, a girls' school, the Kanyapeeth (the chronology traces it to a girls' ashram at Haridwar in 1938; Khilnani dates the Varanasi school to the early 1940s), where girls from the age of six live under a strict routine of worship, singing and lessons. Each year she presided over a week-long retreat, the *samyam vrata*, devoted to self-restraint and meditation.

In August 1982 she asked to be taken to the Kishenpur ashram in Dehradun. She died there on the evening of 27 August 1982, in the room above the one where Bholanath had died. Her body was taken to the ashram at Kankhal, near Haridwar, and buried there, as she had wished, and a shrine was built over it.

## The teaching

Anandamayi Ma wrote nothing. Her teaching survives in conversations noted down by her companions and in collections of sayings such as *Matri Vani*. She spoke simple Bengali and often answered with a question, a story or a single sentence. Educated visitors in Dhaka were struck by how briefly she could answer the philosophical questions they brought.

### One reality in many forms

The centre of her teaching is the Advaita claim that only one reality exists and that everything is a form of it. "There is ONE unchanging indivisible REALITY which, though unmanifest, reveals itself in infinite multiplicity and diversity," runs the first entry of *Matri Vani*. She used a personal word for this reality as often as an abstract one. She spoke of Him, of God, of Bhagavan, and in the same breath of the Self. "God Almighty is nameless and formless; yet all names and forms are His."

This let her move between traditions without strain. A worshipper of Kali, a devotee of Krishna, a monk of Shankara's order and a Muslim at a Sufi shrine were all, in her view, approaching the same One by different doors. She told people to begin where they already stood. "Everyone should start along the lines taught by his own religion," she said, and she told others to progress "along your own path... in your own rhythm." She prescribed no single method. Some she sent to repeat a name, some to study scripture, some to selfless work, some to meditation.

### This body

Her way of speaking about herself was itself a teaching. She called herself "this body" and avoided the first person. When Yogananda asked her to tell him about her life, she answered, in his account: "Father, there is little to tell. My consciousness has never associated itself with this temporary body." As a child, as a girl, as a wife, she went on, she had been the same. Devotees took this as a description of a person who had never identified with the body in the first place.

The same reticence shaped her role as a teacher. She almost never gave formal initiation. Those who wanted a mantra received it from Bholanath in her presence, and after his death from her mother, Muktananda Giri, and later from appointed priests called *acharyas*. She did not call herself anyone's guru. She said she neither asked anyone to take initiation nor refused anyone who asked.

### Remembering Him

In practice, her advice came back to one instruction, to keep God in mind through every part of the day. "Your sole duty is to remember that He alone is, and that everything is His doing." She asked people to treat every kind of work as an offering. "Perform all work as God's service." And she asked them to look for God everywhere: "Try to see God in everything and in everybody, including yourself." She did not separate this from the duties of a householder. Most of her followers were householders, and she told them to do their work well and think of it as His.

Surrender runs through all of this. "Unconditional surrender to Him is the best solace for man," says *Matri Vani*. She told the grieving and the anxious that what happens is His doing and that complaint only adds to the burden. She also pushed people beyond comfort. The human being, she said, is the one creature able to realize God, and "Who am I? This realization is the purpose of human life." Devotion, in her teaching, was a way of arriving at the same recognition that the Advaita teachers reached by inquiry.

I find her hardest to place of anyone in this part of the book. She said almost nothing that a Vedantin or a Vaishnava had not said before, and she founded no school. Yet the people who met her, from Bengali professors to French filmmakers, wrote about her as if they had met the thing their books described. The teaching is in the sayings, and the sayings are plain. The force seems to have been in the way she lived them, and that is the part a written chapter cannot carry.

## Lineage

Anandamayi Ma had no human guru and belonged to no order. Her lineage on the tree runs from the traditions she drew on: the Shakta worship of Bengal, whose Kali puja she joined; the Vaishnava devotion of her family; and the Advaita in whose terms she most often explained herself. Her husband and Bhaiji appear as her first two disciples.

She did not appoint a successor. Her sangha runs her ashrams, and initiation is still given in her name by its acharyas, at her shrine in Kankhal and before the images she consecrated. Her influence spread through people who carried what they had learned from her into their own work, among them the French writer **Arnaud Desjardins**.

## The practice

She gave no single method. The advice that recurs most often in her recorded conversations is this.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Begin from your own religion.</strong> Take up the form of worship or prayer you were raised in, or whichever draws you.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Go at your own pace.</strong> She told people to progress along their own path and in their own rhythm.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>See God everywhere.</strong> Look for Him in everything and everyone, including yourself.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Offer every action.</strong> Do your work as service to God and leave its results to Him.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Surrender.</strong> Abandon yourself to Him without reserve.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Ask who you are.</strong> She called this realization the purpose of human life.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**Orthodoxy and caste.** Anandamayi Ma taught that caste, sect and gender have no meaning at the level of the Self, and her followers included people of every caste, non-Hindus and many women. Her ashrams, however, followed the rules of ritual purity, including caste rules, and she did not interfere with their administration. Some critics, as the scholar Neelima Shukla-Bhatt notes, called her a guru of the elite and the high castes. Her defenders say she accepted every visitor and left each institution and person to their own customs.

**A guru of the powerful.** Her closeness to the Nehru family, to Indira Gandhi and to governors and ministers drew comment in her lifetime. Nothing in the record suggests that she sought political influence. The record does show that many powerful people sought her, and that their presence shaped how the wider public saw her.

**Charity.** Shukla-Bhatt also records the observation that her teaching did not stress social service, though her ashrams ran schools, hospitals and clinics. Her followers answer that, for her, the realization that others are one's own Self was the deepest response to suffering.

**The sources.** Almost everything known about her early life comes from devotees writing after she was revered. The miraculous elements of her biography, from the ecstasies of her infancy to the spontaneous Sanskrit and Qur'anic recitation, are the movement's accounts. Hallstrom's study of 1999 is the main scholarly account.

## Descendants

She left an organisation without a successor. The **Shree Shree Anandamayee Sangha** runs her ashrams and the Kanyapeeth, and continues initiation in her name. In France, **Arnaud Desjardins** became her best-known Western disciple. Her example also mattered for women who came later. On the tree she stands near **Amma** and **Mother Meera**, two later women teachers whom devotees regard as embodiments of the Divine Mother, though neither was her disciple.
