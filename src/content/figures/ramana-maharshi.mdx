<aside class="note"><strong>Pronunciation.</strong> RUH-muh-nuh MAH-hur-shee. Devotees simply say <i>Bhagavan</i>, "the Lord." The name "Ramana" was given to him in 1907 by the Sanskrit poet Ganapati Muni.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>It is a hot afternoon in July 1896, in an upstairs room of an uncle's house in Madurai. A sixteen-year-old schoolboy, more interested in wrestling and football than in his textbooks, is sitting alone. He is in perfect health. Then, with no warning and no cause he could ever name, he is seized by the certainty that he is about to die.</p>
<p>He does not call for a doctor or tell his family. Instead he does something strange. He lies down, stiffens his limbs like a corpse, holds his breath, and keeps his mouth shut so no sound can escape. Then he asks himself, in his own later telling, <q>Now death has come; what does it mean?</q> The body will be carried to the burning ground and reduced to ash. But is that the end of <em>him</em>?</p>
<p>What happened next took perhaps twenty minutes and lasted the rest of his life. He found that something did not go with the body: a living awareness of "I" that the stiffening, the silence and the imagined cremation could not touch. The fear of death vanished, he said, and never returned. Six weeks later, on 29 August, he slipped out of the house with a few rupees and a note saying he had gone in search of his Father. Three days later he walked into the great temple at Tiruvannamalai, at the foot of a hill called Arunachala. He would not leave the area again for fifty-four years.</p>
</section>

## Life

Venkataraman was the second son of a pleader (a small-town lawyer) in a Tamil Brahmin family. Nothing marked him out as religious. He was an indifferent student, known among friends mainly for sleeping so deeply that they could carry him off and beat him without waking him, a detail biographers would later read as a sign. When his father died in 1892, the family scattered and the boys were sent to an uncle in Madurai.

Two things stirred in him before the death experience. A relative mentioned in passing that he had just come from "Arunachala," and the name struck the boy with an awe he could not explain; he had thought it was a mythical place, not a town you could take a train to. Around the same time he read the *Periya Puranam*, the old Tamil lives of the sixty-three Shaiva saints, and was moved to tears by their devotion. Neither was philosophy. Both were longing.

<aside class="note"><strong>Arunachala</strong> is a hill of about 800 m in Tamil Nadu, held to be Shiva himself in the form of fire. Each year at the Karthigai Deepam festival a huge flame is lit on its summit.</aside>

At Tiruvannamalai he threw away his sacred thread, his money and most of his clothes. He sat in the temple's underground shrine, the Patala Lingam, so absorbed that insects ate at his legs and he did not notice. Local boys threw stones at the strange silent youth; an eccentric local saint, Seshadri Swamigal, is said to have kept them away. Over the next twenty-six years he moved slowly up the hill: to a shrine nearby in 1897, to Virupaksha Cave on the slopes from 1899 to 1916, then to Skandashram a little higher. He hardly spoke for years. When visitors pressed questions on him, he answered by gesture or by writing in the sand or on scraps of paper.

His family found him, and his mother begged him to come home. He wrote her a short note saying that what is destined to happen will happen, and stayed. In 1916 she came to him instead, lived with him on the hill, and died there in 1922. He came down to the shrine built over her grave, and an ashram grew up around him. It is still there: Sri Ramanasramam.

The world arrived gradually. The Sanskrit scholar Ganapati Muni proclaimed him a *maharshi*, a great sage, in 1907. The British writer Paul Brunton came in 1931, and his best-seller *A Search in Secret India* (1934) made Ramana famous in the West. Paramahansa Yogananda visited in 1935, and a few seconds of film of that meeting survive. Somerset Maugham came in 1938. Carl Jung wrote about him without visiting. Through all of it, the routine at the ashram hardly changed: Ramana on a couch in an open hall, anyone free to walk in, sit, ask, or simply look.

In 1949 a tumor appeared on his left arm. He let the doctors operate several times but treated the illness as something happening to the body, not to him. When devotees begged him not to leave them, he reportedly answered that they gave too much importance to the body, and asked where he could go. He died on the evening of 14 April 1950. Several people, including the photographer Henri Cartier-Bresson, reported seeing a bright shooting star cross the sky toward the summit at that moment.

## The teaching

Ramana's teaching can be stated in a sentence, which is part of why it spread so far. **You already are what you are looking for; the only obstacle is the mistaken belief that you are this body and this mind, and that belief dissolves when you look closely at who holds it.**

Everything else is method. The key move is about the word "I." Every thought, Ramana pointed out, is somebody's thought. Under all of them runs one root thought, the sense of being a separate "I," which appears when you wake up and vanishes in deep sleep. Instead of fighting the other thoughts one by one, follow that root. Ask where this "I" comes from. Hold attention on the bare sense of being, without describing it. The "I" that seemed so solid turns out to have no ground of its own, and what remains is not nothing but pure awareness, which he called the Self or simply "the Heart."

<aside class="note"><strong>In the original.</strong> Asked "Who am I?", his first written answer was <span class="ta" lang="ta">அறிவே நான்</span> (<i>arive nan</i>): roughly, "Knowing itself is I."</aside>

Three features made this distinctive:

**It is an act of attention, not of argument.** Classical Advaita also asks "who am I?", but usually as a long intellectual process of elimination ("I am not the body, not the senses, not the mind...") carried out under a teacher, alongside scripture study. Ramana thought that reasoning keeps the mind busy. His inquiry is closer to turning a flashlight back on the hand holding it.

**Silence is the highest teaching.** He said repeatedly that his real teaching was given in silence and that words were a concession. He compared himself to the ancient image of Shiva as Dakshinamurti, the young teacher who instructs aged sages without speaking. Visitors' accounts are full of people whose questions dissolved simply sitting in the hall.

**The Heart is on the right.** One idea is entirely his own, and odd. He located the spiritual "Heart," the seat of the Self, on the right side of the chest, about two finger-widths from the center, distinct from the physical heart on the left. He insisted this came from experience, not texts, while also saying that the Self has no location at all and the right-side Heart is a pointer for beginners.

For those who could not inquire, he offered a second road: complete surrender to God or the guru. He did not see these as competing. Either way, the separate "I" gives up its claim to run the show; inquiry dissolves it by looking, surrender by handing it over.

He also had a strong devotional streak that the purely philosophical image of him tends to hide. His most popular compositions are love poems to the hill, the *Five Hymns to Arunachala*, sung at the ashram to this day with the refrain <span class="ta" lang="ta">அருணாசல சிவ</span> (*Arunachala Shiva*).

Here is how I'd put it. Ramana did not add a new idea about what reality is. What he did was remove nearly everything that stood between an ordinary person and the oldest idea in Indian philosophy: the long apprenticeship, the Sanskrit, the caste and ritual qualifications, the institutional guru. He demonstrated the result in his own person and then pointed back at the questioner. That turned out to be enormously portable. Most of what the West now calls "non-duality" passes, in one way or another, through this hall in Tiruvannamalai.

## Lineage

Ramana is the purest case in this book of realization without a lineage. He had no guru, was never initiated into any order, and never formally became a monk, though he lived as one. When asked who his guru was, he pointed to the hill, or to the Self. The tree below therefore looks unusual. Above him there are only influences: books he read and a mountain he loved. Below him the branches are thick.

<MiniTree id="ramana-maharshi" />

Two edges deserve a word. Ramana's "guru," Arunachala, is drawn dotted: it is real as devotion but not a lineage in any historical sense. And the most consequential branch runs through **Papaji**, an army officer and lifelong Krishna devotee who came to the ashram in 1944 asking to see God and, in his own account, found the Self instead. Decades later in Lucknow he taught thousands of Western seekers, and many of them became teachers in turn. That branch is the bridge to Western "Neo-Advaita," which kept Ramana's inquiry but often dropped the surrender, the devotion and the decades of sitting that surrounded it. Papaji's mother was reportedly a sister of Swami Rama Tirtha, which ties this tree to the Neo-Vedanta stream in Part 2 through family rather than teaching.

## The practice

Ramana gave almost no technique, deliberately. But the inquiry as he described it to visitors can be put in a few steps.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Notice a thought arising,</strong> any thought at all, and instead of following it, ask: to whom did this occur?</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>The answer comes back, "to me."</strong> Then ask: who am I? Do not answer with a word or a concept. The question is a direction, not a riddle.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Turn attention toward the bare sense of being "I"</strong>, the feeling of existing before any description of yourself. Rest there.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>When attention wanders, repeat.</strong> Each return weakens the habit of identifying with whatever thought is passing.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Do it during the day, not only on a cushion.</strong> He told householders that inquiry needs no special place, posture or schedule.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

Ramana is one of the very few figures in this book about whom there is no scandal. The real questions are about how his story and teaching have been handled since.

**The story was shaped by devotees.** The famous account of the death experience was set down in 1931 by B.V. Narasimha Swami, decades after the event, and different versions differ in detail. The scholar-devotee David Godman, among others, has pointed out that even the common phrase "death experience" misleads, since Ramana did not physically die in 1896. The outline is well attested; the polish is later.

**Silence invites projection.** A teacher who says little lets everyone hear what they need. Papaji, the Krishna devotee, came away convinced Ramana was secretly a great bhakta. Philosophers read him as a pure Advaitin. Both can cite him.

**The export lost something.** Critics within the tradition argue that Western Neo-Advaita turned Ramana's inquiry into an instant insight available at a weekend satsang, cutting away the devotion, surrender and lifelong steadiness that framed it. Ramana is not responsible for his descendants, but the "no method needed" message is easy to hear as "no effort needed," which is not what he said.

## Descendants

He appointed no successor and founded no order; the ashram is run by his brother's family. His influence instead runs through individuals: the poet **Muruganar**, whose verses record many of his sayings; long-time attendants such as **Annamalai Swami**; **Lakshmana Swamy**, who realized the Self in his presence in 1949; **Papaji** and the Western Neo-Advaita movement; and writers like **Paul Brunton** and **Arthur Osborne** who carried him to readers who would never go to India. He also shapes Part 3 as a whole: Nisargadatta's "I Am" practice, though from a different lineage, is usually read side by side with his.
