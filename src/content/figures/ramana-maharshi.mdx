<section class="scene">
<p>It is a hot afternoon in July 1896, in an upstairs room of his uncle's house in Madurai. Venkataraman, sixteen, is sitting alone. He is a healthy schoolboy who likes wrestling and football more than his lessons. Suddenly he is overwhelmed by the feeling that he is about to die.</p>
<p>He tells no one. He lies down on the floor, stiffens his body like a corpse, holds his breath and keeps his lips shut. Then, by his own later account, he asks himself, <q>Now death has come; what does it mean?</q> The body will be carried to the cremation ground and burned. Will he die with it?</p>
<p>Lying there, he became aware of a living sense of "I" that the stiff body and the imagined funeral left untouched. The fear went away, and he said it never came back. Six weeks later, on 29 August, he slipped out of the house with a few rupees and a note saying he had gone to find his Father. Three days later he walked into the great temple at Tiruvannamalai, at the foot of the hill called Arunachala. He lived there for the remaining fifty-four years of his life.</p>
</section>

## Life

Venkataraman was the second son of a pleader, a small-town lawyer, in a Tamil Brahmin family. He was an average student with no special interest in religion. His friends remembered him mostly for sleeping so soundly that they could carry him off and hit him without waking him, and his biographers later read this as an early sign. After his father died in 1892, he and his elder brother went to live with an uncle in Madurai.

Two things moved him in the year before. An elderly relative mentioned that he had just come from Arunachala, and the name filled the boy with awe. He had assumed it was a place from mythology, and was startled to learn it was a real town with a railway station. Around the same time he read the *Periya Puranam*, the Tamil lives of the sixty-three Shaiva saints, and their devotion moved him to tears. Both were devotional stirrings; he read philosophy only years later.

<aside class="note"><strong>Arunachala</strong> is a hill of about 800 m in Tamil Nadu, held to be Shiva himself in the form of fire. Each year at the Karthigai Deepam festival a huge flame is lit on its summit.</aside>

At Tiruvannamalai he threw away his sacred thread, his money and most of his clothes. He sat for weeks in the Patala Lingam, an underground shrine in the temple, so deep in absorption that insects bit his legs and he paid no attention. Local boys threw stones at the silent youth, and Seshadri Swamigal, an eccentric holy man of the town, is said to have chased them away. Over the next twenty-six years he moved gradually up the hill: to a nearby shrine in 1897, to Virupaksha Cave on its slopes from 1899 to 1916, and then to Skandashram, higher still. For years he barely spoke. When visitors pressed him with questions, he answered with gestures or wrote in the sand or on scraps of paper.

His family tracked him down, and his mother begged him to come home. He wrote her a short note saying that whatever is destined to happen will happen, and stayed. In 1916 she came to live with him on the hill, and she died there in 1922. He moved down to the shrine built over her grave, and an ashram grew up around him. Sri Ramanasramam still stands on that spot.

In 1907 the Sanskrit scholar Ganapati Muni declared him a *maharshi*, a great sage, and gave him the name Ramana. The British writer Paul Brunton arrived in 1931, and his best-seller *A Search in Secret India* (1934) made Ramana known in the West. Paramahansa Yogananda visited in 1935, and a few seconds of film of their meeting survive. Somerset Maugham came in 1938, and Carl Jung wrote about him from a distance. Daily life at the ashram stayed much the same through all of it. Ramana sat on a couch in an open hall, and anyone could walk in, sit down, ask a question or simply look at him.

In 1949 a tumor appeared on his left arm. He allowed several operations and spoke of the illness as something happening to the body. When devotees pleaded with him to stay, he reportedly told them they gave the body too much importance, and asked where he could possibly go. He died on the evening of 14 April 1950. Several people at the ashram, among them the photographer Henri Cartier-Bresson, said they saw a bright shooting star cross the sky toward the summit at that moment.

## The teaching

Ramana taught that the Self a seeker looks for is what the seeker already is, and that the belief in being only this body and mind falls away under close examination of who holds it.

His method centers on the word "I." Every thought, Ramana pointed out, is somebody's thought. Beneath them all runs a root thought, the sense of being a separate "I." It rises when you wake and disappears in deep sleep. He advised following that root thought back to where it comes from, instead of struggling with other thoughts one at a time. Keep attention on the plain sense of being, without describing it. The "I" that seemed so solid turns out to have no ground of its own. What remains is pure awareness, which he called the Self, or simply the Heart.

<aside class="note"><strong>In the original.</strong> Asked "Who am I?", his first written answer was <span class="ta" lang="ta">அறிவே நான்</span> (<i>arive nan</i>): roughly, "Knowing itself is I."</aside>

Three things set his approach apart.

**Inquiry as attention.** Classical Advaita also asks "Who am I?", usually as a long process of reasoning carried out under a teacher alongside study of the scriptures, setting aside the body, then the senses, then the mind. Ramana found that this kind of reasoning keeps the mind busy. He taught people to turn their attention around onto the one who is attending, much as you might turn a flashlight to look at the hand holding it.

**Silence as teaching.** He said many times that his real teaching was given in silence and that words were a concession. He pointed to the old image of Shiva as Dakshinamurti, the young teacher who instructs aged sages without speaking. Visitors describe again and again how their questions faded away while they sat in the hall.

**The Heart on the right.** One idea is entirely his own, and unusual. He placed the spiritual Heart, the seat of the Self, on the right side of the chest, about two finger-widths from the center. He said he knew this from his own experience. He also said the Self has no location at all, and offered the right-side Heart as a pointer for beginners.

For people who found inquiry too hard, he offered a second road: complete surrender to God or to the guru. He saw the two roads as arriving at the same place. In inquiry the separate "I" dissolves when you look at it, and in surrender you hand it over.

He had a strong devotional side that the purely philosophical picture of him tends to leave out. His best-loved compositions are love poems to the hill, the *Five Hymns to Arunachala*, still sung at the ashram with the refrain <span class="ta" lang="ta">அருணாசல சிவ</span> (*Arunachala Shiva*).

Ramana's view of reality is the old Advaita view. What he added was a way in that almost anyone could use. He dispensed with the long apprenticeship, the Sanskrit, the caste and ritual qualifications and the institutional guru, and he answered questions by turning them back to the person who asked. His teaching was easy to carry elsewhere for that reason, and much of what the West now calls non-duality traces back to his hall in Tiruvannamalai.

## Lineage

Ramana is the clearest case in this book of realization outside any lineage. He had no human guru and was never initiated into an order, and although he lived like a monk he never took formal vows. Asked who his guru was, he pointed to the hill, or to the Self. His tree shows only influences above him, the books he read and the mountain he worshipped, and a large number of disciples and readers below.

<MiniTree id="ramana-maharshi" />

The line from Arunachala is dotted because the relationship is devotional, and a devotional bond belongs in a different category from a historical lineage. The branch with the most consequences runs through **Papaji**, an army officer and lifelong devotee of Krishna, who came to the ashram in 1944 asking to see God and, by his own account, found the Self. Decades later in Lucknow he taught thousands of Western seekers, and many of them became teachers too. That branch grew into Western Neo-Advaita, which kept Ramana's inquiry and often left out the surrender, the devotion and the long years of practice that went with it. Papaji's mother was reportedly a sister of Swami Rama Tirtha, so this tree also connects by family to the Neo-Vedanta stream in Part 2.

## The practice

Ramana kept technique to a minimum on purpose. The inquiry as he explained it to visitors comes down to a few steps.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Notice a thought arising,</strong> any thought at all. Instead of following it, ask to whom it occurred.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>The answer comes back: to me.</strong> Then ask who you are. Leave the question unanswered in words and use it to point your attention.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Turn attention toward the bare sense of being "I"</strong>, the feeling of existing before any description of yourself. Rest there.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>When attention wanders, start again.</strong> Each return loosens the habit of identifying with whatever thought is passing.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Keep it up through the day.</strong> He told householders that inquiry needs no special place, posture or schedule.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

Ramana is one of the few figures in this book with no scandal attached. The questions that matter concern how his story and his teaching were handled after him.

**Devotees shaped the story.** The famous account of the death experience was written down in 1931 by B.V. Narasimha Swami, thirty-five years after the event, and the versions differ in detail. The scholar and devotee David Godman has pointed out that the usual phrase "death experience" is misleading, since his body did not die in 1896. The core events are well attested, and the literary polish came later.

**People hear what they want in his silence.** Papaji, the Krishna devotee, left convinced that Ramana was secretly a great bhakta. Philosophers read him as a strict Advaitin. Each side can find passages to support its reading.

**Neo-Advaita simplified him.** Critics within the tradition argue that Western Neo-Advaita turned Ramana's inquiry into an instant insight on offer at a weekend satsang, and dropped the devotion, surrender and steady practice that surrounded it. Ramana is not responsible for his descendants, but his message that no technique is needed is easily heard as permission to make no effort. He told people to keep up the inquiry until it became effortless.

## Descendants

He named no successor and founded no order, and the ashram is run by descendants of his brother. His influence has travelled through individuals: the poet **Muruganar**, whose verses record many of his sayings; long-time attendants such as **Annamalai Swami**; **Lakshmana Swamy**, who realized the Self in his presence in 1949; **Papaji** and the Western Neo-Advaita movement; and writers like **Paul Brunton** and **Arthur Osborne** who carried him to readers who would never go to India. He also shapes Part 3 as a whole. Nisargadatta's "I Am" practice comes from a different lineage, and readers usually study the two side by side.
