<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> His family called him Naren. During his wandering years he used more than one monastic name, and by most accounts he settled on Vivekananda, "the bliss of discernment," in 1893, at the suggestion of his patron Ajit Singh, the Raja of Khetri. The Sanskrit in the title is the motto he gave his order: "for one's own liberation and for the good of the world."</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Chicago, Monday 11 September 1893, the opening day of the World's Parliament of Religions. The great hall of the new building on Michigan Avenue that now houses the Art Institute is packed, and on the platform sit Christian bishops, a Jain lawyer, Buddhist and Brahmo delegates, and a thirty-year-old Bengali monk in an orange robe and turban who has no credentials from any religious body.</p>
<p>He had been admitted on the word of a Harvard classics professor, John Henry Wright, who had met him a few weeks earlier, urged him to represent Hinduism, and vouched for him to the organisers. Told that the monk had no credentials, Wright is said to have replied that asking for them was like asking the sun to state its right to shine. All morning, when the chairman turned to him, he asked to speak later. He had never addressed a large audience. In the afternoon he finally stood up and began, "Sisters and Brothers of America." Biographies say the audience rose and applauded for some two minutes before he could go on.</p>
<p>The speech that followed was short. He said he was proud to belong to a religion that had taught the world tolerance, and he went further. "We believe not only in universal toleration, but we accept all religions as true." He quoted a Sanskrit hymn: "As the different streams having their sources in different places all mingle their water in the sea," so the paths people take all lead to God. He closed by hoping that the bell that had opened the Parliament would ring the death knell of fanaticism.</p>
</section>

## Life

Narendranath Datta was born in north Calcutta on 12 January 1863, into a prosperous Kayastha family. His father, Vishwanath Datta, was an attorney at the Calcutta High Court. His mother, Bhubaneswari Devi, was devout and taught him the epics. He was a restless, athletic, musical boy with a good singing voice and a habit of argument.

He studied at Vidyasagar's Metropolitan Institution from 1871, spent part of his teens with the family in Raipur, and returned to Calcutta in 1879. He went on to the General Assembly's Institution, the Scottish missionary college now called Scottish Church College, and took his BA in 1884. He read Western philosophy seriously, and later translated Herbert Spencer's <i>Education</i> into Bengali. By his own later account he grew sceptical and wanted proof.

He looked for it first in the **Brahmo Samaj**, the reformist movement founded by Rammohan Roy that worshipped one formless God and rejected image worship. Around 1880 he was attending Keshab Chandra Sen's Nava Vidhan (New Dispensation), and he later joined the breakaway Sadharan Brahmo Samaj. Biographies say he went around Calcutta asking religious leaders one question, whether they had seen God, and that Debendranath Tagore, the Brahmo patriarch, gave him no direct answer.

He met Ramakrishna, the priest of the Kali temple at Dakshineswar north of the city, probably in November 1881 at a friend's house, where Naren sang. His first visit to Dakshineswar came in late 1881 or early 1882; sources differ. When he asked his question there, Ramakrishna said yes, he saw God as he saw Naren, only more intensely. For the next four and a half years Naren went back and forth between his college life and Dakshineswar. In the Order's accounts he argued with Ramakrishna, scoffed at the worship of Kali, and at first rejected the Advaita teaching that the self and God are one as blasphemy. The story of that relationship, and of what Ramakrishna himself taught, is told in the Ramakrishna chapter.

In 1884 his father died suddenly and left the family in debt. Naren looked for work and found little. By his own account these were months of hunger and doubt, and it was in this period, according to the Order's biographies, that he came to accept Kali at Ramakrishna's urging. When Ramakrishna fell ill with throat cancer in 1885, Naren led the group of young men who nursed him at Cossipore. Ramakrishna died on 16 August 1886.

The young disciples had nowhere to go. Naren rented a crumbling house at Baranagar, and it became the first monastery of what would become the Ramakrishna Order. On Christmas Eve 1886, at Antpur, he and eight others resolved to become monks, and early in 1887 they took formal vows of renunciation by performing the rite themselves.

From 1888 he wandered. For about five years he travelled India on foot and by train as a *parivrajaka*, a wandering monk, from the Himalaya to the far south, staying sometimes with princes, such as the Raja of Khetri, and more often with clerks, teachers and villagers. He saw the poverty of the countryside at close quarters. In late December 1892 he reached Kanyakumari, the southern tip of India, swam or was rowed out to a rock offshore, and meditated there for about three days. In a letter of March 1894 he looked back on sitting "on the last bit of Indian rock" and thinking about the poor of India and what monks owed them. The Ramakrishna movement now marks this as the moment he decided to go West and raise money and attention for India.

Funded partly by the Raja of Khetri and partly by admirers in Madras, he sailed from Bombay on 31 May 1893 and reached Chicago in July, only to learn that the Parliament was weeks away and that he needed credentials he did not have. Wright's help solved that. After his success in September he spent more than three years lecturing across the United States and in England. In New York in 1894 he started what became the Vedanta Society. In the summer of 1895 he taught about a dozen students for some seven weeks at Thousand Island Park on the St Lawrence River; their notes became *Inspired Talks*. In London, in November 1895, he met Margaret Noble, an Irish schoolteacher who would follow him to India. In 1896 he met the Sanskritist Max Müller at Oxford and the German Indologist Paul Deussen at Kiel, and he published *Raja Yoga*, his book on meditation, which sold widely.

<aside class="note"><strong>Math.</strong> Pronounced "mutt." A Hindu monastery. The Ramakrishna Math is the monastic order; the Ramakrishna Mission, its twin, is the registered body that runs schools, hospitals and relief work.</aside>

He landed at Colombo on 15 January 1897 and was greeted as a hero all the way up the east coast to Calcutta. His speeches on that tour, published as *Lectures from Colombo to Almora*, are the most forceful things he said to Indians. He called on them to stop being ashamed of their tradition, to give up priestcraft and untouchability, and to build strength. On 1 May 1897 he called the monks and lay disciples together in Calcutta and founded the Ramakrishna Mission. In March 1898 he initiated Margaret Noble as a *brahmacharini*, a celibate student, with the name Nivedita. In December 1898 he installed Ramakrishna's relics on land the monks had bought at Belur, across the Ganges from Calcutta, which became the order's headquarters.

He went West again from June 1899 to December 1900, to New York, California and Paris. By then his health was failing; biographies mention diabetes and asthma among other illnesses. Back at Belur he spent his last year and a half teaching the young monks and handing the work over. On the evening of 4 July 1902, after a day of classes and a walk, he went to his room to meditate and died there at about twenty past nine. He was thirty-nine. His disciples say he left the body in a final *samadhi*; the doctors' explanation was a burst blood vessel in the brain.

## The teaching

Vivekananda never wrote a systematic treatise. His teaching survives in lectures, class notes taken by students, conversations and a great many letters, collected after his death in the *Complete Works*, now nine volumes. He repeated a few central ideas in many forms.

**The divine self.** The foundation is Advaita, the non-dual Vedanta associated with the eighth-century philosopher Shankara. There is one reality, Brahman, and the true self of every being, the Atman, is identical with it. Bondage is ignorance of this. Vivekananda put it most compactly in the preface to *Raja Yoga*: "Each soul is potentially divine. The goal is to manifest this Divinity within by controlling nature, external and internal." He added that this could be done through work, worship, mental discipline or philosophy, one or all of them, and that doctrines, rituals, books and temples were secondary details.

**Religion as experience.** He insisted that religion is realisation, something to be seen and lived, and that belief on authority is a lower stage. This was the use he made of Ramakrishna's "I have seen God." Scriptures, in his account, are records of the experiences of the sages, and each person can verify them in principle, the way a student repeats an experiment. He liked to call this the scientific character of Vedanta, and he presented *Raja Yoga* to Western audiences as a practical method for getting such experience.

**Four yogas.** He organised the paths into four, each the subject of a book built from his lectures. *Karma yoga* is the path of work done without attachment to results. *Bhakti yoga* is the path of love for a personal God. *Raja yoga* is the path of meditation and control of the mind, based on Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*. *Jnana yoga* is the path of knowledge, discrimination between the real and the unreal. These four were ancient categories, and the Bhagavad Gita already joins work, devotion and knowledge, but his neat fourfold scheme, each yoga suited to a temperament and each leading to the same goal, became the standard modern way of explaining Hindu spirituality.

**All religions are true.** At Chicago and after, he taught that the religions of the world are different paths up the same mountain, suited to different temperaments and cultures. He took this from Ramakrishna, who had, by his own account, practised several religions in turn and reached the same realisation in each. Vivekananda gave it a philosophical frame. In lectures such as "The Vedanta Philosophy," given at Harvard in 1896, he described dualism, qualified non-dualism and Advaita as progressive stages on one road, and in "The Ideal of a Universal Religion" he said that a universal religion would make room for the philosopher, the devotee, the mystic and the worker. In the same lectures he usually placed Advaita at the summit, as the framework within which the other faiths found their place. Scholars still argue about whether this is pluralism or a generous form of hierarchy (see "Critiques and questions" below).

**Practical Vedanta.** His most consequential move was to turn this metaphysics into ethics. If God is in every being, then serving the poor, the sick and the ignorant is worship. In four London lectures in November 1896, published as *Practical Vedanta*, he said Vedanta must be "intensely practical" and carried from "forest caves to busy streets and cities," into city life, home life and national life. He had said it bluntly at the Parliament itself, on 20 September 1893. "It is an insult to a starving people to offer them religion," he told the audience, and asked why Christians sent missionaries to save the souls of Indians and did nothing to save their bodies from starvation. The Order's tradition traces this to a moment at Dakshineswar when Ramakrishna, speaking of "compassion for living beings," corrected himself and said that no one has the standing to pity others, and that they should be served as God. According to that account Naren came out of the room saying he had heard something new and would proclaim it to the world. The phrase associated with this, *Shiva jnane jiva seva*, "serve beings knowing them as Shiva," became the working creed of the Ramakrishna Mission.

<aside class="note"><strong>Practical Vedanta.</strong> Vivekananda's name for Advaita applied to ordinary life. Since the one Self is present in everyone, work for others, especially the poor, is a form of worship and a path to liberation.</aside>

**Strength.** A strand that runs through his Indian speeches is a demand for courage. He told audiences that weakness was the real sin, that India's young men needed physical and moral strength before anything else, and that a person who knows himself to be the Atman has nothing to fear. This side of him spoke directly to the young nationalists of the next generation.

**Renunciation and work together.** He kept the monastic ideal. The motto he gave the order, *atmano mokshartham jagad hitaya cha*, "for one's own liberation and for the good of the world," joins the two aims that earlier Advaita had kept apart. Within weeks of the Mission's founding, on 15 May 1897, his brother monk Swami Akhandananda opened what the Order counts as its first famine relief work, at Mahula in Murshidabad district.

I give him more credit for originality than the familiar picture of a faithful messenger of Ramakrishna allows. Ramakrishna gave him the experience and the conviction that God can be seen; the Brahmo Samaj and his Western reading gave him the habit of systematising. What he made of the two was his own. Ramakrishna was a mystic of the Kali temple who spoke in parables and lived in ecstasy. Vivekananda took the same insights to Chicago, London and the famine districts, and in doing so changed what they meant. Whether the change was faithful is a fair question, and the next generations of scholars have answered it in very different ways.

## Lineage

Vivekananda stands at the root of the Neo-Vedanta branch of the tree. Above him are Ramakrishna, Shankara's Advaita and the Brahmo reform of Keshab Chandra Sen. Below him are the Ramakrishna Order and a wide band of people he influenced without initiating.

<MiniTree id="vivekananda" />

The edge from **Ramakrishna** is fully documented: four and a half years of visits, then life with him at Cossipore during his last illness. Ramakrishna did not perform a formal monastic initiation; the disciples took their vows together after his death. The Brahmo link through **Keshab Chandra Sen** is documented in his student years. Below him, **Sister Nivedita** was initiated by him in 1898. His influence on **Swami Rama Tirtha** and on **Sri Aurobindo** rests on their own testimony; Aurobindo wrote that in Alipore jail in 1908 Vivekananda "was speaking with me for about 15 days," as a presence in meditation, which is a claim about a spiritual experience. The present-day Ramakrishna Order, including teachers such as **Swami Sarvapriyananda**, descends from him institutionally.

## The practice

He taught meditation to small classes in New York and at Thousand Island Park, and *Raja Yoga* sets out a method. In outline it runs like this.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Prepare the body and conduct.</strong> Keep the ethical restraints of Patanjali (non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity, non-possession) and sit with the spine straight.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Regulate the breath.</strong> He taught simple rhythmic breathing and alternate-nostril breathing as a way to calm the mind, and warned against forcing advanced exercises without a teacher.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Watch the mind.</strong> Let thoughts come and observe them without following, until they slow down of their own accord.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Concentrate.</strong> Hold the mind on one object, a point in the body, a symbol or an idea, and bring it back each time it wanders.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Work as worship.</strong> Outside meditation, do your duty and serve others without attachment to the result, treating the person in front of you as divine.</div></li>
</ol>

## In his own voice

No recording of his voice is known to survive. According to Belur Math, his voice was recorded on a couple of occasions during his life, but the recordings have never been traced, and no arrangement was made to record the Chicago speeches. The audio clips of "Sisters and Brothers of America" that circulate online are re-enactments; one widely shared clip comes from a feature film. What survives are his words, published in full, and a set of photographs, including studio portraits taken in Chicago in 1893.

## Critiques and questions

**How much was Ramakrishna's?** The Ramakrishna Order presents Vivekananda as the instrument of his master's message. Several historians see a larger gap. Ramakrishna was a Kali worshipper whose religion was ecstatic, devotional and Tantric as well as Advaitic; Vivekananda's public teaching put Advaita and service at the centre and said little about Kali. Elizabeth De Michelis, in *A History of Modern Yoga* (2004), argued that the Brahmo Samaj and Keshab Chandra Sen shaped him as much as Ramakrishna did, and placed *Raja Yoga* among nineteenth-century esoteric and Western currents. Swami Medhananda, in *Swami Vivekananda's Vedantic Cosmopolitanism* (2022), argues against both the "Shankara's follower" and the "Westernised Neo-Vedantin" readings and presents him as an original philosopher working out ideas he received from Ramakrishna in conversation with Indian and Western thought. The service ideal in particular is told in two ways: as Ramakrishna's teaching that Naren carried out, and as Vivekananda's own innovation credited back to his master.

**Experience above scripture.** Anantanand Rambachan, in *The Limits of Scripture* (1994), argued that Vivekananda reversed Shankara. For Shankara the Upanishads are the means of knowing Brahman, and experience confirms them; for Vivekananda experience is the final authority and the scriptures are secondary records of it. Rambachan held that this misreads the tradition Vivekananda claimed to represent and leaves his "scientific" defence of religion on weak ground. Defenders reply that Vivekananda drew on strands of Advaita and yoga that already valued direct realisation.

**A Western ethic?** The German Indologist Paul Hacker argued in a 1962 essay that the idea that "you are that" grounds compassion for others came from Schopenhauer, and reached Vivekananda through Paul Deussen, whom he met in September 1896. Dermot Killingley and others have answered that Vivekananda was already linking Advaita and service before he met Deussen, so the meeting at most sharpened an idea he held. The Order's tradition also places the Dakshineswar episode in Ramakrishna's lifetime.

**Hindu nationalism.** Vivekananda is honoured across Indian politics, and since 1985 the Government of India has marked his birthday, 12 January, as National Youth Day. Hindu nationalist organisations claim him as a founding figure. Jyotirmaya Sharma, in *A Restatement of Religion* (2013), argued that his recasting of diverse traditions as a single "Hinduism," his views on caste and his claims for India's spiritual superiority helped prepare the ground for Hindutva. Swami Medhananda and others reply that Vivekananda attacked hereditary caste and untouchability, accepted all religions as paths to salvation, and was a patriot with a cosmopolitan outlook. His writings contain enough of both emphases that each side can quote him.

## Descendants

The Ramakrishna Math and Mission, headquartered at Belur, is his most direct legacy; the organisation runs a large network of branch centres in India and abroad, with schools, hospitals and relief work alongside monasteries. The Vedanta Societies in the United States and Europe, beginning with New York in 1894, were among the first lasting Hindu institutions in the West, and Swami Sarvapriyananda's online talks reach a large audience from the New York society today. Sister Nivedita carried his ideas into Bengali nationalism, art and education. Beyond the Order, his framing of the four yogas, of religion as experience, and of all religions as paths to one truth shaped Sri Aurobindo, Swami Rama Tirtha and many of the yoga and Vedanta teachers who went West after him. Most of the figures in this part, and many in later parts, explain Hinduism in his terms whether or not they mention him.
