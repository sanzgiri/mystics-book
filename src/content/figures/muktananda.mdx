<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> <i>Muktananda</i> joins <i>mukti</i>, liberation, and <i>ananda</i>, bliss: the bliss of liberation. It was the name he received as a young monk. Devotees called him Baba, an affectionate word for father used for holy men across India.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>It is the summer of 1979 at an old Catskills hotel in South Fallsburg, New York, now an ashram. John Chambers, a journalist who writes about the New Age scene, has come for a month, partly in the hope of giving up cigarettes. His days are full of classes on Kashmir Shaivism, chanting and three or four hours of seva, unpaid work for the ashram. He spends his seva washing dishes.</p>
<p>In the evening, by his account, the guru gives a talk of about two hours, and then people line up to approach his chair. Muktananda holds a wand of peacock feathers. As each person kneels he taps them with it, sometimes lightly, sometimes hard. Chambers later wrote that standing near Muktananda he felt a wave of heat, as if an oven door had opened. Around him in the hall, during the weekend programmes the ashram calls Intensives, people laugh, scream and cry out. The ashram explains these movements as <i>kriyas</i>, the work of an inner energy that the guru has awakened in them.</p>
<p>Chambers went four weeks without a cigarette. Fifteen years later he learned from <i>The New Yorker</i> what some of the guru's closest attendants had been saying about him.</p>
</section>

## Life

The boy who became Muktananda was born in 1908 near Mangalore, on the coast of what is now Karnataka. The date the movement keeps is 16 May; the year is the one generally accepted, though some writers have questioned it. His birth name was Krishna. His family was well off. A short biography published on a Canadian university's encyclopedia of goddess traditions, drawing on the movement's own history, says his parents had long wanted a son and received a mantra for one from a holy man at the temple of Manjunatha, the Shiva of Dharmasthala.

At about fifteen, in 1923 by the usual reckoning, he saw a wandering holy man who would become the central figure of his life. This was Nityananda, later known as Bhagavan Nityananda of Ganeshpuri. Soon afterwards the boy left home. By the movement's account he did not see Nityananda again for many years, but the meeting set him on the road.

His first stop was the ashram of Siddharudha Swami at Hubli, in northern Karnataka. Siddharudha was a famous teacher of Advaita Vedanta, the philosophy of one undivided reality, and his ashram drew students from across the south. There the young man studied Sanskrit, Vedanta and the branches of yoga, and took *sannyasa*, the vows of a monk, in the Saraswati order. He received the name Muktananda. After Siddharudha's death in 1929 he studied with one of Siddharudha's disciples, Muppinarya Swami, at Ranebennur, and then wandered India on foot for years. The Shanti Mandir organisation's biography says that in these years he studied hatha yoga, Ayurvedic medicine, Indian martial arts and the Vedic scriptures.

On his travels he met several unconventional holy men. One was Zipruanna, an *avadhuta*, a renunciant who has dropped all social convention. According to a biography drawing on Siddha Yoga sources, it was Zipruanna who sent him back to Nityananda.

Nityananda had by then settled at Ganeshpuri, a village of hot springs in the hills north-east of Bombay. Muktananda went to him, and on the morning of 15 August 1947, the day India became independent, he received what Siddha Yoga calls his *divya diksha*, divine initiation. The movement gives the date and quotes him: "What an auspicious day. How full of nectar it was." His autobiography begins its account of the path with this initiation and describes the inner change that followed as the work of the awakened kundalini.

What followed, by his own account, was nine years of intense and often frightening inner experience. He meditated in a small hut at Yeola, a town in the Nashik district of Maharashtra, and in other places. His publisher describes the book as a candid record of these experiences. It recounts visions in meditation, among them one he called the Blue Pearl, a point of blue light that Siddha Yoga teaches is a form of the Self. The movement says he reached God-realisation about nine years after his initiation. Nityananda, by the same account, declared him realised.

In 1956 Nityananda gave him a small plot of land at Gavdevi, a few kilometres from his own ashram. Muktananda built a hut and planted a rose garden. The place grew into an ashram, Shree Gurudev Ashram, later renamed Gurudev Siddha Peeth. Nityananda died on 8 August 1961. Muktananda was one of several disciples who taught in his name, and he came to present himself as the heir to Nityananda's spiritual power, the *shakti* of the lineage.

Through the 1960s Westerners began to find their way to Ganeshpuri. In 1969, by the movement's reckoning, Muktananda began writing his spiritual autobiography, which he titled in Hindi *Chitshakti Vilas*, the play of the power of consciousness. Harper and Row published it in New York in 1971 as *Guru: Chitshaktivilas, the Play of Consciousness*. Later editions carry the title *Play of Consciousness*. The movement says it has been translated into eighteen languages.

On 21 August 1970 he flew out of Bombay with five devotees on his first world tour. In 101 days he visited Italy, Switzerland, England, France, the United States, Australia and Singapore, and gave, the movement says, ninety public talks. In Boston about a thousand students and professors came to one evening programme. He did not speak English, and his talks were translated.

The second tour, from 1974 to 1976, made him a figure of the American spiritual counterculture. The Shanti Mandir biography says the trainer Werner Erhard had met him at Ganeshpuri in 1973 and invited him, and the World Religions and Spirituality Project's timeline says Erhard supported the tour and paid for his travel. Siddha Yoga Dham Associates, the SYDA Foundation, was founded in the United States in 1974 to run the work. An ashram opened in Oakland, California, in April 1975. The format that defined the movement took shape in these years: the weekend Intensive, at which a large group received shaktipat together. By 1976, according to Harvard's Pluralism Project, there were eighty centres and five ashrams, and the movement claimed about twenty thousand American followers.

The third tour, from 1978 to 1981, was the longest. A former Catskills resort hotel at South Fallsburg, New York, became an ashram, first called Shree Nityananda Ashram and later Shree Muktananda Ashram, and the American headquarters. From 14 October 1980 to April 1981 he stayed in Santa Monica, where the city's mayor welcomed him and a large white tent served as a hall. He held conferences for artists, for business people and lawyers, and for health workers, ran a programme in prisons, and initiated a small group of Western disciples as Siddha Yoga swamis. His farewell talk in Santa Monica, on 24 April 1981, drew more than 3,500 people, the movement says.

He named his successors in stages. In July 1981, at South Fallsburg, he named a young swami, Nityananda, born Subhash Shetty, as his successor. On 26 April 1982 he gave sannyasa to the young man's elder sister, Malti Shetty, who had been his English translator since 1975, and named her Swami Chidvilasananda, the bliss of the play of consciousness. On 3 May 1982 at Ganeshpuri he installed the two together as joint heads of the lineage. Muktananda died at Ganeshpuri on 2 October 1982. His body was buried in a samadhi shrine, the tomb of a saint, at Gurudev Siddha Peeth.

<aside class="note"><strong>Shaktipat.</strong> <i>Shakti</i> is power, especially the divine feminine power that animates the world; <i>pat</i> means descent. Shaktipat is the descent of that power from a realised guru into a disciple, by touch, look, word or thought. In Muktananda's teaching it awakens <i>kundalini</i>, the coiled energy said to sleep at the base of the spine.</aside>

## The teaching

Muktananda taught that God lives inside every person as their own Self, and that the guru's grace can wake a person up to this directly. His best-known sentence, repeated on Siddha Yoga materials to this day, runs: "Honor your Self. Worship your Self. Meditate on your Self. God dwells within you as you."

**The awakening comes first.** In most Indian yoga systems, kundalini rises after long discipline, if it rises at all. Muktananda reversed the order. The guru awakens the disciple's kundalini at the start, through shaktipat, and the awakened energy then does the work of purification by itself. He called the method *maha yoga*, the great yoga, or *siddha yoga*, the yoga of the perfected ones, because, he said, all the classical yogas arise spontaneously once the energy is awake. A person might find their body moving into hatha yoga postures or their breath changing on its own. Others might weep, laugh, roar or see lights. These were *kriyas*, the movements of the energy clearing out old impressions, and the disciple was asked to watch them without fear and without forcing them.

The religion scholar Charles White, who visited Ganeshpuri, described the system in the journal *History of Religions* in 1974 as enlightenment through shaktipat. He noted that observers could find the shrieking and writhing in the meditation hall disturbing, and that in the ashram's view staying in control of oneself was a hindrance.

**Consciousness at play.** For the philosophy behind this experience, Muktananda turned to Kashmir Shaivism, the medieval Tantric philosophy of Kashmir, especially its school of recognition, Pratyabhijna. In that system the one reality is Shiva, pure consciousness, and the world is the free self-expression of his power, which Muktananda usually called *Chiti*, consciousness as a goddess. Nothing has to be escaped, because everything is consciousness at play. Liberation is the recognition of one's own nature as that consciousness. The world-affirming tone of this philosophy suited a teacher who brought his practice into cities, prisons and business conferences. At South Fallsburg in 1979 there were daily classes on Kashmir Shaivism, and he lectured on it in Santa Monica. Muktananda visited Swami Lakshmanjoo, twice by one account, the leading traditional teacher of the system in Kashmir, and the Muktabodha Indological Research Institute, connected with the movement, later published scholarship on the lineage.

**The guru as a principle.** Muktananda taught devotion to the guru in strong terms, and the *Guru Gita*, a Sanskrit hymn in praise of the guru as God, was chanted every morning in his ashrams. Siddha Yoga teaching describes the guru as finally a principle, the grace-bestowing power of God, which lives in the disciple too, with the outer guru needed to awaken the inner one.

**Meditation for everyone.** He presented meditation as open to anyone, of any religion. "Meditation is universal," the Shanti Mandir organisation quotes him. "It is not the property of any particular religion or nationality." He gave his students two simple mantras. *Om Namah Shivaya*, salutation to Shiva, was chanted in long, melodic sessions. *So'ham*, "I am That," was to be heard silently in the natural rhythm of the breath, *so* on the in-breath and *ham* on the out-breath.

I find little in Muktananda's ideas that cannot be found in older Shaiva and Shakta texts, and he never claimed otherwise. What he changed was the setting. Shaktipat in the older texts is a rare, private event between a guru and a tested disciple. He made it the entry point, available on a weekend to a roomful of strangers, and he wrote a long, candid account of what came after it. That shift, from a secret rite to a public offering, shaped much of how kundalini has been taught in the West since.

## Lineage

Muktananda claimed Nityananda of Ganeshpuri as his guru, and the claim is well supported by the movement's records and by outside observers. His earlier formation was at Siddharudha Swami's ashram in Hubli. Through his successors his line divided into two organisations.

<MiniTree id="muktananda" />

The edge from Nityananda is documented in the sense that Muktananda's long association with him and his receipt of initiation are attested by Siddha Yoga sources, by the Shanti Mandir organisation and by outside writers, though the details of the 1947 initiation come from Muktananda's own account. The Hubli link rests on the movement's biography and is repeated by independent summaries. The meeting with Zipruanna is known from Muktananda's own telling. His tie to Kashmir Shaivism is one of chosen influence. He adopted the system as his philosophy, studied its texts and visited Lakshmanjoo, but he was not initiated into Lakshmanjoo's line. His succession to Chidvilasananda and Swami Nityananda is documented in the movement's records and in press reports of the time.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Receive shaktipat.</strong> In Siddha Yoga the path begins with the guru's initiation, given in person or, the movement says, through the guru's presence at an Intensive. The disciple's kundalini is said to awaken.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Meditate on the Self.</strong> Sit quietly and turn attention inward. Let the awakened energy lead, and watch spontaneous movements, sounds or visions without fear or force.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Repeat the mantra.</strong> Join the inner sound <i>so</i> with the in-breath and <i>ham</i> with the out-breath, or repeat <i>Om Namah Shivaya</i>, silently or aloud.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Chant.</strong> Take part in group chanting of the names of God and, in the morning, the <i>Guru Gita</i>.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Serve.</strong> Do seva, selfless work for the guru and the community, as a way of offering action to God.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>See God in everyone.</strong> Carry the recognition into daily life by treating others, and oneself, as forms of the same consciousness.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

Muktananda taught celibacy for monks and strict discipline for residents, and he was a sannyasi under vows of chastity. From 1981 onwards former close followers accused him of breaking those vows with women and girls in his ashrams, and of tolerating intimidation and violence by his staff. No criminal charge against him is reported in the sources consulted for this chapter, and no court ruled on these accusations in his lifetime. The accounts below are allegations made by named and anonymous former followers; the organisation has denied them.

**The 1981 open letter.** In 1981 Stan Trout, an American who had been a Siddha Yoga swami under the name Swami Abhayananda, mailed an open letter to Siddha Yoga ashrams accusing Muktananda of sexual activity with young women and girls in his ashrams and saying that people who talked about it had been harassed and threatened. Muktananda answered with a memo, quoted later by William Rodarmor, saying that "devotees should know the truth by their own experience, not by the letters that they receive."

**Rodarmor, 1983.** The fullest early account was William Rodarmor's article "The Secret Life of Swami Muktananda," published in the Winter 1983 issue of *CoEvolution Quarterly*. Rodarmor, then a journalism student at Berkeley, quoted named former insiders, among them Michael Dinga, a former head of construction and foundation trustee, and Chandra Dinga, a former head of food services, along with several women under pseudonyms. They alleged that Muktananda had sexual contact with many female devotees, some of them minors; that his staff beat or threatened people who displeased him or left; that guns were kept in ashrams; and that large sums were held in undisclosed Swiss accounts. Rodarmor also gave the other side. Asked about sexual contact with women in the ashram, Swami Chidvilasananda told him, "Not as far as I saw," and of the girls, "Those girls never came to us." She said of the finances, "We are a break-even proposition." Rodarmor reported that the foundation's head, Ed Oliver, had acknowledged a Swiss account of 1.5 million dollars in an interview with the *Los Angeles Times* on 1 October 1983. Two swamis and a number of current followers told Rodarmor the charges were not true.

***The New Yorker*, 1994.** Lis Harris's long article "O Guru, Guru, Guru," in *The New Yorker* of 14 November 1994, was a long report on Gurumayi's movement, including the ouster of her brother, that repeated and extended Rodarmor's allegations against Muktananda. It brought the accusations to a much wider readership. John Chambers, whose 1979 stay opens this chapter, first learned of them from it. He later judged that Muktananda's life was driven by a desire to help people and called the late indiscretions forgivable.

**A scholar's reading.** In 2001 the anthropologist Sarah Caldwell, a former Siddha Yoga student, argued in the journal *Nova Religio* that Muktananda's conduct could be read in the light of secret Tantric sexual rites. Her thesis was that "Baba was an initiated Kaula practitioner," Kaula being a school of Tantra with sexual ritual. She also wrote that his practices seemed to retain only "the bodily shell of a Tantric practice," and that he had engaged in actions that were not ethical, legal or liberatory with many disciples. The organisation has not accepted that any misconduct took place.

**Organisational responses.** The SYDA Foundation has consistently rejected these accounts. When *Salon* reported the allegations again in 2010, the foundation's board of trustees said it "emphatically rejects the grossly false picture" drawn by the magazine, called Siddha Yoga "an authentic path of self-knowledge," and said the writer relied on rumours and accusations.

**The 2023 civil suit.** On 22 November 2023, in the closing days of the one-year filing window opened by New York's Adult Survivors Act, three women sued the SYDA Foundation in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, Sullivan County (*Doe, Bridges and Stern v. SYDA Foundation*, Index No. E2023-1840). The complaint brought civil claims of negligence and negligent retention and supervision against the foundation, the only defendant named in it, and sought compensatory and punitive damages. It alleged that Muktananda sexually abused and raped the plaintiffs, one from the age of fourteen in 1977 into 1981 and two in about 1980, at South Fallsburg among other ashrams, one of them when she was nineteen, and that the foundation knew or should have known and failed to protect them. In its answer of April 2024 the foundation denied the core allegations: it denied that it controlled Muktananda or that he was its employee or agent, denied that it had information of the alleged misuse or acted with reckless disregard for the plaintiffs' safety, and raised defences including the First Amendment and a challenge to the constitutionality of the Adult Survivors Act. These are allegations and denials in civil pleadings; they were not tested at trial. In March 2025 the action was discontinued by a stipulation of the parties, according to Leaving Siddha Yoga, a website run by former members that links the filing; that site infers, without being sure, that the case was settled. A former resident, the writer Blair Glaser, wrote in March 2026 that the case settled in March 2025 and that the ashram still denies any wrongdoing. No terms have been made public.

**Questions that remain.** Former members such as the writer Marta Szabo and the psychoanalyst Daniel Shaw have published accounts of life in Siddha Yoga that describe a culture of obedience in which complaints were discouraged. Many other students describe the same ashrams as places of real discipline and transformation, and some, like Glaser, describe both at once. The question the chapter leaves open is the one these accounts pose, about how a teaching that says God dwells in everyone sat beside the conduct alleged against its teacher.

## Descendants

Swami Chidvilasananda, known as Gurumayi, has led the SYDA Foundation and Siddha Yoga alone since November 1985. Swami Nityananda announced on 3 November 1985 that Muktananda had privately told the two successors he would serve as guru for three years; he left the role, was released from his vows for a time, and later took vows again and founded his own organisation, Shanti Mandir. Its own site places the founding three years after Muktananda's death, about 1985; other sources give 1987. Several of Muktananda's Western students later founded their own lines, among them the American-born Swami Shankarananda, who founded the Shiva Ashram in Melbourne in 1991. The organisation says Muktananda established more than six hundred meditation centres and several ashrams. Through these groups, and through books such as *Play of Consciousness*, the idea of shaktipat as a public initiation has spread well beyond the movement he founded.
