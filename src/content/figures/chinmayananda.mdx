## Life

Balakrishna Menon was born in Ernakulam, in the princely state of Cochin, on 8 May 1916. His father, Kutta Menon, was a judge. His mother died when he was young and an aunt helped raise him. He studied in Kochi and Thrissur and then at Lucknow University from 1940, where he took postgraduate degrees in English literature and law and courses in journalism.

During the Quit India movement of 1942 he wrote and distributed leaflets, went into hiding when a warrant was issued, and was arrested and jailed in Punjab in 1944. The mission's biography says he came out of prison seriously ill. He then worked as a journalist, beginning at the *National Herald*, the newspaper Jawaharlal Nehru had founded, and wrote on socialism and social questions.

In 1947, by his own later account, he went to Swami Sivananda's Divine Life Society in Rishikesh intending to write an article exposing the holy men. He stayed. On Mahashivaratri, 25 February 1949, Sivananda gave him sannyasa, the vows of a renunciant monk, and the name Swami Chinmayananda Saraswati. He then went to Swami Tapovan Maharaj, a monk from Kerala who lived at Uttarkashi, higher up the Ganga, and under him studied the Gita, the Upanishads and the Brahma Sutras for about two years.

In December 1951 he gave his first public lecture series, which he called a *jnana yajna*, at a temple in Pune. He spoke in English to audiences of office workers, lawyers and students, and went from city to city doing the same. Chinmaya Mission was formed on 8 August 1953, when listeners in Madras asked for a permanent body for study. In 1963 he opened Sandeepany Sadhanalaya at Powai in Bombay, a residential school where students spent several years studying Vedanta and Sanskrit and then went out as teachers. Its first batch had thirty students. He began teaching abroad in 1965 and from then on travelled almost continuously. He died in San Diego on 3 August 1993, and was buried at the mission's ashram at Sidhbari, near Dharamshala.

## The teaching

Chinmayananda taught Shankara's Advaita. The self is pure consciousness, identical with Brahman, the reality behind everything; suffering comes from mistaking oneself for the body and the mind. The doctrine was classical; his changes were to the audience and the method.

**Vedanta for householders.** In the tradition he entered, the Upanishads with Shankara's commentaries were studied in Sanskrit by monks. He insisted that this knowledge was meant for everyone and could be learned in English by people with jobs and families. The mission's statement of purpose still reads "to provide to individuals from any background, the wisdom of Vedanta and the practical means for spiritual growth." He organised local study groups that read his commentaries week by week, and youth and children's programmes, so that study became a regular part of lay life.

**The BMI chart.** His best-known teaching device was a diagram of the person as body, mind and intellect, each with its own field of experience, all of them lit by consciousness. Unchecked desires and habits, which he called *vasanas*, drive the mind; the work of the student is to strengthen the intellect so that it governs the mind, and finally to recognise the consciousness that witnesses all three. He used the chart to explain the Gita's teaching of action without attachment, and much of his popular appeal came from presenting Vedanta as a method for steadier, more effective living as well as a path to liberation.

**Hindu revival.** He treated the ignorance of educated Hindus about their own scriptures as the main problem of modern Hinduism. In August 1964 he hosted at Sandeepany the founding meeting of the Vishva Hindu Parishad (VHP), organised with S. S. Apte of the RSS, and served as its first president. He is quoted as saying at the time, "Let us convert Hindus to Hinduism, then everything will be all right." The VHP later led the campaign for a Ram temple at Ayodhya; in January 1993, a month after the Babri Masjid was demolished, the Times News Service reported him supporting the temple. Researchers at the University of Münster note that the Chinmaya Mission as an institution stayed out of the Ayodhya conflict.

On the question of this part, his emphasis differed from Ramakrishna's. He spoke of Vedanta as a universal science of the self that people of any background could study, and the mission's own publications place most of his effort in teaching Hindus their own scriptures.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="chinmayananda" />

Two documented edges lead to him. Sivananda gave him sannyasa in 1949, which places him in the Saraswati order of Dashanami monks, and Tapovan Maharaj taught him the texts at Uttarkashi; the mission honours both as its gurus. Through Sandeepany he trained most of the next generation, including Swami Dayananda Saraswati, who took sannyasa from him in 1962 and later founded Arsha Vidya, and Swami Tejomayananda, who succeeded him as head of the mission.
