## Life

Balakrishna Menon was born in Ernakulam, in the princely state of Cochin, on 8 May 1916. His father, Kutta Menon, was a judge. His mother died when he was young and an aunt helped raise him. He studied in Kochi and Thrissur and then at Lucknow University from 1940, where he took postgraduate degrees in English literature and law and courses in journalism.

During the Quit India movement of 1942 he wrote and distributed leaflets. He then worked as a journalist, beginning at the *National Herald*, the newspaper Jawaharlal Nehru had founded, and wrote on socialism and social questions.

In 1947, by his own later account, he went to Swami Sivananda's Divine Life Society in Rishikesh intending to write an article exposing the holy men. The mission's biography dates the visit to September 1947, and Wikipedia to the summer of that year. He stayed, and in 1948 made the pilgrimage to the four sacred sites of the Himalaya known as the Char Dham. On Mahashivaratri, 25 February 1949, Sivananda gave him sannyasa, the vows of a renunciant monk, and the name Swami Chinmayananda Saraswati. He then went to Swami Tapovan Maharaj, a monk from Kerala who lived at Uttarkashi, higher up the Ganga, and under him studied the Gita, the Upanishads and the Brahma Sutras. The mission says he spent more than two years in study, living in a cowshed with a stone for a pillow. He later wrote of Tapovan, "A God without temples, a Veda without language was this master mine." Tapovan kept in touch by handwritten postcards and sent his blessing to the first lecture series.

In December 1951 he gave his first public lecture series, which he called a *jnana yajna*, "knowledge sacrifice," at a Ganesh temple in the Rasta Peth quarter of Pune. The series opened on 31 December. By his own account the audience on the first day was eighteen, and within a month it was four or five hundred. The talks covered the nature of religion and Vedanta, and morning classes from six to seven took up Shankara's commentary on the Upanishads. Summaries of the talks were printed in booklets and distributed. He spoke in English. He went from city to city doing the same.

Chinmaya Mission was formed on 8 August 1953, when listeners in Madras asked for a permanent body for study. In 1963 he opened Sandeepany Sadhanalaya at Powai in Bombay, a residential school where students spent several years studying Vedanta and Sanskrit and then went out as teachers. Its first batch had thirty students.

He began teaching abroad in 1965, with a first tour from 6 March that took in thirty-nine cities in eighteen countries, and from then on travelled almost continuously. He led a prayer at an interfaith seminar at Harvard in 1971, and in December 1992 addressed the United Nations. In 1980, on a teaching visit to the United States, he had heart bypass surgery in Texas.

He fell ill with breathing trouble in San Diego on 26 July 1993, underwent quintuple bypass surgery on 29 July, and died on 3 August. His body was flown to India and laid to rest at the mission's ashram at Sidhbari, near Dharamshala, on 7 August. He was scheduled to attend the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago that month.

## The teaching

Chinmayananda taught Shankara's Advaita. The self is pure consciousness, identical with Brahman, the reality behind everything; suffering comes from mistaking oneself for the body and the mind. The doctrine was classical, and he changed the audience and the method.

**Vedanta for householders.** In the tradition he entered, the Upanishads with Shankara's commentaries were studied in Sanskrit by monks. He held that this knowledge was meant for everyone and could be learned in English by people with jobs and families. The mission's statement of purpose still reads "to provide to individuals from any background, the wisdom of Vedanta and the practical means for spiritual growth." He organised local study groups that read his commentaries week by week, including groups for women, and youth and children's programmes (the mission's Bala Vihar and Yuva Kendra), so that study became a regular part of lay life. Wikipedia counts ninety-five publications under his name, among them forty commentaries. *The Holy Geeta* appeared in 1960.

**The BMI chart.** His best-known teaching device was a diagram of the person as body, mind and intellect, each with its own field of experience, all of them lit by consciousness. Unchecked desires and habits, which he called *vasanas*, drive the mind; the work of the student is to strengthen the intellect so that it governs the mind, and finally to recognise the consciousness that witnesses all three. In the introduction to *The Holy Geeta* he describes the mind as an outer part facing objects, the *manas*, and an inner part that decides, the *buddhi*, and says that selfless action wears down the *vasanas*. He wanted the Gita read closely, stanza by stanza, as a handbook for living Vedanta in daily life, and he used the chart to explain its teaching of action without attachment. Much of his popular appeal came from presenting Vedanta as a method for steadier, more effective living as well as a path to liberation.

**Hindu revival.** He treated the ignorance of educated Hindus about their own scriptures as the main problem of modern Hinduism. Researchers at the University of Münster sum up his reform programme as "converting Hindus to Hinduism."

On the question of this part, his emphasis differed from Ramakrishna's. He spoke of Vedanta as a universal science of the self that people of any background could study, and the mission's own publications place most of his effort in teaching Hindus their own scriptures.

## Lineage

<MiniTree id="chinmayananda" />

Two documented edges lead to him. Sivananda gave him sannyasa in 1949, which places him in the Saraswati order of Dashanami monks, and Tapovan Maharaj taught him the texts at Uttarkashi; the mission honours both as its gurus. Through Sandeepany he trained most of the next generation, including Swami Dayananda Saraswati, who took sannyasa from him in 1962 and later founded Arsha Vidya, and Swami Tejomayananda, who succeeded him as head of the mission.

## The practice

His method was study before sitting. Anyone with a copy of his commentary and a few friends can try the pattern that mission students follow.

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Read one text slowly.</strong> Take the Gita with a commentary and go through it a few stanzas at a time, as his introduction advises.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Meet weekly.</strong> The mission's study groups gather to read and discuss his commentaries.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Map yourself on the chart.</strong> Notice what belongs to the body, to the feeling mind and to the deciding intellect, and ask what is aware of all three.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Act without clutching.</strong> In his reading of the Gita, work done for its own sake gradually exhausts the accumulated impressions he called vasanas.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Go further with a teacher.</strong> For those who want to continue, the mission offers residential study at Sandeepany.</div></li>
</ol>
