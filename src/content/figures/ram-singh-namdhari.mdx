## Life

Ram Singh was born on 3 February 1816 at Bhaini Araian in the Ludhiana district of the Punjab, the eldest child of Jassa Singh and Sada Kaur, a family of carpenters. At about twenty he joined the army of Maharaja Ranjit Singh. In 1841, on service toward Peshawar, he met Balak Singh, a teacher at Hazro, in what is now Attock district of Pakistan. Britannica describes Balak Singh as a man who "did not believe in any religious ritual other than the repetition of God's name." Sources give his birth as 1785, 1797 or 1799, and he died in 1862. The Namdhari account says Balak Singh initiated Ram Singh and passed him the spiritual mantle while he was still a soldier.

After the Anglo-Sikh wars Ram Singh went home and farmed and kept a shop. In the words of the *Encyclopaedia of Sikhism*, "On Baisakhi day, 14 April 1857, he laid down the code of conduct for his followers." (The Tribune gives 12 April.) The movement says he initiated five Sikhs that day and raised a white triangular flag. The code forbade meat, alcohol and the worship of tombs. His village became Sri Bhaini Sahib, still the movement's centre.

The British confined him to the village in 1863. The crisis came over cows. In June 1871 Kukas attacked butchers in Amritsar and killed four people, and three more were killed at Raikot. On 14 and 15 January 1872 a band of Kukas, counted at between one and several hundred in different accounts, raided the fort at Malodh and then the town of Malerkotla, capital of a small Muslim-ruled state. On 17 January the Deputy Commissioner, J.L. Cowan, had forty-nine prisoners tied to the mouths of cannon on the parade ground and blown apart without trial. Sixteen more died the same way the next day. Namdharis commemorate sixty-six dead, Britannica says about sixty-five, and the *Encyclopaedia of Sikhism* gives sixty-eight. An inquiry removed Cowan and the divisional commissioner, T.D. Forsyth, from their posts.

Ram Singh had warned the authorities that trouble was coming. He was taken from the Punjab on 18 January 1872, held at Allahabad and sent to Rangoon, detained without trial under Bengal Regulation III of 1818, and later moved to Mergui on the southern coast of Burma. British records say he died there on 29 November 1885. The historian Jaswinder Singh has questioned that record, citing a letter of August 1886 about moving him to a more remote place. Namdharis hold that he did not die and that he will return.

## The teaching

Almost all of Ram Singh's teaching turned on the Name. The Sikh Gurus taught *nam simran*, the steady remembrance of the divine Name, and Balak Singh had made it nearly the whole of religion. Ram Singh gave it a fixed form. An initiate receives the gurmantar, the name *Vaheguru*, spoken into the ear, and repeats it on a rosary of white wool. The movement's site puts the rule plainly: "One hour of meditation on the Name of the Lord is compulsory for every individual Namdhari."

Here the Name is a word received from the Guru and repeated. The sources consulted describe no map of inner regions and no sound current followed upward of the kind the Radhasoami teachers in this part described. For Ram Singh the way home ran through constant remembrance and a disciplined life.

The discipline was visible. Namdharis wear white handwoven cloth and a white turban tied straight across the forehead, keep a strict vegetarian diet, and honour the Dasam Granth equally with the Guru Granth Sahib. Their worship includes a fire ceremony, the *havan*. Their ecstatic cries during hymn singing, *kuk* in Punjabi, gave them the name Kuka. From 1863 he held simple, inexpensive weddings, often many at once, with the couple circling the fire as hymns were sung. He spoke against dowry, child marriage, female infanticide and the sale of girls, and gave initiation to women. He also told followers to stay away from British courts, schools, goods and service, and Britannica calls him the first Indian to use non-cooperation and boycott as a political weapon.

**The living Guru.** Namdharis believe that Guru Gobind Singh lived on in seclusion until 1812 and passed the guruship to Balak Singh, and that Ram Singh succeeded him. According to the movement, Ram Singh named his younger brother, Hari Singh, to lead in 1875. Partap Singh followed in 1906 and Jagjit Singh in 1959. After Jagjit Singh died on 13 December 2012, his nephew Uday Singh became head on 22 December, appointed, The Tribune reported, by Jagjit Singh's widow, Chand Kaur. A faction at Sirsa led by Uday Singh's elder brother, Thakur Dalip Singh, rejects that succession, and the two groups have clashed. Chand Kaur was shot dead at Bhaini Sahib in April 2016, and the Central Bureau of Investigation later took up the case. Namdharis call each of these leaders Satguru while holding that Ram Singh himself is alive.

**The dispute with the Sikh establishment.** Mainstream Sikhs hold that Guru Gobind Singh vested the guruship in the Guru Granth Sahib and the community. The Sikh Rehat Maryada, the code issued by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, defines a Sikh as one who believes in the ten Gurus "from Guru Nanak Dev to Guru Gobind Singh" and in the Guru Granth Sahib. On that view Ram Singh can be honoured as a reformer and martyr, and cannot be a Guru. In October 2013 the jathedar of the Akal Takht reserved the title *Patshahi* for the Gurus, and SikhNet reported that Namdhari heads had been using it. Namdharis call themselves the Sant Khalsa and rest their line on the succession of 1812.
