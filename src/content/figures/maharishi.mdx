<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> Maharishi means great seer. It is a title, and followers use it alone, as a name. Mahesh was his given name, and Yogi marks him as a man of meditation. He never became a sannyasi. While he served his teacher he was known as Bal Brahmachari Mahesh, a celibate student.</aside>

<p>It is the evening of 24 August 1967, in a function room at the London Hilton on Park Lane. A small Indian teacher with long hair and a beard is giving a public lecture on a meditation that, he says, anyone can learn. In the audience are George Harrison and his wife Pattie, John Lennon, Paul McCartney and Jane Asher. Ringo Starr has stayed at home, because his son was born five days earlier.</p>
<p>After the lecture the Beatles ask to see him, and he gives them a private audience of about ninety minutes. He invites them to a course he is about to hold in Bangor, in north Wales. The next day they board a train at Euston with Mick Jagger and Marianne Faithfull. Cynthia Lennon is held back by a security guard who takes her for a fan, and the train leaves without her. On 27 August, in Bangor, the Beatles learn that their manager, Brian Epstein, has been found dead in London. He would later describe them as <q>such practical and intelligent young boys</q>. The meeting made him famous in the West.</p>

## Life

The details of his early life are uncertain, partly because he declined to discuss them. He was born Mahesh Prasad Varma; some sources use the surname Srivastava, which appears among his relatives. Most accounts, including *Britannica*, place his birth in Jabalpur in the Central Provinces, now Madhya Pradesh, though other places in the province are also named. The movement keeps 12 January as his birthday. The year is given as 1917 by the Associated Press, which added that he refused to confirm it, and with a question mark by *Britannica*; as 1918 in many movement sources; and as about 1911 in at least one reference work, which gives the date as 18 October. His father, Ram Prasad, is described as a local tax official, though one source says he worked in forestry. Most sources say the family were Kayasthas, a caste of scribes and clerks, and that fact later mattered.

He studied physics at Allahabad University and took his degree in 1942. By then he had found his teacher. In April 1941 Swami Brahmananda Saraswati was installed as Shankaracharya of Jyotirmath, the northern monastery that tradition traces to the eighth-century philosopher Shankara. He had been born Rajaram Mishra in a village near Ayodhya (in 1868, 1870 or 1871, depending on the source) and had taken sannyasa in 1904. Disciples called him Guru Dev, divine teacher.

Mahesh became his secretary, by most accounts in 1941, and stayed with him until Brahmananda's death in 1953, about thirteen years in all. He took the name Bal Brahmachari Mahesh. He was not a Brahmin, so he could not be considered for the Shankaracharya's seat, and he never claimed it. After Brahmananda died he went to Uttarkashi, in the Himalayan foothills, and spent about two years in retreat.

<aside class="note"><strong>Shankaracharya.</strong> The head of one of the monasteries that tradition says Shankara founded at the four corners of India. The office is held by a sannyasi of the Brahmin caste, which is why Mahesh, a Kayastha and a brahmachari, could serve his teacher but never succeed him.</aside>

In 1955 he came down to the south and began to teach in Kerala. He called the method Transcendental Deep Meditation and presented it as Guru Dev's teaching. His first booklet, *Beacon Light of the Himalayas*, appeared that year. At a seminar of spiritual leaders in Madras he inaugurated the Spiritual Regeneration Movement; the movement's own reference works date this to 31 December 1957, and some accounts place it in 1958. He then set off round the world. The first tour took him through Burma, Thailand, Malaya, Singapore and Hong Kong to Hawaii, and in 1959 he taught in several American cities and in London. In the United States the method took the shorter name Transcendental Meditation, and the International Meditation Society was formed in 1959.

The movement grew by training teachers. In 1961 he ran the first teacher training course in Rishikesh, with about sixty participants. His ashram there, on the slope above the Ganga, later became famous. In 1963 he published *Science of Being and Art of Living*, his main statement of the teaching. In 1966 he founded the Students' International Meditation Society to reach American campuses, and in 1967 he published a translation and commentary on the first six chapters of the *Bhagavad Gita*.

The Beatles brought him celebrity. In February 1968 they went to Rishikesh with their wives and partners, along with Donovan, Mike Love of the Beach Boys and the actress Mia Farrow. Ringo Starr left after about ten days and Paul McCartney some weeks later. John Lennon and George Harrison left together on 12 April, after Alexis Mardas, an electronics engineer in the Beatles' circle, claimed that the teacher had sought sexual favours from women on the course. The episode is set out in the last section of this chapter. By one count the band wrote about forty songs in Rishikesh, many of which appeared on *The Beatles*, known as the White Album, and on *Abbey Road*.

He turned away from celebrity soon after. He announced that he would concentrate on training teachers, moved his headquarters to Seelisberg in Switzerland, and held large residential courses in Maine and California in 1970. That year Robert Keith Wallace, a young physiology researcher who practised TM, published a study of meditators' metabolism and breathing in *Science*, and from then on the movement presented TM as a technique validated by research. Maharishi International University was founded in 1971, held its first classes in Santa Barbara, California, in 1973, and moved in 1974 to Fairfield, Iowa, which became the movement's main American centre. In 1975 he declared the Dawn of the Age of Enlightenment and toured five continents. An appearance on Merv Griffin's television talk show in April 1975 brought a wave of new students; *Christianity Today* reported in 1976 that more than 700,000 Americans had been taught since 1965, and that about 30,000 a month were then being initiated.

In 1975 or 1976 (sources differ) he introduced the TM-Sidhi programme, an advanced course built on Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*, including a practice he called Yogic Flying. In 1977 a federal court in New Jersey ruled that a TM course in public high schools was religious teaching. Both episodes are covered below.

From the late 1970s the movement bought property in Europe, America and India, and its programmes multiplied: a system of health care marketed as Maharishi Ayur-Veda, a school of architecture, organic farming and schools. Deepak Chopra, an endocrinologist in Massachusetts, met him in 1985, directed the movement's Ayurvedic centre at Lancaster, Massachusetts, and helped popularise this side of the work before parting from him in the early 1990s. A tax dispute in India in 1988 preceded his departure from the country, and in 1990 he settled in Vlodrop, in the Dutch province of Limburg, in a former monastery that became his last home. He rarely appeared in public after 1991, the year Chopra later said he was seriously ill, and afterwards spoke to his followers mostly by video link.

In 1992 the movement launched the Natural Law Party, which put up candidates in dozens of countries. George Harrison played a concert at the Royal Albert Hall on 6 April 1992 in support of its British campaign, three days before the general election. The physicist John Hagelin ran for President of the United States on its ticket in 1992, 1996 and 2000. In 2000 Maharishi founded the Global Country of World Peace, a country without territory with its own officers and its own currency, and named the physician and neuroscientist Tony Nader its head, with the title Maharaja Adhiraj Rajaraam. In May 2002 he gave his first mainstream American television interview in many years to Larry King, speaking by video from Vlodrop.

In January 2008 he announced that he was stepping down and would retire into silence. <q>I think I have done whatever can be done,</q> he said. He died at Vlodrop on the evening of 5 February 2008, of natural causes according to his spokesman, Bob Roth. His body was flown to his ashram at Allahabad, where thousands filed past it, and on 11 February he was cremated at the Sangam, where the Ganga and the Yamuna meet, in the presence of about 2,000 followers from abroad and some 5,000 local people. Tony Nader, as Maharaja Adhiraj Rajaraam, took over leadership of the organisations.

## The teaching

Maharishi's teaching rests on one idea from Advaita Vedanta, the non-dual philosophy of Shankara's tradition. Beneath all thought and all things there is one unchanging reality. He called it Being, a word he used where the Upanishads say Brahman or Atman. Being is pure consciousness, without content, and it is the source of everything that changes. *Science of Being and Art of Living* describes it as the transcendental field of existence and says that anyone can experience it directly.

**The mind goes on its own.** His central claim about method was that the mind, left to itself, moves toward greater happiness. Being is the source of happiness, so a mind that is given the right conditions will settle by its own tendency toward quieter and subtler levels of thought, until it goes beyond thought altogether. The meditator does not have to concentrate, control the mind or push thoughts away. The meditator repeats a mantra silently and easily, lets the mantra fade into fainter and fainter forms, and returns to it gently when other thoughts come. In the 2002 interview he described the whole thing as <q>a means to do what one wants to do in a better way</q>, and added: <q>There is nothing difficult, there is nothing complicated.</q>

**The mantra as sound.** The mantras are short Sanskrit sounds chosen by the teacher. He explained their effect in terms of vibration, telling a gathering in Kerala in 1955 that <q>the theory of Mantras is the theory of sound</q>, and taught that the sound had to be suited to the person. Former teachers and critics say that in practice mantras were assigned from a short list according to age and sex. In the movement's later teaching the meaning of the mantra plays no part in the practice. Scholars have pointed out that the sounds come from the stock of bija, or seed, mantras used in Tantric and devotional practice, where they are linked to particular deities; that question returns in the last section.

**Stress and the body.** He taught that the deep rest of meditation releases accumulated stress from the nervous system. This gave the teaching a physiological language from the start, and it made the technique easy to study. Followers measured oxygen consumption, skin resistance and brain waves, and Maharishi welcomed their papers as evidence that an ancient state of consciousness could be shown on instruments.

**Seven states of consciousness.** He described growth as a sequence. Waking, dreaming and deep sleep are the three ordinary states. The fourth, transcendental consciousness, is the silent state touched in meditation, the *turiya* of the Upanishads. When that silence persists through waking, dreaming and sleep, he called it cosmic consciousness. A further refinement of perception, in which the finest levels of creation are seen, he called God consciousness. The last, unity consciousness, is the state in which everything is experienced as one with the Self. The scheme reads as a modern restatement of Advaita, ordered as stages a practitioner could expect to pass through.

**Action without renunciation.** His reading of the *Bhagavad Gita* gave this a practical turn. He read Krishna's teaching to Arjuna as instruction to settle the mind in Being first and then act, so that action itself becomes effortless and successful. His publishers described the commentary as designed to <q>restore the fundamental truths</q> of the Gita. This meant a householder needed no renunciation, no change of diet or belief and no special posture. Twenty minutes in the morning and twenty in the evening, sitting comfortably in a chair, was the whole of the daily practice.

**Science of Creative Intelligence.** From the early 1970s he presented the teaching under a second name, the Science of Creative Intelligence, and taught it as an academic subject. In later years he drew on the language of physics, identifying Being with what physicists call the unified field. An Associated Press obituary summed up his claim for TM as access to <q>the unified field of all the laws of nature</q>. Hagelin, trained in particle physics, developed this identification at length.

**The Maharishi Effect.** He also taught that meditation affects society. In 1960, according to movement sources, he predicted that when one per cent of a population practised TM, the whole population would benefit. After the TM-Sidhi programme began, the figure became the square root of one per cent, provided that those practising the advanced techniques did so together in one place. The movement calls this the Maharishi Effect and has organised large group meditations to produce it, from campaigns for world peace to the 1993 project in Washington, D.C., discussed below.

I find it hard to name an idea in Maharishi's teaching that Shankara's tradition would not recognise. What I cannot find before him is the package. He took one method, made it the same in every country, trained thousands of teachers to give it word for word, priced it, and asked laboratories to measure it. The claim that meditation should be effortless is his strongest contribution as a teacher, and it has outlived the movement's more extravagant claims. The Maharishi Effect and Yogic Flying belong to a different order of claim, and they have done more than anything else to make scientists suspicious of the research he was proudest of.

## Lineage

Maharishi's only teacher was Brahmananda Saraswati, and through him he claimed the line of Shankara. He was a disciple and secretary, without monastic initiation, and his authority came from that personal service.

<MiniTree id="maharishi" />

The edge from Brahmananda is documented by the movement and by biographers, and Brahmananda's picture stands at the centre of every TM initiation. The edge to Sri Sri Ravi Shankar is documented: he worked and travelled with Maharishi from the mid-1970s until about 1980–81. Deepak Chopra's years in the movement, from 1985 to the early 1990s, are documented by Chopra himself and by scholars. Tony Nader succeeded to the leadership through Maharishi's own appointment, an institutional edge.

## The practice

<ol class="steps">
  <li><div><strong>Introductory lecture.</strong> A free public talk on the benefits claimed for TM, followed by a second lecture on how the technique works.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Personal interview.</strong> A short private meeting with the certified teacher who will give the instruction.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Initiation.</strong> The student brings flowers, fruit and a white handkerchief. The teacher performs a short ceremony in Sanskrit before a picture of Brahmananda Saraswati, honouring the line of masters, then gives the mantra the teacher has chosen and teaches the student to use it.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Checking.</strong> Three group sessions on following days, in which the teacher answers questions and corrects mistakes. Students are asked to keep the mantra private.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Daily practice.</strong> Sit comfortably with eyes closed for about twenty minutes, morning and late afternoon, and think the mantra without effort, coming back to it easily when the mind wanders.</div></li>
  <li><div><strong>Advanced courses.</strong> Long-term meditators may take the TM-Sidhi programme, which adds mental repetition of sutras drawn from Patanjali, including the Yogic Flying technique, practised in groups.</div></li>
</ol>

## Critiques and questions

**The research programme.** No meditation has been studied more than TM, and the movement says the literature runs to hundreds of peer-reviewed papers from a wide range of institutions. A large share of that work was done by scientists at the movement's own university or by researchers who practise TM, and critics have argued that this weakens it. Independent reviews have been mixed. A 2014 meta-analysis in *JAMA Internal Medicine* by Madhav Goyal and colleagues, covering 47 trials of meditation programmes, found moderate evidence that mindfulness programmes helped with anxiety, depression and pain, and low or insufficient evidence of effect for mantra-based programmes, TM among them. In 2013 a scientific statement from the American Heart Association on alternatives to drugs for high blood pressure said that TM <q>may be considered</q> in clinical practice, a weak recommendation, and rated other kinds of meditation lower. In June 2011 the *Archives of Internal Medicine* pulled a TM heart study led by Robert Schneider of the movement's university twelve minutes before its scheduled online publication, saying it needed time to review additional data the authors had sent the day before. A revised version appeared in *Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes* in November 2012, with changed event counts. Its authors reported a 48 per cent lower risk of death, heart attack or stroke combined among the meditators; critics said the result was too large for a trial of about 200 patients. The movement's position is that the results have been replicated many times and published in mainstream journals.

**The Maharishi Effect.** From 7 June to 30 July 1993 about 4,000 practitioners of the TM-Sidhi programme gathered in Washington, D.C. A paper by Hagelin and colleagues, published in *Social Indicators Research* in 1999, reported that homicides, rapes and aggravated assaults fell by up to 23.3 per cent below the level their model predicted, controlling for temperature, policing and earlier trends. Critics replied that the theory has no plausible mechanism and that the analysis depends on a predicted baseline. The physicist Robert Park called one Maharishi Effect study a <q>clinic in data distortion</q>, and Evan Fales and Barry Markovsky argued that the theory does not meet minimal standards of meaningfulness. In 1994 Hagelin was given a satirical Ig Nobel Peace Prize for the Washington claim. The movement continues to cite the study and others like it.

**Religion or technique.** The movement has always said that TM involves no religious belief. In 1976 a professor at its university told *Christianity Today* that the initiation was <q>not a religious ceremony at all</q>. Christian critics and some scholars have argued the opposite, pointing to the Sanskrit puja before Brahmananda's picture and to the origin of the mantras. In *Malnak v. Yogi* (1977), the United States District Court for the District of New Jersey held that a Science of Creative Intelligence and TM elective taught in five New Jersey high schools in 1975–76 was religious in nature and that teaching it in public schools violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment. The court relied on the textbook and on the puja, in which offerings were made to Guru Dev. The Third Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed in February 1979. On 7 May 2025 the United States District Court for the Northern District of Illinois approved a settlement of 2.6 million dollars, paid by the Chicago Board of Education and the David Lynch Foundation, in a class action over the Quiet Time programme, in which instructors hired by the foundation had taught TM in Chicago public schools. The plaintiffs alleged that the puja and mantras were religious and that the programme violated the Establishment Clause; the settlement contained no admission of liability. Chicago Public Schools had since stopped offering the programme. The foundation did not answer requests for comment when the class was certified in 2024, and its chief executive, Bob Roth, has long maintained that TM is not a religion.

**Yogic Flying in court.** Early advertising for the TM-Sidhi course spoke of powers such as levitation. In practice the flying is a series of hops from the lotus posture, and the movement says floating and flying are later stages; no demonstration has shown more than hopping. Robert Kropinski, a former TM teacher, sued the World Plan Executive Council and Maharishi International University for fraud and negligence, alleging that the movement had promised benefits including the ability to fly. A jury in Washington, D.C., found for him on fraud and negligence after a trial that ran from late 1986 into January 1987, and awarded him 137,890 dollars. On 5 August 1988 the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit dismissed his claim for psychological injury, held that expert testimony on thought reform had been wrongly admitted, and sent the fraud claim (because the jury had been misdirected on the statute of limitations) and the physical-injury claim back for a new trial. No published ruling records a retrial; former members critical of the movement say the case was later settled out of court for an undisclosed sum.

**Money and the Beatles.** Fees rose from about 35 dollars for students in the late 1960s to 2,500 dollars in the United States in the early 2000s, before falling again. In 2008, NPR reported, a spokesman told a journalist that Maharishi's personal fortune might exceed a billion dollars, and a former member said he had seen internal figures for the movement's worth of two to three billion. Maharishi said the fees supported the teaching centres; according to his obituary in <i>The Times</i>, he said the money did not go to him and that he himself had nothing. The Rishikesh allegation of 1968 was never tested. Alexis Mardas made it, and Lennon's own account has Mardas accusing the teacher of improper behaviour with a young American woman. Cynthia Lennon later wrote that she suspected Mardas was <q>being rather mischievous</q> and that the Maharishi was given no chance to reply. Lennon wrote a song about the episode, first titled after Maharishi and then renamed *Sexy Sadie* at Harrison's suggestion, and later said the teacher had seemed too interested in celebrity and money. Harrison later supported the movement in public and played for the Natural Law Party in 1992.

**Orthodox objections.** *Time* and *Newsweek* reported in his lifetime that Indian scholars and holy men criticised his programme as oversimplified and at odds with traditional Hindu belief and practice. Others said his success owed more to business skill than to spiritual teaching. He never claimed a Shankaracharya's authority, and he always presented the technique as his teacher's, handed down from the Vedic tradition.

## Descendants

The organisations he founded continue under Tony Nader and national TM bodies, and the university in Fairfield, known for some years as Maharishi University of Management, is again called Maharishi International University. The David Lynch Foundation, set up by the film director in 2005, teaches TM in schools, to veterans and in prisons. His broader descendants are harder to count. Sri Sri Ravi Shankar's Art of Living took over his model of a single technique in a fixed course, Deepak Chopra built a career on the mind-body language he learned in the movement, and the whole field of meditation research in medicine, much of it now on mindfulness, began in part with the laboratory studies of TM in the early 1970s.
