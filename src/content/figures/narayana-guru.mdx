<aside class="note"><strong>The name.</strong> NAH-rah-yuh-nuh GOO-roo. In Kerala he is usually called Sree Narayana Guru, or simply Guru. He was born into the Ezhava community, a large caste of toddy-tappers, farmers and physicians that the Brahmin order of Travancore treated as polluting.</aside>

<section class="scene">
<p>Aruvippuram, a few kilometres from Neyyattinkara in southern Travancore, on the night of Shivaratri in 1888. The Neyyar river runs between rocks below a small hill. A crowd of devotees has built a canopy on the bank. The accounts, written down later by his followers, agree on the outline of what happened next.</p>
<p>Around midnight the thirty-odd-year-old ascetic they call Nanu Asan walks into the river. He comes out holding a stone from its bed, a rough natural lingam, the emblem of Shiva. In the small hours, around three in the morning according to one account, he sets it on its pedestal and consecrates it. No Brahmin priest takes part. Under the laws and customs of the time, only Brahmins could install a deity, and Ezhavas were barred even from approaching most temples.</p>
<p>Word reached the orthodox, and they asked by what right he had done it. The reply his followers remember has been repeated in Kerala ever since. In one common translation he said, <q>This is not a Brahmin Shiva but an Ezhava Shiva.</q> Another version has him say that it was only a Thiyya, or Ezhava, Shiva that he had consecrated. Later a short Malayalam verse was put up on the temple wall, describing the place as one where people live as brothers, free of caste division and religious hatred.</p>
</section>

## Life

Narayanan, called Nanu at home, was born in Chempazhanthy, a village about twelve kilometres north of Thiruvananthapuram. The year varies with the source. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* and the Narayana Gurukula give 1854, the Sree Narayana Guru Mission gives 28 August 1855, and Kerala Tourism's official site gives 28 August 1856. His birthday is kept on the day of the Chathayam star in the Malayalam month of Chingam, which falls in August or September.

His father, Madan Asan, was a village teacher who knew some Sanskrit, astrology and Ayurveda, and his mother was Kuttiyamma. The family was Ezhava, and in nineteenth-century Travancore that meant real limits. Ezhavas were kept at a fixed distance from upper-caste people on public roads, were kept out of most temples and were largely shut out of Sanskrit learning. Nanu's mother died when he was about fifteen.

He learned his letters from a local schoolmaster, Chempazhanthi Mootha Pillai, and at about twenty-one he went north to Karunagappally to study with Kummampilli Raman Pillai Asan, a Sanskrit scholar who taught the Vedas, the Upanishads, poetry and logic. This was unusual for an Ezhava boy, and it gave him the classical training he later used in his Sanskrit works. On his wanderings he met Kunjan Pillai, later known as **Chattampi Swamikal**, at the house of a physician in Perunnelli. Chattampi introduced him to **Thycaud Ayyavu**, a teacher of hatha yoga, and Nanu learned yoga and meditation from him.

He came home around 1881 when his father fell ill and opened a village school, which gave him the name Nanu Asan, "Nanu the teacher." He was married, to a woman named Kaliamma according to the Sree Narayana Guru Mission. The sources disagree about what followed: the Mission says he soon left the marriage, and *Britannica* says his wife died a few years later. Either way he left home and lived as a wandering ascetic. For several years, eight according to the Mission, he lived in the Pillathadam cave on Maruthwamala, a hill near Kanyakumari, practising meditation and yoga. Kerala Tourism records that he and Chattampi meditated together there. His followers date his realization to this period.

The Aruvippuram consecration in 1888 made him a public figure. Ezhavas and others began to ask him to consecrate temples for them, and over the next forty years he installed images or emblems in dozens of shrines across Kerala, at Thalassery, Kannur, Kozhikode, Thrissur and Anchuthengu, and as far north as Mangalore. In 1903 the physician **P. Palpu**, an Ezhava who had been refused a government post in Travancore, organised the Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana Yogam (SNDP Yogam), registered on 15 May 1903, with the Guru as its president. It became the main organisation of Ezhava social reform.

In 1904 he settled at Sivagiri, a hill near Varkala, and opened a school there that took children of any caste free of charge. A temple to Sarada, the goddess of learning, was completed there in 1912 after seven years of building. In 1913 (1914 in *Britannica*) he founded the Advaita Ashram at Aluva, with the motto *Om Sahodaryam Sarvatra*, roughly "brotherhood everywhere."

His relations with the SNDP Yogam cooled. According to the Narayana Gurukula, he objected that its rules defined its community as the Ezhava, Thiyya and Billava castes, and in 1916 he stepped away from it. A public statement from about this time, as it is usually translated, says that it was years since he had left caste and religion, and that he did not belong to any particular caste or religion.

The same year he travelled to Kancheepuram to lay the foundation of an ashram. On the way back, invited by one of Ramana Maharshi's disciples, he climbed to Skandashram on Arunachala. The two sat together mostly in silence, ate a meal and parted. The Guru then wrote five Sanskrit verses, the *Nirvriti Panchakam*, and had them presented to Ramana. Ramanasramam's own account quotes Ramana as saying afterwards, <q>Guru was a great man. He had nothing to speak with me. He knew everything.</q>

By the 1920s he was the most visited teacher in Kerala. Rabindranath Tagore came to Sivagiri on 15 November 1922 with C.F. Andrews. In the visitors' book Tagore wrote that he had <q>never come across one who is spiritually greater than Swami Narayana Guru</q>. Mahatma Gandhi came on 12 March 1925, during the Vaikom Satyagraha, the campaign to open the roads around the Vaikom temple to lower castes. They discussed untouchability, conversion and the satyagraha itself. Gandhi said afterwards that it had been <q>a great privilege in his life to have the darshan of an esteemed sage like Sree Narayana Guru</q>. They did not fully agree. Gandhi still defended a reformed caste order at the time, and Kerala Tourism records that at Aluva he disputed the "one caste, one religion" part of the Guru's formula.

Aluva hosted a Conference of Universal Brotherhood in 1921 and, in 1924 (1923 in some sources), a conference of all religions, which his followers describe as the first of its kind in India. The Guru proposed a *Brahma Vidyalaya*, a school for the comparative study of religions. The notice at the entrance read, <q>We meet here not to argue and win, but to know and be known.</q>

In his last years his consecrations became more unusual. At Murukkumpuzha he installed a metal arch with *Om* at its centre and four words, *satyam, dharmam, daya, shanti* (truth, right conduct, compassion, peace). In June 1927 at Kalavancode he installed a mirror. He fell ill in 1927 and was treated by Ayurvedic and Western doctors in Aluva, Thrissur, Palakkad and Madras. He died at the Sarada Mutt in Sivagiri on 20 September 1928.

## The teaching

Narayana Guru was an Advaitin. He held that one reality, which he called *arivu* (knowledge, or consciousness) or *karu* (the core, the source), appears as knower, known and the act of knowing, and that the separate self is a misreading of it. He set this out in verse, in Malayalam for ordinary readers and in Sanskrit for the learned, and drew from it a social conclusion that most Advaitins before him had not drawn: if all people are one in essence, a hierarchy of birth has no ground.

### The hundred verses

The *Atmopadesa Satakam*, "One Hundred Verses of Self-Instruction," is his central philosophical work. It is usually dated to around 1897, early in his writing career. The poem is addressed by the poet to himself and opens with a call to bow to the source of knowing. In Guru Nitya Chaitanya Yati's translation the first verse reads, <q>Permeating the knowledge which shines at once within and without the knower is the karu; to that, with the five senses withheld, prostrate again and again with devotion and chant.</q>

<aside class="note"><strong>Karu.</strong> A Malayalam word for core, embryo or mould. The Guru uses it for the one source from which knower and known both arise.</aside>

From there the poem works through the ground of classical Vedanta, but in its own images. Knowledge divides into "this" and "that"; the "this" is the self, the "that" is the world; both are modes of one *arivu*. Many verses turn on everyday perception, a pot, a rope, a dream, the sea and its waves, to show how one thing appears as many. Others are frankly devotional.

The poem also contains an ethic. A well-known group of verses near the middle teaches that what one does for one's own good should also bring good to others, since the "other" and oneself are the same reality. The Guru's followers read this as his version of the golden rule, derived from metaphysics.

Verses 44 to 50, at the exact middle of the hundred, deal with religion. In Vinaya Chaitanya's rendering, they warn against <q>not seeing that the various religions in the world are essentially the same</q>, and compare those who argue over them to the blind men and the elephant, each holding one part and mistaking it for the whole. The advice is to stop the quarrel and settle down. This is the philosophical root of the slogan he is best known for.

### One caste, one religion, one God

The slogan comes from a short Malayalam poem on caste, usually called *Jati Mimamsa*, "An Inquiry into Caste." Its first lines run, in one translation: <q>One in Kind/Caste, One in Faith/Religion and One in God is Man; Of One womb, of One form, difference herein none.</q> In Malayalam the first line is *oru jati, oru matam, oru daivam manushyanu*.

The argument is simple and deliberately biological. In a related poem, *Jati Nirnayam* (Kerala Tourism dates it to 1914), he points out that a species, a *jati*, is recognised by form and by who can breed with whom. Cows are one *jati* and can be told from horses at a glance. Human beings are all born the same way and look alike, so they make one *jati*, and the hereditary castes are a human invention. The word *jati* means both "species" and "caste" in Malayalam and Sanskrit, and the poem turns on that double meaning.

The second and third claims follow from the first. If human beings are one kind, their religions are different paths taken by one kind of being towards one goal, and their gods are names for one reality. The Narayana Gurukula maps the three onto the classical description of Brahman as being, consciousness and bliss. One caste corresponds to being, one religion to consciousness, and the one goal of happiness to bliss. The Guru put the practical upshot in a sentence his followers repeat constantly. In Kerala Tourism's version, <q>Whatever the religion, it is enough if man becomes good.</q>

He did not ask anyone to leave their religion. He advised people to stay in the faith they had and to study the others, and he opposed conversion as a way out of caste, since in his view caste followed converts into their new religion.

### Aruvippuram as philosophy

The 1888 consecration is usually told as an act of protest, and it was one. It also made a philosophical claim. If Shiva is the one reality present in every being, then no caste can own access to him, and the rule that only a Brahmin may install him confuses a social custom with a fact about God. The reply attributed to him, that he had consecrated an Ezhava Shiva, can be read as a joke at the expense of the critics. It concedes their own premise, that gods belong to castes, and turns it against them.

The verse put up on the temple wall states the positive side. In Vinaya Chaitanya's translation it describes the place as one <q>Devoid of dividing walls of caste or race, Or hatred of rival faith</q>, where all live in brotherhood. The temple was meant as a model of how society should be, and the Guru's later consecrations carry the same idea further. The images grew more abstract: a lamp, the syllable *Om*, a plaque of four virtues. At Kalavancode in 1927 he installed a mirror, so that worshippers looking for God at the centre of the shrine saw their own faces. His Advaitin followers read the mirror as a statement that the worshipper and the worshipped are one. He is reported to have said around this time that schools were now to be preferred to temples.

### The prayer

The *Daiva Dasakam*, "Ten Verses on God," written in 1914, is a short Malayalam prayer composed for children to chant, at the Aluva ashram according to Kerala Tourism, or at the evening prayers of orphans at Sivagiri according to the Narayana Gurukula. It is sung daily in thousands of Kerala homes and schools. It asks God to steer the boat of life across the sea of becoming, and pictures the world as waves on an ocean, with God as the depth beneath them. Its language is plain enough for a child, and its view is the same Advaita as the *Atmopadesa Satakam*.

### Darsana Mala

The *Darsana Mala*, "Garland of Visions," is his most systematic work, written in Sanskrit. Ramanasramam's archive notes that some of its verses date from around his 1916 visit to Ramana. It surveys the ways the one reality can be seen, from the creation of the world through illusion, meditation and devotion to final absorption. It was the basis of his disciple Nataraja Guru's long commentary, *An Integrated Science of the Absolute*.

Classical Advaita held that all beings are one Self and still kept caste in the world of practice, on the ground that social rules belong to the level of appearance. Narayana Guru closed that gap. He took the doctrine at its word and applied it to the temple, the school and the dining hall. I find him the most consistent thinker in this part. Vivekananda said many of the same things to larger crowds, but the Guru was himself an Ezhava, and he built what he taught in stone.

## Lineage

Narayana Guru had teachers but no formal initiating guru in a monastic order, and he founded no line of succession in the classical sense. His tree shows the Sanskrit schooling and yoga training above him, and a fellow disciple beside him.

<MiniTree id="narayana-guru" />

His relationship with Chattampi Swamikal is shown as companionship. Both learned from Thycaud Ayyavu, both wandered and meditated together, and they remained close all their lives. When Chattampi died in 1924 the Guru wrote a verse in his memory. Some of Chattampi's followers have described him as the Guru's teacher, and Narayana Guru's followers generally reject this; the two men themselves seem never to have framed it that way.

The line from Ramana is shown dotted. They met once, in 1916, and there is no sign that either changed the other's teaching.

Below him the branches run in three directions. The poet **Kumaran Asan** carried his ideas into Malayalam literature. The SNDP Yogam and the Sivagiri Mutt carry on his institutional work. The most philosophical line runs through **Nataraja Guru** (1895–1973), Dr Palpu's son, who founded the Narayana Gurukula in 1923 and spent his life writing commentaries on the Guru's works, and through Nataraja Guru's disciple **Nitya Chaitanya Yati**, whose commentary on the hundred verses, *That Alone*, is the most widely read in English.

## Critiques and questions

**Was he a Hindu reformer or beyond Hinduism?** This argument is still alive in Kerala politics. On his death anniversary in September 2016 the Bharatiya Janata Party's Kerala unit called him a great Hindu sannyasi. The Chief Minister, Pinarayi Vijayan of the Communist Party of India (Marxist), replied that the Guru had foreseen attempts to lock him into one religion, and cited his statement that he had no caste. Writers on the Hindu side point out that he consecrated Shiva and Murugan, wrote Vedanta and lived as a Hindu ascetic. Writers on the other side point to the 1916 statement and to the mirror. His texts support both readings, since he wrote within Advaita and argued that its truth was the same as that of every religion.

**Reform or caste politics?** Critics on the left have argued that the SNDP Yogam, built in his name, became an organisation for the advancement of one caste, which he himself complained about in 1916. His defenders reply that he cannot be held responsible for what the organisation did after his death, and that he had distanced himself from it while alive.

**Gandhi and the limits of agreement.** The 1925 meeting is now celebrated as a meeting of minds, and the government of India marked its centenary in 2025. At the time the two men differed. Gandhi defended caste as a division of labour to be purged of untouchability, and the Guru held that caste had to go entirely. Kerala Tourism credits the Guru with influencing Gandhi's later, harder position.

**How much is legend?** Most details of the Aruvippuram night, including the exact words of his reply, come from followers' accounts written down later, and they differ in wording. The consecration itself, its date and its effect are well documented.

## Descendants

The Sivagiri Mutt and the Sree Narayana Dharma Sangham, the order of monks he founded, keep up his temples and schools, and the Sivagiri pilgrimage he planned in his last years now draws large crowds each December. The SNDP Yogam remains a major social organisation in Kerala. The Narayana Gurukula of **Nataraja Guru** and **Nitya Chaitanya Yati** spread his philosophy to Europe, the United States and Australia. The poet **Kumaran Asan** made him a presence in modern Malayalam literature. His slogan "one caste, one religion, one God for man" is quoted in Kerala by politicians of every party.
