Every figure in this book was handed something before they added anything. Ramana read the lives of Tamil saints before he ever heard of Shankara. Vivekananda grew up inside a reform movement arguing with missionaries. Krishnamurti was raised by Theosophists who had borrowed half their ideas from India and the other half from Victorian spiritualism. To see what was new in any of them, you need a rough map of what was already there.

This prologue is that map. It is deliberately brief and deliberately simplified. Scholars spend careers on any one of these traditions; the goal here is only to give you enough to recognize the roots when they appear on the tree. Each one is a root node on the lineage graph, and you will see the same names again and again in the chapters that follow.

## One reality: the Upanishads

The oldest layer that matters for this book is the Upanishads, philosophical dialogues composed roughly between 800 and 200 BCE at the end of the Vedas. They are not a system. They are teachers and students, kings and sages, a father and a son, arguing and wondering about what is ultimately real.

Out of that conversation came two ideas that almost every modern figure in this book either builds on or argues with. The first is **Brahman**: a single reality underneath the endless variety of the world. The second is **Atman**: the true self, underneath the body, the senses and the restless mind. The boldest Upanishadic claim is that these two are the same. What you most deeply are and what the universe most deeply is are not two things.

Notice what kind of claim this is. It is not a belief to be accepted but a discovery to be made. The Upanishads are full of methods: questioning, meditation, the patient removal of everything you are not. That emphasis on direct experience, rather than creed, is the thread the nineteenth-century reformers would grab and pull.

## Three readings: Vedanta

Vedanta, "the end of the Vedas," is the name for the schools that tried to make the Upanishads consistent. Three readings dominate.

**Advaita**, "not-two," is associated above all with Shankara, usually dated to the eighth century. It takes the identity of Atman and Brahman literally. Only the one reality truly exists; the world of many separate things is an appearance, like a rope mistaken for a snake in dim light. Liberation is not going anywhere or becoming anything. It is the end of a mistake. Shankara also founded monasteries (*mathas*) whose heads still carry the title Shankaracharya, and one of them appears in Part 2.

**Vishishtadvaita**, "qualified non-dualism," is Ramanuja's answer, from the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Yes, there is one reality, he argued, but it is a personal God, and souls and the world are real as God's body. You are not identical to God; you belong to God. Devotion is not a beginner's step; it is the point.

**Dvaita**, "dualism," was taught by Madhva in the thirteenth century. God, souls and matter are eternally distinct. Liberation is endless closeness to God, not merger.

Most of the modern figures in this book lean toward Advaita, and that is not an accident. Advaita's claim that all paths lead to one reality made it ideal for a modern argument that all religions are one. Keep the other two in mind, though: they come back in force in Part 7, where love of a personal God is the whole story.

## The body as a path: yoga and the Naths

"Yoga" today usually means postures in a studio. Its older meaning is discipline: the practice of stilling the mind. The classic text, Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*, compiled in the early centuries CE, describes an eightfold path of ethics, posture, breath, withdrawal of the senses, concentration and absorption, aimed at isolating pure consciousness from the churn of nature.

A second, more physical tradition grew up in the medieval period among the **Nath** yogis, associated with the semi-legendary Gorakhnath. Hatha yoga, with its postures, breathing methods and talk of energy channels and a dormant power at the base of the spine, comes largely from this world. Its key manual, the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, dates from the fifteenth century.

The Naths matter for this book in two quite different ways. Their techniques were reshaped in the twentieth century into modern postural yoga (Part 5). And one Nath lineage, the Navnath Sampradaya, produced Nisargadatta Maharaj, one of the most radical non-dual teachers of all (Part 3).

## The world as play: Tantra and Kashmir Shaivism

Tantra is the most misunderstood word in this book. In the West it has come to mean sex. In India it names a large family of traditions, Hindu, Buddhist and Jain, that share a striking idea: the body, the senses and the world are not obstacles to liberation but means to it. Energy (*shakti*), ritual, mantra, the guru's transmission, and sometimes deliberately transgressive practices are all tools.

The philosophical peak of Tantra is **Kashmir Shaivism**, above all the work of Abhinavagupta around the year 1000. Like Advaita it is non-dual, but with a crucial difference. For Shankara the world is an appearance to see through. For Abhinavagupta the world is the real self-expression of consciousness, Shiva's play. You do not escape the world; you recognize it as yourself.

Tantra's goddess-centered form, **Shakta** worship, was especially strong in Bengal. When Ramakrishna was initiated into tantric practice by a wandering woman teacher, it was this world he entered (Part 2). Part 6 is built almost entirely on these roots.

## Love as the path: bhakti

From around the sixth century, a revolution began in the Tamil south. Poet-saints, the Shaiva **Nayanars** and the Vaishnava **Alvars**, sang passionate songs to Shiva and Vishnu in the local language rather than Sanskrit. Many were from lower castes; some were women. Their message was that love of God is open to everyone, and that it outranks learning, ritual and birth.

This devotional movement, **bhakti**, spread north over the following centuries. In Bengal in the early 1500s, **Chaitanya** made ecstatic love of Krishna, sung and danced in public, the center of a new tradition, Gaudiya Vaishnavism. Four centuries later, one of its monks would take it to New York and found ISKCON (Part 7).

Bhakti is easy to underrate in a book about "philosophers." Don't. Ramana wept over the lives of the Nayanars before he ever heard the word Advaita. Many figures who are remembered as cool philosophers were, in their own lives, lovers of God.

## The formless God: the Sants and the Sikh Gurus

In north India from the fifteenth century, another strand of bhakti took a different turn. The **Sants**, poets like Kabir, a Muslim-born weaver in Varanasi, and Ravidas, a leatherworker, worshipped a God without form or name. They mocked idols, temples, mosques, caste and the authority of priests on both sides. What mattered was the inner name of God and the guru who could reveal it.

**Guru Nanak** (1469–1539) grew up in this world and founded the Sikh tradition, carried forward by nine more Gurus until 1708, when the line of human Gurus was closed and authority passed to the scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib.

The Sant idea of the "inner sound" or "Word" (*shabd*) as the path home flows directly into the Radha Soami movement and its many branches (Part 9). And Kabir turns up in an unexpected place: B.R. Ambedkar counted him among his three gurus (Part 11).

## The Sufis

Islam reached India early, and its mystical dimension, **Sufism**, put down deep roots from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The **Chishti** order, centered on the shrine of Moinuddin Chishti in Ajmer and later of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, emphasized love, music and service. The **Naqshbandi** order favored silent remembrance of God and the transmission of spiritual states from master to disciple, heart to heart.

Sufi shrines became places where Hindus and Muslims prayed side by side, and the boundary between a Sufi *pir* and a Hindu saint could be hard to locate. That borderland is Part 8, home to Shirdi Sai Baba and Meher Baba. And one of the more surprising edges on the tree runs from a Naqshbandi master into a Hindu meditation movement, Sahaj Marg (Part 9).

## The renouncers: Jains and Buddhists

Two traditions older than Vedanta itself rejected the authority of the Vedas altogether. Both arose in the fifth century BCE, around Mahavira and the Buddha.

The **Jain** path is one of rigorous ethics, above all *ahimsa*, non-harm, carried further than in any other tradition, and of the soul's slow purification from the karma that clings to it. Jainism survived in India unbroken, especially in Gujarat and Rajasthan. Its influence on modern India runs straight through a Jain mystic named Shrimad Rajchandra to the young Mohandas Gandhi (Part 10).

**Buddhism** largely vanished from India after about the thirteenth century, surviving in Sri Lanka, Tibet and Southeast Asia. Its return to the land of its birth in the twentieth century, as a revival, as a meditation movement and as Ambedkar's mass conversion of 1956, is the story of Part 11.

## The guru and the lineage

One institution runs through all of these: the **guru**. In most Indian traditions, real knowledge is not taken from books but received from a teacher who has it, in person. The teacher was taught by a teacher, who was taught by a teacher, back to a founder, a sage or a god. That chain is a **parampara**, and the tradition it carries is a **sampradaya**. Entry is by **initiation** (*diksha*).

This is why a lineage tree is the right shape for this book, and also why it has to be drawn carefully. Lineage is authority. Claiming a famous predecessor, or a visionary meeting with an immortal master, is a way of claiming legitimacy, and the tree has to show the difference between an initiation that is documented and one that is told only by devotees. Some of the most original figures in this book, Ramana and Krishnamurti among them, had no lineage at all, and that absence is itself a statement.

## What changed around 1830

So why start the book in the 1830s, rather than with Shankara or Kabir?

Because something new happened. British rule brought English education, the printing press, railways, and Christian missionaries who described Hinduism as superstition and idolatry. European scholars, starting with William Jones's Asiatic Society in Calcutta in 1784, translated the Upanishads and Sanskrit classics and sometimes admired them extravagantly. Educated Indians, especially in Bengal, now read their own scriptures through European eyes, and argued with Europeans about them in English.

Out of that pressure came a new kind of figure: the reformer who reread the old texts by the light of reason and defended them to the modern world. Then came the teachers who exported Indian spirituality to the West, and then the West came looking for itself, from the Theosophists to the Beatles at Rishikesh in 1968. The traditions above did not disappear. They were sorted, translated, simplified, defended, mixed and sold. Every chapter in this book is one person's answer to that moment.

The first answer was an argument about reason. It begins with a Bengali scholar who learned Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, Greek and English, read the scriptures of all of them, and concluded that the Upanishads taught one formless God and that idol worship was a corruption. His name was Rammohan Roy, and he opens Part 1.
