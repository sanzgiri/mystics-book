Every person in this book started with something they were given. Ramana read the lives of the Tamil saints years before he heard of Shankara. Vivekananda grew up in a Calcutta reform movement that spent its energy arguing with Christian missionaries. Krishnamurti was raised by Theosophists, whose ideas came partly from India and partly from Victorian spiritualism. To judge what each of them added, it helps to have a rough map of what was already there.

This prologue is that map. It simplifies a great deal, since scholars spend whole careers on any one of these traditions. The aim is to give you enough to recognize each root when it turns up in a chapter. Each one appears as a root on the lineage tree, and the same names come back again and again.

## One reality: the Upanishads

For this book, the story starts with the Upanishads, philosophical dialogues composed roughly between 800 and 200 BCE as the closing part of the Vedas. They read as conversations: teachers and students, kings and sages, a father and his son, asking what is ultimately real.

Two ideas from those conversations run through almost every chapter that follows. The first is **Brahman**, a single reality underlying the variety of the world. The second is **Atman**, the true self, deeper than the body, the senses and the restless mind. The boldest claim in the Upanishads is that Atman and Brahman are the same. What you most deeply are and what the universe most deeply is turn out to be one thing.

The Upanishads present this as something a person can discover for themselves. They are full of methods for doing so: questioning, meditation, and the patient setting aside of everything you can observe about yourself. That emphasis on direct experience is the thread the nineteenth-century reformers would pick up and pull hard.

## Three readings of the Upanishads: Vedanta

Vedanta, "the end of the Vedas," is the name for the schools that tried to make the Upanishads into a consistent system. Three of them dominate.

**Advaita**, meaning "non-dual," is linked above all with Shankara, usually dated to the eighth century. It takes the identity of Atman and Brahman literally. Only the one reality truly exists, and the world of separate things is an appearance, like a rope mistaken for a snake in dim light. Liberation, for Shankara, means seeing through that mistake. He also founded monasteries, called *mathas*, whose heads still carry the title Shankaracharya. One of them has a chapter in Part 2.

**Vishishtadvaita**, "qualified non-dualism," is Ramanuja's answer, from the eleventh and twelfth centuries. He agreed that there is one reality, and said that it is a personal God, with souls and the world forming God's body. In his view you belong to God and remain yourself, and devotion is the whole of the path.

**Dvaita**, "dualism," was taught by Madhva in the thirteenth century. God, souls and matter are eternally distinct, and liberation means endless closeness to God.

Most of the modern figures in this book lean toward Advaita, for a clear reason. Its claim that every path leads to the same reality suited a modern argument that all religions are one. The other two schools come back strongly in Part 7, where love of a personal God is the whole story.

## The body as a path: yoga and the Nath yogis

Today "yoga" usually means postures in a studio. Its older meaning is discipline, and above all the practice of stilling the mind. The classic text is Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras*, compiled in the early centuries CE. It describes an eight-limbed path of ethics, posture, breath, withdrawal of the senses, concentration and absorption, with the goal of separating pure consciousness from the restless activity of nature.

A second, more physical tradition grew up in the medieval period among the **Nath** yogis, who trace themselves to the semi-legendary Gorakhnath. Hatha yoga, with its postures and breathing methods and its map of energy channels and a dormant power at the base of the spine, comes largely from the Naths. Its best-known manual, the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, dates from the fifteenth century.

The Naths matter to this book in two quite different ways. Their techniques were reworked in the twentieth century into modern postural yoga, the subject of Part 5. And one Nath line, the Navnath Sampradaya, produced Nisargadatta Maharaj, one of the most radical non-dual teachers in Part 3.

## The world as divine play: Tantra and Kashmir Shaivism

In the West, Tantra has come to mean sacred sex. In India the word covers a large family of Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions that share one striking idea: the body, the senses and the world can be used as means of liberation. Their tools include energy (*shakti*), ritual, mantra, the guru's transmission, and sometimes practices that deliberately break social rules.

The philosophical high point of Tantra is **Kashmir Shaivism**, above all the work of Abhinavagupta around the year 1000. Like Advaita, it is non-dual. Where Shankara treats the world as an appearance to see through, Abhinavagupta treats it as the real self-expression of consciousness, the play of Shiva. Liberation, for him, means recognizing the world as your own Self.

Tantra's goddess-centered form, **Shakta** worship, was especially strong in Bengal. When a wandering woman teacher initiated Ramakrishna into tantric practice in the 1860s, this is the world he entered. Part 6 rests almost entirely on these roots.

## Love as the path: bhakti

From around the sixth century, a revolution began in the Tamil south. Poet-saints, the **Nayanars** devoted to Shiva and the **Alvars** devoted to Vishnu, sang passionate songs to God in Tamil instead of Sanskrit. Many came from lower castes, and some were women. Their message was that love of God is open to everyone and matters more than learning, ritual or birth.

This movement, called **bhakti**, spread north over the following centuries. In Bengal in the early 1500s, **Chaitanya** made ecstatic love of Krishna, sung and danced in the streets, the center of a new tradition, Gaudiya Vaishnavism. Four hundred years later one of its monks took it to New York and founded ISKCON, the Hare Krishna movement, in Part 7.

It is easy to underrate bhakti in a book about philosophers. Ramana wept over the lives of the Nayanars long before he heard the word Advaita, and many figures now remembered as cool thinkers were passionate lovers of God in their own lives.

## The formless God: the Sants and the Sikh Gurus

In north India from the fifteenth century, another strand of bhakti took a different direction. The **Sants**, poets such as Kabir, a weaver from a Muslim family in Varanasi, and Ravidas, a leatherworker, worshipped a God without form or name. They mocked idols, temples and mosques, the caste system, and priests of every kind. What mattered to them was the inner name of God and a guru who could reveal it.

**Guru Nanak** (1469–1539) grew up in this world and founded the Sikh tradition. Nine more Gurus followed until 1708, when the line of human Gurus closed and authority passed to the scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib.

The Sant teaching of an inner sound or Word (*shabd*) as the way home flows directly into the Radha Soami movement and its many branches in Part 9. Kabir also turns up somewhere you might not expect: B.R. Ambedkar counted him as one of his three gurus (Part 11).

## The Sufis

Islam arrived in India early, and its mystical side, **Sufism**, put down deep roots from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The **Chishti** order, centered on the shrine of Moinuddin Chishti in Ajmer and later of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, emphasized love, music and service. The **Naqshbandi** order favored silent remembrance of God and the passing of spiritual states from master to disciple, heart to heart.

Sufi shrines became places where Hindus and Muslims prayed side by side, and the line between a Sufi saint and a Hindu one could be hard to draw. That borderland is Part 8, home to Shirdi Sai Baba and Meher Baba. One of the more surprising lines on the tree runs from a Naqshbandi master into a Hindu meditation movement, Sahaj Marg, in Part 9.

## The renouncers: Jains and Buddhists

Two traditions as old as the Upanishads rejected the authority of the Vedas altogether. Both took shape in the fifth century BCE, around Mahavira and the Buddha.

The **Jain** path is one of strict ethics, above all *ahimsa*, non-harm, carried further than in any other tradition, and of the soul's slow purification from the karma that clings to it. Jainism has survived in India without a break, especially in Gujarat and Rajasthan. Its influence on modern India runs straight through a Jain mystic named Shrimad Rajchandra to the young Mohandas Gandhi (Part 10).

**Buddhism** had largely disappeared from India by about the thirteenth century, surviving in Sri Lanka, Tibet and Southeast Asia. Its return to the land where it began, as a revival, as a meditation movement and through Ambedkar's mass conversion in 1956, is the story of Part 11.

## The guru and the lineage

One institution runs through all of these traditions: the **guru**. In most of them, real knowledge comes from a teacher who has it, in person, more than from books. That teacher learned from a teacher, who learned from another, back to a founder, a sage or a god. The chain is called a **parampara**, and the tradition it carries is a **sampradaya**. You enter it through **initiation**, or *diksha*.

That is why a tree is the right shape for this book, and why the tree has to be drawn with care. A lineage confers authority. Claiming a famous predecessor, or a visionary meeting with an immortal master, is a way of claiming legitimacy, so the tree marks the difference between an initiation that is documented and one known only from devotees' accounts. Some of the most original people in this book, Ramana and Krishnamurti among them, stood outside any lineage, and that fact says something about them too.

## Why the story starts around 1830

Shankara and Kabir are part of the background. The book itself begins in the 1830s, because that is when something new started.

British rule brought English education, the printing press, railways, and Christian missionaries who described Hinduism as superstition and idolatry. European scholars, starting with William Jones and the Asiatic Society he founded in Calcutta in 1784, translated the Upanishads and other Sanskrit classics, and some of them admired what they found extravagantly. Educated Indians, especially in Bengal, began reading their own scriptures through European eyes and arguing about them with Europeans, in English.

That pressure produced a new kind of figure: the reformer who reread the old texts in the light of reason and defended them to the modern world. Next came teachers who carried Indian spirituality to the West, and then Westerners who came to India looking for it, from the Theosophists in the 1870s to the Beatles at Rishikesh in 1968. The older traditions survived all of this. They were sorted, translated, simplified, defended, blended and sold, and each chapter in this book follows one person's response to that moment.

The first response was an argument about reason, made by a Bengali scholar who learned Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, Greek and English so that he could read the scriptures of several religions in their own languages. He concluded that the Upanishads taught one formless God and that image worship was a later corruption. His name was Rammohan Roy, and Part 1 begins with him.
