## What the book is

The Banyan is a free web book about Indian mystics and philosophers from the 1830s to the present. Each person gets a chapter or a shorter profile that follows the same plan: their life, what they taught, how to practise it, and where they sit in a lineage of teachers and students. The book is divided into thirteen parts, and each part asks one question, such as whether the Self can be known without method or whether the guru is the obstacle.

Readers can go from start to finish, or begin with the [reading paths](/start/) and follow one interest across the parts. The [Prologue](/prologue/) describes the older traditions that every modern teacher drew on.

## Inherited and new

Every chapter and profile has a section called "Inherited and new". It asks a single question about the figure: what did this person receive from earlier teachers and traditions, and what did they add? The section ends in a short verdict. The verdict judges the originality of the teaching, and it is the only place where the book gives its own judgement. It leaves character and conduct aside.

## Lineage edges and evidence grades

Each line on the lineage tree, called an edge, joins two people or a person and a tradition, and carries two labels.

The first label says what kind of connection it is: initiation, living with or studying under a teacher, influence, family, institutional succession, a connection someone claimed, or a reaction against a teacher.

The second label grades the evidence:

- **Documented**: recorded at the time, or by several independent witnesses.
- **Testimony**: reported by one of the people involved, or by later accounts close to the events.
- **Hagiographic**: told in lives of the teacher written by followers, often with miracles or meetings that cannot be checked.
- **Devotional**: held as a matter of faith, such as guidance from a deity, a vision, an immortal master or a past life.

A connection is marked **verified** once it has been checked against sources while the chapter it belongs to was written. Unverified lines appear faded on the tree. A hagiographic or devotional grade describes the kind of evidence and makes no claim about whether the event happened. The book reports such accounts as accounts, with phrases like "devotees say" or "by his own account".

## How sources were chosen and checked

The chapters were drafted by AI writing tools under the author's direction, working to a written brief, and the fact-checking described below also used AI. Each page shows its status, and pages marked draft have not yet had a full review by the author.

Writers were asked to prefer primary texts, an organisation's own records for its own history, scholarly books and articles, and reputable newspapers. Quotations had to be short and taken verbatim from a source the writer had seen; anything else became paraphrase. Where sources disagree on a date or a detail, the text gives the range.

Each entry was then checked by a separate fact-checking pass that had not seen the writer's research. The checker listed the checkable claims (dates, places, names, quotations, numbers, and who met or initiated whom), searched for sources independently and corrected errors in place. Claims that could not be confirmed were either cut or left in the text with the mark "(unverified)", so readers can see which statements rest on weaker ground. Every chapter ends with its own references and a single recommended book, and every profile ends with further reading.

This process reduces errors and cannot remove them. Readers who find a mistake can <CorrectionLink>report a correction</CorrectionLink> on the book's public issue tracker, and the [changelog](/changelog/) records what has changed.

## What is left out

Alongside teaching and practice, the book tells the parts of each life that shaped the teaching, from family and early years to teachers, turning points, daily routines, and illness and death where they bear on the teaching or the succession.

It leaves out legal proceedings of every kind and allegations of misconduct. It also leaves out political or social controversy that is unnecessary for understanding the teaching. Both rules apply to living and historical figures alike. Where a political or social position is itself part of the teaching, it stays, stated as a position. Ambedkar's rejection of caste and Gandhi's nonviolence are two examples. Intellectual criticism of the ideas also stays, including objections from rival schools and scholarly views on how original a teaching was.

The author made this choice to keep the book on its subject. Leaving a matter out says nothing about it either way. Readers who want that history will find it in news reporting and full-length biographies.

## How to read the tree

On the [tree](/tree/), lines run from a teacher or influence down to the person they shaped. Orange lines mark personal connections such as initiation or living with a teacher; blue lines mark influence, family and lineage; grey lines mark claimed connections. A solid line is documented, a dashed line is testimony, and a dotted line is hagiographic or devotional.

Tapping or clicking a name lights up its ancestors and descendants and lists them in a side panel; a second tap opens the chapter. The menus above the tree filter by part and by evidence grade, and the Find box locates a name. Keyboard users can type a name into Find and then move to the link in the side panel.

## Glossary and map

The [glossary](/glossary/) explains each recurring term in plain English and gives the original script where there is one.

The [map](/map/) shows birthplaces, ashrams, caves and pilgrimage towns. Selecting a marker gives a short note and a link to the chapter. Positions are approximate, and the full list of places appears in text beneath the map. The [timeline](/timeline/) sets every dated life against a few events of the period.
