Each chapter in this book ends with a verdict on what one person inherited and what they added. This epilogue reads those verdicts side by side, together with the lineage tree drawn from the chapters. The numbers below come from the tree as it stood when the last chapter was written, and they will shift a little as edges are checked and added.

## 1. The shape of the tree

The tree has 322 names on it. Twenty-four are roots, the older traditions described in the Prologue. Eighty-six are people with entries in this book: 31 with full chapters, 53 with shorter profiles and 2 with sidebars. The other 212 are teachers, disciples, successors and institutions that appear only on the tree.

Four hundred and ten lines join them. The most common kind is influence (164 edges), followed by initiation (79), lineage (51), living with a teacher (36) and institutional ties (35). Twenty edges mark a claimed relationship, fifteen mark family and eight a deliberate reaction against a teacher or tradition. By evidence, 308 edges rest on documents, 85 on someone's own testimony, 11 on hagiography and 6 on devotional belief, and 396 have been checked against sources.

The most connected name is a root. Advaita Vedanta, the non-dual school linked to Shankara, sends 22 edges into the modern period, more than twice as many as the next tradition, Shakta Tantra, with 10. Following its lines downward, 35 of the 86 people with entries descend from Advaita by at least one path.

Among people, [Ramana Maharshi](/figures/ramana-maharshi/) has the most edges, fourteen, and ten of them run outward to disciples, visitors and writers. [Ramakrishna](/figures/ramakrishna/) and [Osho](/figures/osho/) have thirteen each, Ramakrishna's split almost evenly between lines coming in and going out. Osho receives eleven, from Jain, Buddhist, Sufi, Tantric, Christian and Western sources among others, and sends two, both to his own organisation, which is his verdict's remark that he borrowed almost every ingredient drawn as a diagram. [Swami Sivananda](/figures/sivananda/) has eight outgoing lines, all to monks he initiated.

One connected body holds 291 of the 322 names. Six small islands sit apart from it, among them the BAPS succession of [Shastriji Maharaj](/figures/shastriji-pramukh/), the vipassana line of [Dipa Ma](/figures/dipa-ma/) and [Neem Karoli Baba](/figures/neem-karoli-baba/), whose only edge runs to Ram Dass and who has no recorded teacher. [Ayya Vaikundar](/figures/ayya-vaikundar/) has no edges, because the links popular accounts draw between him and other southern reformers could not be verified.

The roots do most of the holding together. With them set aside, the people fall into 36 separate families. The largest has 132 names and contains 42 of the 86 entries, including the Bengal reformers, the Ramakrishna circle, the Theosophists and the direct-path teachers. The next largest are the Radhasoami and Namdhari lines (20 names), Sivananda's monastic family (18) and the cluster around [Shirdi Sai Baba](/figures/shirdi-sai-baba/), [Meher Baba](/figures/meher-baba/) and [Sathya Sai Baba](/figures/sathya-sai-baba/) (17).

Thirty-three edges cross from one part of the book to another. [Keshab Chandra Sen](/figures/keshab-chandra-sen/) influenced [Vivekananda](/figures/vivekananda/), [Dayananda Saraswati](/figures/dayananda-saraswati/) influenced [Aurobindo](/figures/aurobindo/), and [Phule](/figures/jyotirao-phule/) shaped [Ambedkar](/figures/ambedkar/). [Rama Tirtha](/figures/rama-tirtha/) was the maternal uncle of [Papaji](/figures/papaji/), by Papaji's account. [Papa Ramdas](/figures/papa-ramdas/) has four such lines, from Ramana, Ramakrishna, Vivekananda and Rama Tirtha, all resting on his own testimony.

## 2. Five real innovations

The book judges originality of teaching, and read together the verdicts place most of what was new somewhere other than doctrine. By my rough count, about two in three of the 84 verdicts locate the new thing in delivery, form, practice, institution or the shape of a life. My early candidates included Ramana's inquiry and [Krishnamacharya](/figures/krishnamacharya/)'s postural yoga, and their own verdicts place them among the innovators of practice, discussed below. The five here are the cases where a verdict says a teaching itself had no clear precedent, ranked by how strongly the chapter makes the case.

**[Aurobindo](/figures/aurobindo/)'s evolutionary Vedanta** is the strongest case. His verdict calls it among the most original systems in the book. In it a Vedantic Absolute descends into matter and climbs back through life and mind toward a Supermind, and a yoga exists to help the climb. The chapter also gives the counter-case. Every part of the system has an Indian ancestor and some have European relatives, Hegel and Bergson among them; defenders of Shankara say his attack on illusionism misreads its target; and a claim about the future of evolution may be untestable. The verdict survives these objections because it concerns the shape of the thought and leaves its truth aside.

**[J. Krishnamurti](/figures/j-krishnamurti/)'s refusal of every teacher, himself included** comes second. His verdict says it has no real precedent in the Indian traditions he grew up beside. Raised to be a World Teacher, he dissolved the order built for him at Ommen on 3 August 1929. The counter-case comes from inside the chapter. The idea that the self is constructed is very old and he never named a source for it, and critics say he became a guru of a new kind, speaking from a platform to thousands a year for half a century.

**[Ambedkar](/figures/ambedkar/)'s Buddhism** is third. His verdict calls it a deliberate rewriting of an old religion for a new purpose, more original in its reading of suffering and karma than anything else in the book done with a scripture its author accepted. He read suffering chiefly as social suffering, limited karma to this life and wrote 22 vows for converts. Traditional Buddhists answer that the Four Noble Truths and rebirth are central to the Buddha's teaching, and Richard Gombrich has argued that the Buddha did not set out to reform society.

**[Dayananda Saraswati](/figures/dayananda-saraswati/)'s Vedic canon** is fourth. He limited scripture to the four hymn collections, read them as strict monotheism and taught a liberation that ends after a cosmic period. The chapter calls the result one of the most original pieces of religious thinking of the century. The counter-case is that most specialists outside the Arya Samaj think his readings of the hymns are wrong, a judgement the chapter keeps separate from the verdict on originality.

**[Iqbal](/figures/iqbal/)'s doctrine of the self** is fifth. *Khudi*, a self to be strengthened and never dissolved, reversed the Sufi goal of passing away into God, and he argued for it from inside the Sufi poetic tradition. Khwaja Hasan Nizami and other Sufis protested in print, and Iqbal built with Bergson and Nietzsche as well as with Rumi and the Quran.

Three other verdicts make claims nearly as strong: [K. C. Bhattacharyya](/figures/kc-bhattacharya/)'s account of the self as freedom won in stages, [Dada Lekhraj](/figures/dada-lekhraj/)'s new revelation inside Hindu names, and [Gopi Krishna](/figures/gopi-krishna/)'s biological theory of kundalini. I ranked them lower because they reached smaller circles, and a reader could fairly rank them higher.

The innovations of practice form a second list. [Ramana](/figures/ramana-maharshi/)'s verdict calls his view of reality two thousand years old and the simple method of inquiry his own. The question "Who am I?" was already in the *Vivekachudamani*, which he translated, so his case rests on turning a question into a practice open to anyone, with initiation, Sanskrit and renunciation left optional. Krishnamacharya set postures to the breath, and his verdict says the result is the form of yoga most people in the world now practise. [Ramakrishna](/figures/ramakrishna/)'s practice of walking several religions' paths and reporting back belongs here, and so do [Gandhi](/figures/gandhi/)'s monastic vows carried into public life. Measured by how many people it reached, this second list outweighs the first.

## 3. The great restatements

Most of the people in this book said they were passing on what they had received, and their verdicts mostly agree.

[Bhaktivinoda](/figures/bhaktivinoda/) put sixteenth-century Gaudiya doctrine into print and into English. [Prabhupada](/figures/prabhupada/) carried a whole devotional culture, diet, temple schedule and Sanskrit names included, into America. [Chinmayananda](/figures/chinmayananda/) turned monastic Vedanta into a course of study for the English-educated middle class, and [Sivananda](/figures/sivananda/) turned the classical paths into a daily routine. [Maharishi Mahesh Yogi](/figures/maharishi/) taught one technique in the same words everywhere, and his verdict says every later global meditation brand copied the format. [Yogananda](/figures/yogananda/) made initiation available by post. [B. K. S. Iyengar](/figures/iyengar/)'s codified method, by his verdict, changed how the world practises yoga more than any idea did. [Papaji](/figures/papaji/)'s open satsang shaped how most Westerners now meet Advaita.

Some restatements kept a tradition alive or restored it. [Swami Lakshmanjoo](/figures/lakshmanjoo/)'s verdict says that without him Kashmir Shaivism would very likely have survived only in libraries. [Swami Dayananda of Arsha Vidya](/figures/dayananda-arsha/) recovered Shankara's view that the Upanishads are themselves a means of knowledge. The [Kanchi Paramacharya](/figures/chandrasekharendra-saraswati/) gave an old position a lucid modern voice and, his chapter says, would have taken a low score for originality as praise.

The tree suggests why this work mattered. The new systems have few lines running out of them: Aurobindo has three, Bhattacharyya one and Iqbal none. Sivananda has eight and Krishnamacharya six, and Sivananda's monks, the Mysore yoga line and the Gaudiya mission form some of the largest families once the roots are set aside. The teachings that reached the most people travelled in forms the restaters built.

## 4. The Vivekananda bottleneck

A good deal of what the world now calls Indian spirituality passed through one reframing in the 1890s. [Vivekananda](/figures/vivekananda/)'s verdict lists what was new in it: the four yogas as one teachable system, religion presented as experience open to verification in the language of science, non-dualism as the ground of social service, and Hinduism presented abroad as a single universal religion with Advaita at its centre. It became, in the verdict's words, the default modern form of Hinduism, in India and abroad.

The tree records only part of this. Vivekananda has six direct lines, to Aurobindo, [Radhakrishnan](/figures/radhakrishnan/), Rama Tirtha, Papa Ramdas, Sister Nivedita and [Swami Sarvapriyananda](/figures/sarvapriyananda/), and 17 names descend from him. More of his reach shows in the inherited lists of later chapters. Radhakrishnan's verdict says his central ideas were Vivekananda's. Yogananda inherited the precedent of an Indian monk teaching yoga to Americans as universal religion, and the anthropologist Sarah Strauss connects Sivananda's four main verbs to Vivekananda's four yogas. The reframing produced a monastic order built around both meditation and relief work, and through Sivananda's routine it reached much of the yoga taught today.

The chapters also record what it left out, and a dispute over how much. Walter Neevel and Narasingha Sil argue that Ramakrishna's disciples, above all Vivekananda, presented him after his death as a calm Advaitin and played down the Kali worship that dominated his life. Anantanand Rambachan argues that Vivekananda reversed Shankara by making experience the final authority and scripture its secondary record. Paul Hacker argued that the link between non-duality and compassion came from Schopenhauer, and Dermot Killingley answered that Vivekananda held it before he met Deussen. Jyotirmaya Sharma argues that the single Hinduism he described helped prepare the ground for Hindutva, while Swami Medhananda and others point to his attacks on hereditary caste and his acceptance of every religion as a path to salvation. The chapter concludes that his writings hold enough of both emphases for each side to quote him.

The tree adds one more measure. Fifty-one of the 86 entries have no path from the Advaita root at all. The Radhasoami teachers, the Jain reformers of Part 10, the saints of the Hindu and Muslim borderland, the Gaudiya missionaries and the Buddhist revival grew from other roots. Prabhupada's chapter draws the contrast directly. Vivekananda translated Vedanta into the vocabulary of universal religion, and Prabhupada refused that translation. The Paramacharya accepted Vivekananda's premise that all paths lead to one goal and concluded that each person should keep the path of their birth.

## 5. Lineage and legitimacy

Twenty edges on the tree are typed as claims. Only one rests on a document, and what it documents is the claim itself, in C. W. Leadbeater's letters of 1909 about Krishnamurti's past lives. The others rest on testimony (8), hagiography (7) or devotional belief (4). Across all 410 edges, 17 rest on hagiography or devotion, and most fall into three groups.

The first is immortal and Himalayan masters. Mahavatar Babaji has five edges, to [Lahiri Mahasaya](/figures/lahiri-mahasaya/), [Sri Yukteswar](/figures/yukteswar/), Yogananda, [Sri M](/figures/sri-m/) and Sri M's teacher Maheshwarnath, and every one is hagiographic; for Lahiri's initiation in 1861 there is no evidence outside the lineage. Krishnamacharya's seven and a half years with a Himalayan teacher and Swami Rama's upbringing by Bengali Baba rest on each man's own account, and the Krishnamacharya chapter says historians treat his story as one that gave a new practice an old pedigree.

The second is reincarnation and past lives. Sathya Sai Baba declared in 1940, at thirteen, that he was Sai Baba of Shirdi. [Sadhguru](/figures/sadhguru/) grounds part of his authority in a past-life lineage that he alone reports. The Namdhari belief that Guru Gobind Singh lived on until 1812 and passed the guruship to Balak Singh is rejected by mainstream Sikh bodies.

The third is visionary or divine initiation. The Brahma Kumaris hold that the Supreme Soul spoke through Dada Lekhraj. Ramana named the hill of Arunachala as his guru. Shirdi Sai Baba's guru Venkusa appears only in accounts written after his death.

The pattern is fairly consistent. Claimed edges gather where a new practice needed an old source, as with Kriya Yoga and Krishnamacharya's sequences, and where authority rested on the person, as with Sathya Sai Baba and Dada Lekhraj. They are rare among the Bengal reformers, the philosophers and the Jain teachers, whose authority rested on texts and argument.

Some figures refused a lineage. Krishnamurti dissolved the organisation built around him, [Anandamayi Ma](/figures/anandamayi-ma/) by her own account initiated herself in 1922, and Radhakrishnan had no guru. On the tree, 41 of the 86 entries have no edge of initiation, lineage or living-with from a human teacher, though some of that reflects how edges are typed; Sarada Devi's tie to Ramakrishna is marked as marriage. Others needed a line of succession and fought over it, and the Radhasoami, Namdhari, Sahaj Marg and Dada Bhagwan successions were all contested.

Ordinary initiations are mostly well attested. Of the 39 initiation edges into people with entries, 25 are documented, 11 rest on testimony and 3 on hagiography. The doubtful edges are few, and most sit at the top of a lineage, where the founding claim is made.

## 6. The question that remains

The book asks thirteen questions, one for each part. I expected the tree to answer several of them, and it answers fewer than I hoped.

It answers the historical questions best. Part 1 shows that the Vedas could be reread by reason in a plain sense, since Rammohan printed the Upanishads, argued from them and gave every later reformer a method to argue with. Whether the Buddha could come home has a historical answer in Ambedkar's ceremony at Nagpur on 14 October 1956, though his chapter records how far his Buddhism stands from the one Dharmapala wanted India to honour. Whether one master can be Hindu and Muslim at once has a partial answer in Shirdi, where a fakir lived in a mosque he named after Krishna's city, though most of the evidence comes from Hindu sources.

The tree also answers part of the question of Part 13, who gets to speak for the sacred. The edge data show that direct mandates, from an immortal master, a past life or God speaking through a person, were claimed most often around a new practice or a new kind of personal authority. That is a finding about how authority is claimed, and it says nothing about whether any claim was true.

Six questions it cannot answer: whether all religion is one, whether the Self can be known without method, whether spirit is evolving, whether surrender is the path, whether the inner Word leads home, and whether awakening is a transmitted force. These are questions about reality, and a lineage tree holds evidence about people. The chapters report what each teacher said and how they lived, and none of them can test the Supermind or the sound current.

The remaining three are partly empirical. Kuvalayananda measured what instruments could measure in yoga, which was mostly the body. Gandhi tried the monk's vows on a nation, and that can be studied as history. Krishnamurti's refusal of authority can be judged by what happened to his listeners, and his own last recorded statement was that nobody had lived the teaching. In each case the tree shows what people did with the question and leaves the question open.

What I take from the whole is narrower than a verdict on Indian spirituality. Across 190 years the doctrines changed slowly and the means of passing them on changed fast. Print, English, the lecture tour, the postal course, the weekend intensive and the recorded answer did more to change what ordinary people received than any new metaphysics did. I came to this book looking for the innovators, and I finish it with more respect than I expected for the people who carried old teachings carefully.
