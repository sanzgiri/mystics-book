Each chapter in this book ends with a verdict on what one person inherited and what they added. This epilogue reads those verdicts side by side, together with the lineage tree drawn from the chapters. The figures below come from the tree as it stood when the last chapter was written. They will move a little as edges are checked and added, and the shape they describe will move less.

## 1. The shape of the tree

The tree has 322 names on it. Twenty-four are roots, the older traditions described in the Prologue, from the Upanishads and the Vedic hymns to the Sufi orders, the Sikh Gurus and the Jain and Buddhist traditions. Eighty-six are people with entries in this book: 31 with full chapters, 53 with shorter profiles and 2 with sidebars. The other 212 are teachers, disciples, successors and institutions that appear only on the tree.

Four hundred and ten lines join them. The most common kind is influence, with 164 edges, followed by initiation (79), lineage (51), living with a teacher (36) and institutional ties (35). Twenty edges mark a claimed relationship, fifteen mark family, eight mark a deliberate reaction against a teacher or tradition, and two mark protection. By evidence, 308 edges rest on documents, 85 on someone's own testimony, 11 on hagiography and 6 on devotional belief. Almost all of them, 396, have been checked against sources during the writing.

The most connected name is a root. Advaita Vedanta, the non-dual school linked to Shankara, sends 22 edges into the modern period, more than twice as many as any other tradition. Shakta Tantra comes next with 10, then the Sufi orders with 9. Following those lines downward, 35 of the 86 people with entries descend from Advaita by at least one path.

Among people, [Ramana Maharshi](/figures/ramana-maharshi/) has the most edges, fourteen, and ten of them run outward, to disciples, visitors and writers. [Ramakrishna](/figures/ramakrishna/) and [Osho](/figures/osho/) have thirteen each, with opposite shapes. Ramakrishna receives seven lines, from his tantric teacher, the monk who gave him sannyasa and the traditions he practised, and sends six. Osho receives eleven, from Jain, Buddhist, Sufi, Tantric, Christian and Western sources among others, and sends two, both to his own organisation. His verdict says he borrowed almost every ingredient and said so, and the tree draws that sentence. [Swami Sivananda](/figures/sivananda/) has eight outgoing lines, nearly all to monks he initiated who went on to found their own missions.

The tree is less of a single tree than it looks. One connected body holds 291 of the 322 names. Six small islands sit apart from it: the BAPS succession of [Shastriji Maharaj](/figures/shastriji-pramukh/), the vipassana line of [Dipa Ma](/figures/dipa-ma/), the Radhavallabh line of [Premanand Maharaj](/figures/premanand-maharaj/), the Himalayan line of [Swami Rama](/figures/swami-rama/), the shrine family of [Dhirendra Shastri](/figures/dhirendra-shastri/), and [Neem Karoli Baba](/figures/neem-karoli-baba/), whose only edge runs to Ram Dass and who has no recorded teacher at all. [Ayya Vaikundar](/figures/ayya-vaikundar/) has no edges, because the links that popular accounts draw between him and other Kerala reformers could not be verified.

The roots do most of the holding together. If they are set aside, the people fall into 36 separate families. The largest has 132 names and contains 42 of the 86 entries, the Bengal reformers, the Ramakrishna circle, the Theosophists and the direct-path teachers among them. The next largest are the Radhasoami and Namdhari lines (20 names), Sivananda's monastic family (18), and the cluster around [Shirdi Sai Baba](/figures/shirdi-sai-baba/), [Meher Baba](/figures/meher-baba/) and [Sathya Sai Baba](/figures/sathya-sai-baba/) (17). The Gaudiya teachers and the Mysore yoga line each form their own. Most of these families share an ancestor only at the level of an old tradition.

Thirty-three edges cross from one part of the book to another, and they are where the branches meet. [Keshab Chandra Sen](/figures/keshab-chandra-sen/) influenced [Vivekananda](/figures/vivekananda/), and [Dayananda Saraswati](/figures/dayananda-saraswati/) influenced [Aurobindo](/figures/aurobindo/). [Phule](/figures/jyotirao-phule/) shaped [Ambedkar](/figures/ambedkar/), and [Gandhi](/figures/gandhi/) is the figure Ambedkar most argued against. [Rama Tirtha](/figures/rama-tirtha/) was the maternal uncle of [Papaji](/figures/papaji/), by Papaji's account. Sivananda gave sannyasa to [Chinmayananda](/figures/chinmayananda/). [Papa Ramdas](/figures/papa-ramdas/) alone has four lines from other parts, from Ramana, Ramakrishna, Vivekananda and Rama Tirtha, all resting on his own testimony.

## 2. Five real innovations

The book judges originality of teaching. Read together, the verdicts locate most of what was new somewhere other than doctrine. By my own rough count, about two in three of the 84 verdicts place the new thing in delivery, form, practice, institution or the shape of a life, and say the doctrine was largely inherited. That changes the list I expected to write. The early candidates for this section included Ramana's inquiry and [Krishnamacharya](/figures/krishnamacharya/)'s postural yoga. Their own verdicts place them with the innovators of practice, and I discuss them there. The five below are the cases where a verdict says a teaching itself had no clear precedent, ranked by how strongly the chapter makes the case.

**[Aurobindo](/figures/aurobindo/)'s evolutionary Vedanta** is the strongest case. His verdict calls it among the most original systems in the book: a Vedantic Absolute that descends into matter and climbs back through life and mind toward a Supermind, with a yoga that exists to help the climb, aimed at a changed life on earth. The chapter also gives the counter-case. Every part of the system has an Indian ancestor and some have European relatives, Hegel and Bergson among them. Defenders of Shankara argue that his attack on illusionism misreads the doctrine it rejects, and the claim about the future of evolution may not be testable. The originality survives these objections, because the verdict concerns the shape of the thought and leaves its truth aside.

**[J. Krishnamurti](/figures/j-krishnamurti/)'s refusal of every teacher, himself included** comes second. His verdict says it has no real precedent in the Indian traditions he grew up beside. He was raised to be a World Teacher and dissolved the order built for him at Ommen on 3 August 1929. The counter-case is strong and comes from inside the chapter. The idea that the self is constructed is very old, he never named a source for it, and critics say he became a guru of a new kind, speaking from a platform to thousands a year for half a century. [U. G. Krishnamurti](/figures/ug-krishnamurti/)'s claim that enlightenment does not exist is a further original position in the same line.

**[Ambedkar](/figures/ambedkar/)'s Buddhism** is the third. His verdict calls it a deliberate rewriting of an old religion for a new purpose, more original in its reading of suffering and karma than anyone else in the book attempted with a scripture they accepted. He read suffering chiefly as social suffering, limited karma to this life and set out 22 vows for converts. Traditional Buddhists answer that the Four Noble Truths and rebirth are central to what the Buddha taught, and Richard Gombrich has argued that the Buddha did not set out to reform society. Ambedkar's openness about his departures is part of what the chapter credits.

**[Dayananda Saraswati](/figures/dayananda-saraswati/)'s Vedic canon** is fourth. He limited scripture to the four hymn collections, read them as strict monotheism and taught a liberation that ends after a cosmic period. His verdict calls the result a radical reconstruction, and the chapter calls it one of the most original pieces of religious thinking of the century. The counter-case is that most specialists outside the Arya Samaj think his readings of the hymns are wrong. A reconstruction can be original and mistaken, and the chapter keeps the two judgements apart.

**[Iqbal](/figures/iqbal/)'s doctrine of the self** is fifth. *Khudi*, a self to be strengthened and never dissolved, reversed the Sufi goal of passing away into God from inside the Sufi poetic tradition. His verdict counts it among the most original positions in the book. Khwaja Hasan Nizami and other Sufis protested in print, and Iqbal built with Bergson and Nietzsche as well as with Rumi and the Quran.

Three other verdicts make claims almost as strong. [K. C. Bhattacharyya](/figures/kc-bhattacharya/) is called the most original technical philosopher of colonial India, for his account of the self as freedom won in stages. [Dada Lekhraj](/figures/dada-lekhraj/)'s movement is called one of the most original theologies in the book, a new revelation inside Hindu names. [Gopi Krishna](/figures/gopi-krishna/)'s biological theory of kundalini is called new. I ranked them lower because each reached a smaller circle of readers or rests on a single claimed experience, and a reader could reasonably rank them higher.

The innovations of practice form a second list. [Ramana](/figures/ramana-maharshi/)'s verdict says his view of reality is two thousand years old and that the simple method of inquiry is his own. The question "Who am I?" was already in the *Vivekachudamani*, which he translated, so the case for him rests on turning a question into a practice anyone could use without initiation, Sanskrit or renunciation. Krishnamacharya set postures to the breath, and his verdict says the result is the form of yoga most people in the world now practise. [Ramakrishna](/figures/ramakrishna/)'s practice of walking several religions' paths and reporting back, and [Gandhi](/figures/gandhi/)'s monastic vows carried into public life, belong here too. Measured by how many people their work reached, this second list outweighs the first.

## 3. The great restatements

Most of the people in this book said they were passing on what they had received, and their verdicts mostly agree with them. That is a description of their work, and the chapters treat it as one.

The restatements take several forms. [Bhaktivinoda](/figures/bhaktivinoda/) put the sixteenth-century Gaudiya doctrine into print and into English. [Prabhupada](/figures/prabhupada/) carried the whole devotional culture, diet and temple schedule and Sanskrit names, into America without translating it into universal religion. [Chinmayananda](/figures/chinmayananda/) turned monastic Vedanta into a course of study for the English-educated middle class. [Sivananda](/figures/sivananda/) reduced the classical paths to a daily routine anyone could follow. [Atmananda Krishna Menon](/figures/atmananda-krishna-menon/) made an old method of inquiry into a curriculum. [Maharishi Mahesh Yogi](/figures/maharishi/) taught one technique in the same words everywhere, and his verdict says every later global meditation brand copied the format. [Yogananda](/figures/yogananda/) sent initiation by post. [B. K. S. Iyengar](/figures/iyengar/) codified a way of teaching postures, and his verdict says it changed how the world practises yoga more than any idea did. [Papaji](/figures/papaji/)'s open satsang shaped how most Westerners now meet Advaita.

Some restatements kept a tradition alive. [Swami Lakshmanjoo](/figures/lakshmanjoo/)'s verdict says that without him Kashmir Shaivism would very likely have survived only in libraries. [Swami Dayananda of Arsha Vidya](/figures/dayananda-arsha/) recovered Shankara's view that the Upanishads are themselves a means of knowledge, against the experience-centred reading that dominated modern Vedanta. The [Kanchi Paramacharya](/figures/chandrasekharendra-saraswati/) gave an old position a clear modern voice and, by his verdict, would have taken a low score for originality as praise.

This work mattered for a reason the tree shows. The new systems of Part 4 have few descendants on it, while the restatements have many. Sivananda's monks, the Mysore yoga line and the Gaudiya mission are among the largest families once the roots are set aside. A teaching travels in the form someone makes portable, and the restaters were the people who made old teachings portable.

## 4. The Vivekananda bottleneck

A good deal of what the world now calls Indian spirituality passed through one reframing in the 1890s. [Vivekananda](/figures/vivekananda/)'s verdict names what was new in it: the four yogas as a single teachable system, religion presented as experience open to verification in the language of science, non-dualism as the ground of social service, and Hinduism presented abroad as one universal religion with Advaita at its centre. It became, in the verdict's words, the default modern form of Hinduism, in India and abroad.

The tree shows only part of this. Vivekananda has six direct lines, to Aurobindo, [Radhakrishnan](/figures/radhakrishnan/), Rama Tirtha, Papa Ramdas, Sister Nivedita and [Swami Sarvapriyananda](/figures/sarvapriyananda/), and 17 names descend from him. Much of his influence appears in the inherited lists of later chapters. Radhakrishnan's verdict says the central ideas were Vivekananda's. Rama Tirtha called his teaching practical Vedanta, Vivekananda's phrase. Yogananda inherited the precedent of an Indian monk teaching yoga to Americans as universal religion. Chinmayananda inherited the call to make Vedanta practical, and Sarah Strauss connects Sivananda's four main verbs to Vivekananda's four yogas.

The reframing made a great deal possible. It gave educated Indians a way to defend their tradition in the terms of the colonial university, gave Western audiences a form of Hinduism they could approach without converting, and joined meditation to famine relief and schooling in a single monastic order.

The chapters also record what it left out, and they record a dispute about how much. Walter Neevel and Narasingha Sil argue that Ramakrishna's disciples, above all Vivekananda, presented him after his death as a calm Advaitin and played down the Kali worship that dominated his life. Anantanand Rambachan argues that Vivekananda reversed Shankara by making experience the final authority and scripture its secondary record. Paul Hacker traced the link between non-duality and compassion to Schopenhauer, and Dermot Killingley answered that Vivekananda held it before he met Deussen. Jyotirmaya Sharma argues that the single Hinduism he described helped prepare the ground for Hindutva, and Swami Medhananda and others point to his attacks on hereditary caste and his acceptance of every religion as a path to salvation. The chapter concludes that his writings contain enough of both emphases for each side to quote him.

The tree adds one more measure of what the reframing did not reach. Fifty-one of the 86 entries have no path from the Advaita root at all. The Radhasoami teachers, the Jain reformers of Part 10, the saints of the Hindu and Muslim borderland, the Gaudiya missionaries and the Buddhist revival grew from other roots. Prabhupada's verdict draws the contrast directly: Vivekananda translated Vedanta into universal religion, and Prabhupada refused the translation. The Paramacharya accepted Vivekananda's premise that all paths lead to one goal and drew the opposite conclusion, that each person should keep the path of their birth.

## 5. Lineage and legitimacy

The tree marks how each edge is known, and the claimed edges cluster in recognisable places.

Twenty edges are typed as claims. Only one rests on a document, and what it documents is the claim itself: C. W. Leadbeater's letters of 1909 record his belief that he had read Krishnamurti's past lives. The rest rest on testimony (8), hagiography (7) or devotional belief (4). Across all 410 edges, 17 rest on hagiography or devotion, and they fall into three groups.

The first group is immortal and Himalayan masters. Mahavatar Babaji has five edges on the tree, to [Lahiri Mahasaya](/figures/lahiri-mahasaya/), [Sri Yukteswar](/figures/yukteswar/), Yogananda, [Sri M](/figures/sri-m/) and Sri M's teacher Maheshwarnath, and every one is hagiographic. The tree's note on the first, Lahiri's initiation in 1861, records no evidence outside the lineage. Krishnamacharya's seven and a half years with a teacher in the Himalaya and Swami Rama's upbringing by Bengali Baba rest on each man's own account. The Krishnamacharya chapter says historians treat his story as one that gave a new practice an old pedigree.

The second group is reincarnation and past lives. Sathya Sai Baba declared in 1940, at thirteen, that he was Sai Baba of Shirdi, and in 1963 foretold a third incarnation as Prema Sai. [Sadhguru](/figures/sadhguru/) has described a past-life guru who passed on the knowledge to consecrate the Dhyanalinga. The Namdhari belief that Guru Gobind Singh lived on in seclusion until 1812 and passed the guruship to Balak Singh is rejected by mainstream Sikh bodies.

The third group is visionary or divine initiation. The Brahma Kumaris hold that the Supreme Soul spoke through Dada Lekhraj's body. Ramana named the hill of Arunachala as his guru. The edge giving Shirdi Sai Baba a guru named Venkusa comes from accounts written after his death, with no contemporary evidence.

The pattern in these groups is fairly consistent. Claimed edges gather where a new practice needed an old source, as with Kriya Yoga and Krishnamacharya's vinyasa, and where a person's authority rested on who they were, as with Sathya Sai Baba and Dada Lekhraj. They are rare among the Bengal reformers, the philosophers and the Jain teachers, whose authority rested on texts and arguments.

Some figures refused a lineage outright. Krishnamurti dissolved the organisation that had been built around him. [Anandamayi Ma](/figures/anandamayi-ma/), by her own account, initiated herself in 1922. Radhakrishnan had no guru and took no initiation. On the tree, 41 of the 86 entries have no edge of initiation, lineage or living-with from a human teacher, though some of these reflect how edges are typed. Sarada Devi's tie to Ramakrishna, for example, is marked as marriage. Others needed a line of succession and fought over it. The Radhasoami, Namdhari, Sahaj Marg and Dada Bhagwan successions were all contested, and the claims on the tree that rest on testimony include several made by rival heirs.

The initiations into people with entries are mostly sound. Of 39 such edges, 25 are documented, 11 rest on testimony and 3 on hagiography. The doubtful edges are few and concentrated, and most of them sit at the top of a lineage, where the founding claim is made.

## 6. The question that remains

The book asks thirteen questions, one for each part, and I set out expecting the tree to answer several of them. It answers fewer than I hoped, and it answers some I had not asked.

It answers the historical questions best. Whether the Vedas could be reread by reason is settled by Part 1: Rammohan printed them and argued from them, and every later reformer argued with his method. Whether the Buddha could come home has a historical answer in Ambedkar's ceremony at Nagpur on 14 October 1956, though the chapter records how far his Buddhism is from the one Dharmapala wanted India to honour. Whether one master can be Hindu and Muslim at once has a partial answer in Shirdi, where a fakir lived in a mosque he named after Krishna's city, and where the evidence comes overwhelmingly from Hindu sources.

The tree also answers part of the question of Part 13, who gets to speak for the sacred. The edge data show that people who claimed a direct mandate, from an immortal master, a past life or God speaking through them, did so most often when they brought something new and needed it to sound old. That is a finding about how authority is claimed. It says nothing about whether any claim was true.

Several questions it cannot answer. Whether all religion is one, whether the Self can be known without method, whether spirit is evolving, whether surrender is the path, whether the inner Word leads home, and whether awakening is a transmitted force are questions about reality, and the evidence a lineage tree holds is evidence about people. The chapters report what each teacher said and how they lived. They cannot test the Supermind or the sound current, and I have tried throughout to say so.

Three questions sit between these. Whether liberation can be a technology, whether right conduct is itself the path and whether the guru is the obstacle are partly empirical. Kuvalayananda measured what instruments could measure, which was mostly the body. Gandhi tried the monk's vows on a nation, and the result can be studied as history. Krishnamurti's refusal of authority can be judged by what happened to his listeners, and his last recorded statement was that nobody had lived the teaching. In each case the tree shows what people did with the question, and the question itself stays open.

What I take from the whole is narrower than a verdict on Indian spirituality. Across 190 years the doctrines changed slowly and the means of passing them on changed fast. Print, English, the lecture tour, the postal course, the weekend intensive and the recorded answer did more to change what ordinary people received than any new metaphysics. The few genuinely new systems, Aurobindo's, Iqbal's and Dayananda's among them, are still read mostly by specialists and devotees. The restatements filled the yoga studios and the satsang halls. I came to this book looking for the innovators, and I finish it with more respect for the people who carried old things carefully.
