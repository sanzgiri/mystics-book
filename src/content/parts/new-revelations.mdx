Most of the people in this book got their authority from somewhere. They had a guru who initiated them, a scripture they explained, a tradition that recognised them, or an experience that older teachers accepted as real. The people in this part claimed something more direct. They said that God spoke through them, or that they were a returning saint or a new incarnation, or that their own insight needed no lineage at all. Who gets to speak for the sacred?

Sathya Sai Baba announced at thirteen that he was Sai Baba of Shirdi reborn and built one of the largest devotional movements of modern India from a village in Andhra. Dada Lekhraj, a retired Sindhi jeweller, said in the 1930s that Shiva spoke through his mouth, and handed his fortune and his movement to a committee of young women. Osho, a philosophy lecturer from Madhya Pradesh, mixed Tantra, Zen and Western therapy into loud meditations and gathered followers in Poona and Oregon. Sri Chinmoy left the Sri Aurobindo Ashram for New York in 1964 and taught that a marathon could be a prayer. Kalki Bhagavan and his wife Amma Bhagavan offered the Oneness blessing, a transfer of grace by touch. Ramdev taught breathing exercises to a television audience of millions. Nithyananda left India around 2019 and declared a nation of his own called Kailasa, and Dhirendra Shastri of Bageshwar Dham in Madhya Pradesh holds public courts where, devotees say, he names people's troubles before they speak.

They disagree about almost everything that matters. Some claim to be divine and some only to be messengers. Some ask for total surrender to the person of the guru, and others hand followers a technique and send them home. The Brahma Kumaris see a world about to end and begin again, while Osho's ideal of Zorba the Buddha joined meditation to the pleasures of ordinary life. And they disagree, by example, about what a following is for, whether prayer, service, business or politics.

I have tried to treat this part the way I treat the rest. I describe each teaching first in its own terms, report miracles and claims as claims, and set out controversies with the dates, the courts and the organisations' answers. Several of these figures are surrounded by accusations, and I have stated only what courts have decided or what has been well reported, and no more.

## A note on godmen

Indian newspapers use the word godman, often with "self-styled" in front of it, for a guru with a mass following, a large ashram and influential friends. A few such figures are known mainly for criminal cases, and the record they left is a court record. I have kept them out of the chapters and give their legal position here, as it stood in October 2026. Controversies around figures who do have entries, such as Ramdev, Sadhguru, Sri Sri Ravi Shankar and Muktananda, are dealt with on their own pages.

**Asaram.** In April 2018 a special court in Jodhpur sentenced Asaram, born Asumal Harpalani, to life imprisonment for raping a minor girl at his ashram in 2013; in May 2026 the Rajasthan High Court upheld the rape conviction and life term while acquitting him of gang rape, and his appeal is now before the Supreme Court, which declined in mid-2026 to suspend his sentence. In January 2023 a sessions court in Gandhinagar gave him a second life sentence for raping a woman disciple at his Motera ashram near Ahmedabad between 2001 and 2006, and his appeal against that conviction is pending in the Gujarat High Court.

**Gurmeet Ram Rahim Singh.** In 2017 a special CBI court in Panchkula sentenced the head of the Dera Sacha Sauda of Sirsa to twenty years' imprisonment for raping two of his followers, a sentence he is serving in Sunaria jail, Rohtak, with frequent periods of parole. Life sentences imposed on him for the murders of the dera's former manager Ranjit Singh and the Sirsa journalist Ram Chander Chhatrapati were set aside by the Punjab and Haryana High Court in May 2024 and March 2026, and appeals against both acquittals are before the Supreme Court.

**Rampal.** In October 2018 a court in Hisar sentenced Rampal, head of the Satlok Ashram, to life imprisonment in two murder cases arising from the deaths of five women and an infant at his ashram in Barwala during the police operation to arrest him in November 2014. The Punjab and Haryana High Court suspended his life sentence in the larger case in 2025 while his appeal is heard, and in April 2026 granted him bail in a separate sedition case from the same events, which is still on trial.

**Swami Premananda.** In August 1997 a sessions court in Pudukkottai, Tamil Nadu, convicted the Sri Lankan-born head of an ashram near Tiruchirappalli of raping girls living there and of the murder of a man named Ravi, and gave him a double life sentence, which the Madras High Court and, in April 2005, the Supreme Court upheld. He maintained his innocence and died in Cuddalore Central Prison on 21 February 2011.

**Chandraswami.** A Delhi court acquitted Chandraswami in December 2003 in the Lakhubhai Pathak cheating case, in which former prime minister P. V. Narasimha Rao was also acquitted, and again in October 2004 in a forgery case concerning documents about accounts in St Kitts; the Jain Commission's report on the assassination of Rajiv Gandhi named him, but no charge followed. The Enforcement Directorate imposed penalties of about ₹9 crore on him for foreign-exchange violations, which he was still contesting in 2011, and he died in New Delhi on 23 May 2017 without a criminal conviction.
