Most of the people in this book got their authority from somewhere. They had a guru who initiated them, a scripture they explained, a tradition that recognised them, or an experience that older teachers accepted as real. The people in this part claimed something more direct. They said that God spoke through them, or that they were a returning saint or a new incarnation, or that their own insight needed no lineage at all. Who gets to speak for the sacred?

Sathya Sai Baba announced at thirteen that he was Sai Baba of Shirdi reborn and built one of the largest devotional movements of modern India from a village in Andhra. Dada Lekhraj, a retired Sindhi jeweller, said in the 1930s that Shiva spoke through his mouth, and handed his fortune and his movement to a committee of young women. Osho, a philosophy lecturer from Madhya Pradesh, mixed Tantra, Zen and Western therapy into loud meditations and gathered followers in Poona and Oregon. Sri Chinmoy left the Sri Aurobindo Ashram for New York in 1964 and taught that a marathon could be a prayer. Kalki Bhagavan and his wife Amma Bhagavan offered the Oneness blessing, a transfer of grace by touch. Ramdev taught breathing exercises to a television audience of millions. Nithyananda left India around 2019 and declared a nation of his own called Kailasa, and Dhirendra Shastri of Bageshwar Dham in Madhya Pradesh holds a twice-weekly divya darbar, or divine court, where, devotees say, he names people's troubles before they speak.

They disagree about almost everything that matters. Some claim to be divine and some only to be messengers. Some ask for total surrender to the person of the guru, and others hand followers a technique and send them home. The Brahma Kumaris see a world about to end and begin again, while Osho's ideal of Zorba the Buddha joined meditation to the pleasures of ordinary life. And they disagree, by example, about what a following is for, whether prayer, service, business or politics.

I have tried to treat this part the way I treat the rest. I describe each teaching first in its own terms and report miracles and claims as claims, in the words of the people who make them.

## A note on godmen

Indian newspapers use the word godman for a guru with a mass following and a large ashram. The word covers several different claims, and the teachings in this part keep them apart, so I find it more useful to ask what each person said they were and how their followers took it.

The oldest of these claims is the avatar, God descended in human form. Hindu tradition holds that Kalki, the tenth and final avatar of Vishnu, will come at the end of the present dark age. Vijay Kumar took that name, and early followers worshipped him as the avatar. Later he said that Kalki was a collective awakening, and the sanctum of the Oneness Temple held a golden orb for meditation on each person's own idea of the divine. Sathya Sai Baba set out his own theology in a discourse of 1963. Shiva and Shakti, he said, had promised the sage Bharadwaja three births in his lineage, as Sai Baba of Shirdi, as himself and as a Prema Sai still to come, and devotees call him an avatar. Nithyananda's organisation also calls him an avatar, of what it names superconsciousness, and his teaching aims at the realisation "I am Shiva."

The Brahma Kumaris make a different claim. For them God is the Supreme Soul, Shiva, a point of light with no body of his own, and from the 1930s, members believe, he spoke through Lekhraj's body. Lekhraj, renamed Brahma, was in this view the medium through which the revelation came, and his talks are still read aloud each morning in the movement's centres as *murlis*.

Osho rested his authority on his own experience. He said he became enlightened under a tree in Jabalpur on 21 March 1953, and he had no initiation from any teacher and never claimed one. The title he took in 1971, Bhagwan, is the word devotees use for God and for some saints. Dhirendra Shastri stands closer to the older pattern. He describes himself as a disciple of Jagadguru Rambhadracharya, and he says that his knowledge of what troubles his visitors comes from Hanuman.

Each chapter sets out the claim in the terms the teaching gives it, alongside the practice it came with and the line of teachers, if any, behind it.
