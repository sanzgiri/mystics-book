The Buddha was born, taught and died in the Gangetic plain, and for more than a thousand years his teaching was one of the great religions of India. By about the thirteenth century it had all but vanished from the country, while it went on living in Sri Lanka, Tibet, Burma and the rest of Asia. The question of this part is whether it can come home, and what it would have to become in order to do so.

The revival began from outside. In 1891 a young Sinhalese Buddhist, Anagarika Dharmapala, visited Bodh Gaya, the place of the Buddha's awakening, found the temple in the hands of a Hindu abbot, and founded the Maha Bodhi Society to win it back. He appears on the tree here as the man who reopened the road between Buddhist Asia and India. The chapter of this part belongs to B. R. Ambedkar, born into a caste of untouchables, who spent twenty-one years deciding which religion to leave Hinduism for. In 1956 at Nagpur he took the Buddhist refuges and gave them to several hundred thousand people in a single morning. His last book retold the Buddha's life and teaching for those converts.

The two men wanted different things from the same Buddha. Dharmapala wanted India to honour the Buddhism that Asia had kept. Ambedkar respected that tradition and took his refuges from a monk trained in it, but he also questioned some of its central doctrines, the Four Noble Truths, karma and rebirth among them, and he read the Buddha's teaching as a guide for remaking society. Traditional Buddhists still argue about whether his reading is a school of Buddhism or something new.

I have tried to set out Ambedkar's Buddhism in his own terms first and his critics' afterwards. The chapter follows his religious thought and leaves his career as a lawmaker in the background.
