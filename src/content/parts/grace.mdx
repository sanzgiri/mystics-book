Most of the teachers so far have offered a method. You inquire, or breathe, or meditate, and the method does the work if you do it well enough. The people in this part put the weight somewhere else. They taught that the decisive thing is love for a personal God, and that what finally changes a person is grace, which no technique can earn. The question of this part is whether surrender is the path.

Anandamayi Ma, a village girl from East Bengal with almost no schooling, initiated herself in 1922 and spent sixty years telling visitors to remember God in everything and give their work to Him. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami sailed to America in 1965 at sixty-nine to teach the chanting of Krishna's name, carrying a devotional tradition that Bhaktivinoda Thakura and his son Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati had already rebuilt for modern Bengal. Mata Amritanandamayi, a fisherman's daughter from Kerala, made the embrace into a form of darshan and teaches love as service. Shastriji Maharaj founded BAPS in 1907 on the belief that God's ideal devotee is always present on earth as the guru. Papa Ramdas, a failed businessman, left home in 1922 with a mantra his father had given him. Neem Karoli Baba wrote nothing, fed whoever came and became famous in the West through Ram Dass. Premanand Maharaj, who left home at thirteen and came to Vrindavan as a mendicant, now answers ordinary people's questions about marriage, family and work with the same advice, to keep repeating the names of Radha and Krishna.

All of them treat God as someone to be loved, and they disagree about much else. For the Gaudiya teachers and for BAPS, grace comes through a line of gurus and a defined practice, and the chain matters as much as the chanting. Anandamayi Ma, Amma and Ramdas had no human guru, and they often spoke of the God they loved in the language of Advaita, as the one reality inside every form. They also differ on how much the devotee has to do. Prabhupada asked his disciples for sixteen rounds of the mantra a day and four strict rules of conduct. Neem Karoli Baba left no recorded discourses and no systematic teaching, and his devotees remember him through short instructions, the people he fed and the stories they tell.

I find this part the hardest to judge by the book's own measure, originality of teaching, because these teachers mostly claimed to add nothing. Their newness lies in who they reached and how. I have tried to describe each devotion in its own terms, and to report the miracles their followers tell as their followers' accounts.
