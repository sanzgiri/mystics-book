Most of us treat ethics as the ground floor of religion. You keep your promises and do no harm, and then, if you have the time and the inclination, you go on to prayer, meditation or the search for God. The people in this part asked whether the ground floor might be the whole building. Can a vow, kept honestly for a lifetime, be the path to liberation itself?

Gandhi is at the centre. He took the vows of the monk, truth, nonviolence, celibacy and non-possession, and tried to live them as a lawyer, a householder and a political leader, calling each campaign an experiment with truth. As a young man in Bombay he met Rajchandra, a Jain jeweller and poet who wrote a 142-verse manual on the soul before he was thirty, and Gandhi later named him first among the modern men who had marked his life. Vinoba Bhave, Gandhi's closest disciple, spent his life explaining the *Bhagavad Gita* and walked across India asking landowners to give land to the landless, which he treated as a spiritual practice. Acharya Tulsi and his successor Mahapragya, monks of the Jain Terapanth order, offered lay people of any religion the monk's great vows in a smaller form, the Anuvrat pledge of 1949, and later a meditation course. Kanji Swami, a Sthanakvasi Jain monk, left his order in 1934 after reading Kundakunda's *Samayasara* and taught lay followers at Songadh. Dada Bhagwan, a Gujarati contractor, said that knowledge of the Self could be given in a single ceremony.

They disagree about the order of things. For Gandhi, Vinoba and the Terapanth teachers, conduct comes first and does the work. You take the vow, keep it, fail, and keep it again, and the self is purified in the keeping. Kanji Swami and Dada Bhagwan reversed the order. Kanji Swami held that vows, giving and fasting were worthless without an understanding of the soul, and Dada Bhagwan taught that the knowledge comes first, as a gift, and the long path of renunciation can be skipped. Rajchandra is harder to place. He stayed a married man in the pearl and diamond trade and took no monastic vows, yet his *Atmasiddhi* puts knowledge of the soul, and a living teacher who can show it, at the centre.

Most of these figures come from Jain or Jain-influenced settings, and Gandhi grew up among Jain neighbours in Kathiawar, so the part is partly a conversation inside Jainism with Gandhi listening in. I suggest reading Gandhi's chapter first, because the others are easiest to understand in relation to him. The profiles can then be read in any order.
