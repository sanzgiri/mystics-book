Almost every chapter of this book rests on the assumption that someone who has seen the truth can lead someone who has not, and that the way to it runs through a teacher, a lineage and a practice. The people in this part asked whether that arrangement is itself what stands in the way. They argued that a seeker who leans on a teacher never learns to look for himself, and that any authority in these matters, however sincere, puts someone else's conclusions where one's own seeing should be.

The part opens with an institution. The Theosophical Society, founded in New York in 1875 and settled at Adyar near Madras from 1882, taught that a brotherhood of hidden Masters guided humanity and that a World Teacher would soon appear. In 1909 its leaders picked out a thin boy on the Adyar beach, J. Krishnamurti, and raised him to be that Teacher's vehicle. On 3 August 1929, at Ommen in the Netherlands, he dissolved the order built around him and spent the next fifty-six years telling audiences to follow no one, himself included. U. G. Krishnamurti, no relation, grew up in a Theosophist household, listened to the older man for years and broke with him. In 1967 he said that enlightenment does not exist and that he had nothing to teach. The sidebar on Periyar comes at the question from outside religion altogether. He was a Tamil rationalist who left the Congress in 1925 and preached that god, scripture and priest together held the caste order in place.

They disagree about what is left once the authority goes. Krishnamurti kept a sense of the sacred and wrote in his private journal of a state he called the benediction; he said that change comes from a direct seeing of how one's own mind works, without a method or a master. U.G. said there was no such state to reach and that the search itself was the trouble. Periyar had no interest in an inner state. He treated religion as a social institution and asked people to judge it, gods included, by reason and by whether it treated them as equals. All three drew followers anyway. Krishnamurti spoke from a platform for half a century, admirers gathered U.G.'s conversations into books, and Periyar's slogans are inscribed under his statues.

I have read these men as philosophers first. The objection that Krishnamurti and U.G. became gurus of a new kind is recorded in their entries, and the objections to Periyar's language in his. Read Krishnamurti first, since U.G. is arguing with him.
