Most teachers in this book gave their students something to do. The people in this part treated the practice as the heart of the matter. Learn the method correctly, repeat it for long enough, and the result follows, much as it does in a gymnasium or a laboratory. The question of this part is whether liberation can be produced that way, as the output of a technique, or whether a technique can only prepare the ground for something it cannot cause.

The oldest line here starts in Benares. Lahiri Mahasaya, a government clerk, said that in 1861 a Himalayan master he called Babaji gave him a breathing method, Kriya Yoga, which he passed on to householders in graded initiations. Paramahansa Yogananda took it to America in 1920 and wrote that half a minute of Kriya equals a year of natural spiritual growth. Sri M, born in Kerala in 1948, teaches a Kriya that he says reached him from the same Babaji. A second line runs through the body. In Mysore, T. Krishnamacharya set postures to the breath, and two of his students, B.K.S. Iyengar and K. Pattabhi Jois, built two of the best-known styles of yoga taught today. At Lonavla in 1924 Swami Kuvalayananda began testing hatha yoga with X-rays and pressure gauges. At Rishikesh Swami Sivananda wrote a daily routine anyone could follow, and his disciple Swami Satyananda went on to found the Bihar School of Yoga. A third line turned meditation into a course. Maharishi Mahesh Yogi taught one method in the same words in every country and asked scientists to measure it. Sri Sri Ravi Shankar, who travelled with him, built the Art of Living around a single breathing sequence. In Tamil Nadu, Vethathiri Maharishi reduced kundalini yoga to a graded syllabus for working people. The part also includes Swami Rama.

They disagree about what the method is for. Yogananda was the most confident, and he did the arithmetic of progress per breath. Maharishi said meditation should take no effort at all. Kuvalayananda measured what instruments could measure, which was mostly the body. Sivananda prescribed technique mixed with devotion, service and study. Krishnamacharya, late in life, shaped practice for one person at a time. The other quarrel is about the teacher. Some of these lines hold that the technique works only when a guru gives it, and others teach it for a set fee to anyone who enrols.

A technique can be taught, checked and handed on, and I have tried to describe each method as its teachers describe it, and to keep their claims about results separate from what anyone has been able to measure.
