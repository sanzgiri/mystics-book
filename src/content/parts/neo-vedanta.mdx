Most people grow up assuming that religions are rivals. Each makes claims about God, salvation and the right way to live, and the claims cannot all be true. The people in this part start from an old Indian line, that the sages call the one truth by many names, and ask whether it can be taken literally in a world of missionaries, colonial universities and printing presses. Is all religion one?

The part begins at the Kali temple at Dakshineswar, where Ramakrishna practised the disciplines of several traditions, Islam and Christianity among them, and reported that they led to the same God. His wife Sarada Devi, who never gave a lecture, became the guru of the monks he left behind and treated a Muslim labourer as her son. Vivekananda carried the claim to Chicago in 1893 and turned Advaita into a creed of service. In Lahore a mathematics teacher heard him, became Swami Rama Tirtha, and took the same message to Japan and California. In Travancore, Narayana Guru and his companion Chattampi Swamikal drew from Advaita an argument against caste. S. Radhakrishnan made the case in the language of academic philosophy. Swami Chinmayananda and his student Swami Dayananda of Arsha Vidya taught the Gita and the Upanishads in English to the new middle class, in India and abroad. Swami Sarvapriyananda teaches in New York today, and Acharya Prashant teaches online to a young Hindi-speaking audience. Against all of them stands the Kanchi Paramacharya, who accepted that the paths lead to one goal and concluded that each person should keep to the path of their birth.

They disagree on three things. The first is what the unity of religions means in practice. For Ramakrishna it licensed trying other paths; for the Paramacharya it made conversion and mixing pointless; Arsha Vidya's teachers argued hard against conversion, while Narayana Guru proposed a single religion for humanity. The second is whether the unity is found in experience or in knowledge. Vivekananda and Radhakrishnan rest the claim on mystical experience that anyone can test, while the Arsha Vidya teachers hold that the Upanishads are a means of knowledge in their own right and that liberation comes from understanding them properly taught. The third is caste, which the Kerala reformers attacked from the same metaphysics that the Paramacharya used to defend it.

Critics have said that Neo-Vedanta made Hinduism look like Advaita, and Advaita look like every other religion. I have tried to let each figure answer the question in his or her own terms. The chapters on Ramakrishna and Vivekananda are the place to start; the profiles can be read in any order.
