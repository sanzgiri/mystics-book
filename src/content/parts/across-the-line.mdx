Today Hindu and Muslim are usually treated as two communities with a clear line between them, and the first thing people ask of a holy man is which side he stood on. In the villages of the Deccan a century ago the line was fainter. Hindus and Muslims honoured the same saints, living and buried, as they had for centuries. This part asks whether one master can belong to both religions at once, and what the question even means.

Its first chapter is about Sai Baba of Shirdi, a fakir of unknown birth who lived about sixty years in a half-ruined mosque, which he named after Krishna's city of Dwarka. He kept a fire burning in it, said "Allah Malik," God is the master, let Hindu devotees perform worship with lamps inside it, and allowed the Muslim procession of the Urus festival in the same season as Rama's birthday. Upasni Maharaj, a Brahmin Ayurvedic doctor sent to him in 1911, was at first put off by a saint who seemed to him a Muslim, then spent more than a year in the village's Khandoba temple on his instructions. At Sakori he later trained women as Vedic priests. Meher Baba was born a Zoroastrian in Pune. By his own account his inner life was opened in 1913 or 1914 by Hazrat Babajan, an elderly Muslim woman who sat under a neem tree in the city, and completed over seven years by Upasni. He also visited Sai Baba and Tajuddin Baba, a Sufi of Nagpur. He named these saints, with Narayan Maharaj of Kedgaon, as the five Perfect Masters of his age, and in 1954 he declared himself the Avatar.

They give three different answers. Devotees and scholars still argue whether Sai Baba he was a Sufi whose Islam later Hindu writers played down, a Hindu saint in a fakir's robe, or a man who refused to choose and moved between both idioms on purpose. Upasni took what he learned from a fakir who lived in a mosque and built something more strictly Brahmin and Vedic. Meher Baba put Hindu and Muslim saints into one scheme that belonged to no single religion, and named Zoroaster, Rama, Krishna, Buddha, Jesus and Muhammad as the Avatar's earlier appearances before himself.

I have tried to keep in mind how lopsided the evidence is. Most of what we know about Sai Baba comes from a Marathi verse life written by a Brahmin for Hindu readers, and the main Muslim source is one attendant's small notebook. Read the Sai Baba chapter first, since the others take their bearings from him.
