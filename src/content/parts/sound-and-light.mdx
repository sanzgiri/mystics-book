Most religion reaches people from outside, through a scripture read aloud, a temple, a priest or a rite. The teachers in this part said that the decisive thing is already inside the body, a Name, a sound or a light, and that a person who turns the attention toward it can follow it home. The question of the part is whether that inner Word is the way.

The fullest answer came from Agra. Shiv Dayal Singh, a banker's son who had spent years in private meditation, opened his meetings to the public in 1861, and the Radhasoami faith grew from them. He taught a method of withdrawing the attention to a point behind the eyes and following an inner sound upward through regions of light. Through the Beas line of Jaimal Singh and Sawan Singh the method passed to Kirpal Singh, an accounts officer who carried it to Delhi and to the West. A few years before Agra, in 1857, Ram Singh began the Namdhari movement in a Punjab village, with a white dress, a woollen rosary and a rule of daily remembrance of the Name. Inayat Khan, a court musician from Baroda, took a Chishti Sufi teaching rich in music and sound to Europe and America. In Tamil country Vallalar sang of God as the light of grace, and in southern Travancore Ayya Vaikundar's followers built a worship without idols for people the temples kept out. Two men named Ram Chandra, Lalaji and Babuji, made a meditation on the heart in which a teacher passes spiritual energy to the student.

They differ on what the Word is. For Shiv Dayal Singh and his successors it is a current of sound and light to be heard and seen within. For Ram Singh it is the Name received from the Guru and repeated. Inayat Khan treated sound and music as the deep structure of everything, and Vallalar placed light and compassion at the centre. They also differ on who may guide the journey. The Radhasoami teachers and the Namdharis insist on a living master, the Sikh establishment holds that the Guru now lives in the scripture and the community, and the succession after Shiv Dayal Singh, Kirpal Singh, Inayat Khan, Babuji and the Namdhari Gurus was contested.

I have tried to set out each teaching in its own vocabulary before comparing them, because the words Naam, Shabd and Light do not mean the same thing in each place they appear. Several of these figures are known mainly to their own followers, and much of what is told about them comes from devotional sources, which I have marked as such.
