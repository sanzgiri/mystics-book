Most spiritual traditions are ladders. You purify your conduct, train your body, study the texts, receive initiation, practice for years, and somewhere near the top, if you are lucky, you see. The three men at the center of this part kicked the ladder away.

Ramana Maharshi realized the Self as a sixteen-year-old schoolboy, with no teacher and no practice, and spent the next fifty years telling visitors that what they were looking for was what was looking. Nisargadatta Maharaj, a Bombay shopkeeper who sold cheap cigarettes from a stall, was given one instruction by his guru, to hold on to the bare sense "I am," and found that it was enough. Papaji, an army officer who had spent twenty-five years chanting the name of Krishna, met Ramana and stopped looking.

What they share is a claim that sounds either profound or absurd depending on the day: there is nothing to attain, because what you are is already here, and every method risks reinforcing the very seeker it is supposed to dissolve. What they disagree about, more than their admirers like to admit, is whether that means no effort is needed at all.

I find this part the hardest to write about, and the most interesting. The teaching is almost too simple to explain without either flattening it or turning it into a technique, which is exactly what these teachers warned against. So I have tried to let their lives carry the argument. Read the chapters in order, and notice how each one answers the question on this page a little differently.
