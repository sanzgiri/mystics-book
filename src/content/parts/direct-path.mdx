Most spiritual traditions work like a ladder. You purify your conduct, train your body, study the texts, receive initiation and practice for years, and if you are lucky you reach the top and see clearly. The three men at the center of this part rejected that sequence.

Ramana Maharshi realized the Self as a sixteen-year-old schoolboy, with no teacher and no practice, and spent the next fifty years telling visitors that what they were searching for was the one doing the searching. Nisargadatta Maharaj ran a small shop in Bombay selling hand-rolled cigarettes. His guru gave him a single instruction, to hold on to the bare sense "I am," and he found it was enough. Papaji was an army officer who had chanted the name of Krishna for twenty-five years. He met Ramana, and his search ended there.

All three taught that there is nothing to attain, because what you are is already present, and that any method can end up strengthening the very seeker it is meant to dissolve. They disagreed, more than their admirers like to admit, about whether that means a person needs to make any effort at all.

I have found this part the hardest to write and the most interesting. The teaching is so simple that explaining it tends either to flatten it or to turn it into a technique, which these teachers warned against. So I have relied on their lives more than on summaries of doctrine. The chapters are best read in order, since each answers this part's question a little differently.
