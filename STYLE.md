# House style

The book should read like a well-researched long-form magazine piece written by a person who cares about the subject. Plain, warm, specific.

## Do

- Write plain declarative sentences. Say what happened and what the person taught.
- Lead with concrete detail: dates, places, objects, what someone said or did.
- Explain a term once, in everyday words, the first time it appears.
- Vary sentence length the way speech does.
- Use the first person only in part introductions, the "Inherited and new" verdict, and the Epilogue.
- Report legend as legend: "devotees say," "by his own account," "tradition holds."

## Avoid

- Contrast-by-negation for effect: "not X, but Y," "X is not A; it is B," "A banyan never has just one trunk." State the positive claim directly. Plain factual negatives are fine ("He had no human guru").
- Aphoristic one-liners and paired fragments: "Neither was philosophy. Both were longing."
- Colon-then-reveal sentences and dramatic setups.
- Em-dash asides.
- Triplets used for rhythm rather than content.
- Stock phrases: "worth noting," "it's important to remember," "in many ways," "a testament to."
- Scare quotes around ordinary words.
- Ending paragraphs on a punchline.

## Quick test

Read a paragraph aloud. If it sounds like a slogan, a TED talk or a book blurb, rewrite it the way you would explain it to a friend.
